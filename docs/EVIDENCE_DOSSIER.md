## C1 — Selected-cohort reporting can't separate selection from program effect

- **[VERIFIED]** The "Elite Illusion": at Boston and NYC exam schools, a fuzzy regression-discontinuity design around admission cutoffs found *little to no causal effect* of exam-school attendance on state test scores, PSAT/SAT/AP scores, or college quality — despite exam-school students having far higher raw achievement. The high performance of these students "largely reflects selective admission rather than value added by the school." Estimates were precise enough to rule out large effects. (Source: Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist & Pathak, *Econometrica* 82(1):137–196, 2014; NBER WP 17264, 2011 — https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/The%20elite%20illusion.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Regression to the mean formalizes the C1 warning: when a cohort is selected on an extreme score, part of the score is genuine ability and part is noise that does not repeat, so the group's later average moves toward the mean *even with no intervention* — meaning post-admission gains of a top-selected cohort are partly a statistical artifact, not program effect. Guarding against it "chiefly" requires a randomized control group subject to the same regression. (Source: J-PAL / Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab, 2024 — https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/2-27-24/regression-mean-what-it-and-why-it-matters-impact-evaluations)
- **[INFERENCE]** These two findings compound directly for our current process: observing that admitted (top-cutoff) students later score highly is exactly the pattern the "elite illusion" RD design showed is *not* evidence of program value, and regression to the mean predicts their measured "growth" will partly reverse regardless of the program. A selected-cohort outcome report cannot distinguish these from a true effect.

## C2 — A single test cutoff misses capable students and tracks family advantage

- **[VERIFIED/PRESS]** SAT scores rise monotonically with family income. Test-takers in the top income quintile (≥ $117,610) averaged 1152 total; the gap between lowest and highest income quintiles was ~265 points. Older bracket data showed a ~300-point gap between the <$20k and $200k+ brackets. (Source: College Board data as analyzed by BestColleges, 2024 — https://www.bestcolleges.com/research/average-sat-score-full-statistics/ ; note: College Board's 2022+ reports switched to census-tract income estimates, so exact self-reported-bracket tables are pre-2020/third-party.)
- **[VERIFIED]** A single hard cutoff misclassifies students near the boundary because every score is a confidence band, not a point: individual IQ scores carry ~±3–5 points of measurement error, and testing authorities explicitly warn scores near ±2 SD are *not* to be "automatically" classified gifted. Best practice is 95% CIs (±2 SEM), not a threshold. (Source: guidance summarized re: Wechsler standard error of measurement — https://www.gavinpublishers.com/article/view/applying-and-interpreting-the-standard-error-of-measurement-and-standard-error-of-the-estimate-in-intelligence-testing)
- **[VERIFIED]** Error is *asymmetric* at the gifted cutoff due to regression to the mean: for a WAIS-IV FSIQ of 130, the standard-error-of-estimate band is ~125–133 (5 points below, 3 above), i.e., the true-score band leans *below* the cutoff — so a student scoring exactly 130 is more likely a false positive, and a truly-gifted student is easily pushed just under. Reliability is also *lowest in young children*, precisely when gifted screening happens. (Source: same SEM/SEE analysis, Gavin Publishers)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Newer College Board tract-based income data (Class of 2023, primary source):* mean SAT total rose monotonically across census-tract income quintiles — Lowest ($0–$53,263) **891**, 2nd Lowest **942**, Middle **984**, 2nd Highest **1039**, Highest (≥$113,341) **1148** — a **257-point** bottom-to-top gap on the current methodology. Benchmark attainment ("Met Both") tracked the same gradient: 15% (lowest quintile) → 63% (highest). Quintile Ns range 203,941–504,985. (Source: College Board, *2023 Total Group SAT Suite of Assessments Annual Report*, p.4 — https://reports.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/2023-total-group-sat-suite-of-assessments-annual-report%20ADA.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Even among students with high standardized test scores, Black students are significantly less likely to be assigned to gifted services in both math and reading, controlling for health, SES, and school/classroom characteristics — i.e., a test-plus-referral process tracks factors beyond measured ability. (Source: Grissom & Redding, *AERA Open* 2(1):1–25, 2016, ~10,000 kindergartners, ECLS — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858415622175)
- **[VERIFIED]** Teacher-referral discretion tracks family/social advantage by race: high-achieving Black students were only about **one-third as likely** as similar White students to be assigned to gifted programs when taught by a non-Black teacher, but roughly as likely when taught by a Black teacher. (Source: Grissom & Redding, *AERA Open* 2016 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858415622175)

## C3 — Broader, multi-signal capability measures work without lowering the bar

- **[VERIFIED]** Spatial ability predicts STEM achievement *above and beyond* general intelligence and verbal/math ability. Across Project Talent (N ≈ 400,000 U.S. high-schoolers, 11-yr follow-up) plus GRE and SMPY data, spatial scores added incremental validity for STEM degrees, patents, and technical innovation — a "sleeping giant" that verbal/math talent searches systematically miss. (Source: Wai, Lubinski & Benbow, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 2009 — https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2009-wai.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** The predictive power of spatial ability holds over 25+ years: spatial ability measured at age 13 predicted STEM accomplishment at age ~38 even after partialling out age-13 verbal and math talent. (Source: Wai, Lubinski, Benbow & Steiger, 2010; Vanderbilt longitudinal study reported in *Psychological Science*, 2013 — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/07/130715070347.htm)
- **[VERIFIED]** Spatial skills are trainable, so broadening the signal doesn't just relabel fixed talent: meta-analysis of 217 studies found a training effect of Hedges's g = 0.47, durable and transferable across delays. (Source: Uttal et al., *Psychological Bulletin*, 2013 — https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/uttal/vittae/documents/ContentServer.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** A nonverbal ability test can identify near-equal proportions across groups without lowering the standard: on the NNAT, similar percentages of White (5.6%), Black (5.1%), and Hispanic (4.4%) children reached the 95th-percentile score of 125 (N = 20,270, K–12, demographically representative). (Source: Naglieri & Ford, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 47(2), 2003 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001698620304700206)
- **[VERIFIED]** Caveat on the *bar* argument — nonverbal/nontraditional methods help but don't fully close the gap alone: meta-analysis of 54 studies (85 effect sizes, ~191M students) found nontraditional identification narrows the underrepresentation gap (risk ratio 0.34 vs. 0.27 for traditional methods) but does not eliminate it, and CogAT composite showed greater predictive validity than NNAT2 on WISC-IV follow-up. Argues for *multi-signal* screening, not swapping one single test for another. (Source: meta-analysis summarized via Gifted Child Quarterly literature; Lohman critique — https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ996235)

## C4 — Universal/broadened screening increases identification of underrepresented gifted students

- **[VERIFIED]** **Headline effect size (Card & Giuliano, Broward County FL):** universal second-grade screening — *with no change in the gifted-eligibility standard* — raised the odds of gifted identification by **174% for disadvantaged (FRL/ELL) students**, **118% for Hispanic**, and **74% for Black** students, vs. only 11–12% for non-disadvantaged/White students. (Source: Card & Giuliano, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, 2016; NBER WP 21519 — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113 ; PMC5137751)
- **[VERIFIED]** In percentage-point terms, gifted-identification rates (pre-2004/05 → post-2006/07 cohorts): disadvantaged/Plan B 1.4% → 4.3% (+2.9 ppt); Hispanic 2.1% → 5.7% (+3.6 ppt); Black 1.1% → 2.7% (+1.6 ppt); all students 3.3% → 5.5%. School-level difference-in-difference effect = +1.6 ppt (SE 0.3). Analysis N = 78,065; Plan B subsample N = 37,947. (Source: Card & Giuliano, *PNAS* 2016 — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113)
- **[VERIFIED]** The newly-identified students were genuinely capable, not marginal: among disadvantaged "compliers" newly caught by screening, **a full 20% had IQs of 130 or higher** — students the referral-based system was missing entirely. Compliers were ~67% FRL and ~46% Hispanic. (Source: Card & Giuliano, *PNAS* 2016 — PMC5137751)
- **[VERIFIED]** Screening also de-concentrated giftedness across schools: before universal screening 18% of district schools enrolled 50% of all gifted students; afterward 57% of schools housed half the gifted population. (Source: Card & Giuliano, *PNAS* 2016)
- **[VERIFIED]** Structural access barrier quantified: ~90% of White students attend a school that offers a gifted program vs. ~83% of Black students — part of the underrepresentation gap is availability, which universal/broadened screening within a program directly addresses. (Source: Grissom & Redding, *AERA Open* 2016 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858415622175)
- **[VERIFIED]** *National-scale baseline the screening effect works against (OCR data):* in 2017–18, Black and Hispanic students were only **55%** and **77%** as represented in the identified gifted population as in the overall K–12 population, while White and Asian students were **108%** and **196%** as represented — the disproportionality that universal second-grade screening reversed in the Card & Giuliano natural experiment. (Source: U.S. Dept. of Education Office for Civil Rights data, cited by NAGC — https://www.nagc.org/identification)
- **[ESTIMATE]** *Non-Broward corroboration (practitioner, unnamed district):* a district that replaced a one-time referral test with universal screening plus a strengths-based "Stretch or Support" model reported roughly **tripling** the number of students identified for advanced learning services over two years — directional support that the Broward effect is not district-idiosyncratic, though this is journalistic, not a controlled design. (Source: K-12 Dive / NAPSA — https://www.k12dive.com/news/schools-turn-to-universal-screening-to-increase-equity-in-gifted-programs/430797/)

## C5 — Randomized admission among qualified applicants best isolates program effect

- **[VERIFIED]** Randomization is the primary defense against regression-to-the-mean and selection bias: "chiefly with a randomized control group... because the control group is subject to exactly the same regression as the treatment group, any difference between them at retest cannot be regression and can be attributed to the treatment." Matching/covariate adjustment on a single selection score does not achieve this. (Source: J-PAL, 2024 — https://www.povertyactionlab.org/blog/2-27-24/regression-mean-what-it-and-why-it-matters-impact-evaluations)
- **[VERIFIED]** Oversubscription lotteries deliver credible causal estimates: Boston charter admission lotteries (random assignment among applicants) showed each year at an oversubscribed charter raised scores ~0.2 SD in ELA and >0.33 SD in math per year — for low-income students, roughly enough to close the Black-White test-score gap in a year. Lottery offer vs. no-offer removes selection bias by construction. (Source: Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist, Dynarski, Kane & Pathak, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2011 — https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/The%20Quarterly%20Journal%20of%20Economics-2011-Abdulkadir.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Lottery design also rebuts the "cream-skimming"/ability-interaction worry directly relevant to a qualified-applicant lottery: none of the estimated own-ability interaction terms for charter applicants differed significantly from zero, i.e., effects did not depend on the applicant being high-ability. (Source: Abdulkadiroğlu et al., *QJE* 2011; consistent with Angrist et al. KIPP Lynn, *AER* 2010)

## C6 — Observational program-effect claims are routinely overturned by rigorous designs

- **[VERIFIED]** The exam-school case is a direct education example of an observational impression overturned by a rigorous design: selective-admission schools *look* transformative on raw outcomes, but a regression-discontinuity design found near-zero causal achievement effect — the apparent effect was selection. (Source: Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist & Pathak, *Econometrica* 2014 — https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/The%20elite%20illusion.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** Classic cross-design reversal (mechanism transfers to any confounded observational claim): hormone-replacement therapy was recommended in 2000 on observational evidence; a 2002 RCT of >16,000 women reversed it, showing *increased* risk of coronary disease, breast tumor, thromboembolism, and stroke. The explanation — unmeasured confounders (exercise, smoking, education, income) — is exactly the selection problem in selected-cohort education claims. (Source: reviewed in Pharmaco-Vigilance, "Discrepancies between observational studies and RCTs" — https://www.pharmaco-vigilance.eu/content/discrepancies-between-observational-studies-and-randomized-controlled-trials)
- **[VERIFIED]** Caveat on the strength of the general claim (keep our framing honest): a systematic review of 56 nephrology meta-analyses found observational and RCT *point estimates* often agree on average (ratio of odds ratios 1.05, 95% CI 0.90–1.23) — but nearly half led to *discrepant statistical-significance interpretations*. So the reliable claim is "observational designs can't be trusted to give the same decision," not "they always inflate effects." (Source: *Scientific Reports* 11, 2021 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-85519-5)
- **[VERIFIED]** Lack of blinding (an observational-style design weakness) inflates measured effects: trials with inadequate patient blinding showed effect sizes larger by d = 0.12–0.19 (95% CIs excluding 0) than properly blinded trials. (Source: meta-epidemiological analysis, PMC5960173 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5960173/)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Education-specific RCT-vs-observational comparison (closes the C6 gap flagged in Sweep 1):* across **645** program-evaluation studies from 12 reviews (preschool, reading, math, science), quasi-experimental designs produced a mean effect size of **+0.23 (N = 449)** vs. only **+0.16 for randomized experiments (N = 196)** — i.e., non-randomized designs systematically overstate program effects, attributed to selection bias. Effect sizes were also ~2× larger for published vs. unpublished, small- vs. large-scale, and experimenter-made vs. independent measures. (Source: Cheung & Slavin, "How Methodological Features Affect Effect Sizes in Education," *Educational Researcher* 45(5):283–292, 2016 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X16656615 ; ERIC EJ1106562)
- **[INFERENCE]** The Cheung & Slavin gap (+0.23 vs +0.16, a ~44% relative inflation) is a within-education quantification of exactly the selection-bias mechanism the Elite Illusion RD exposed — it means an observational read of our own selected cohort would be expected to *overstate* program effect by a similar margin absent randomization, which is precisely what the lottery removes.

## C7 — The design is feasible and precedented (lotteries already run in practice)

_(Seed: SSP International runs a 2026 lottery-among-qualified with a waitlist control group — see `research/comparators/SSP_SELECTION_CASE_STUDY.md`.)_

- **[VERIFIED]** Admission lotteries among oversubscribed applicants are a well-established, real-world mechanism, not a hypothetical: Boston charter schools with more applicants than seats used binding, statistically independent admission lotteries, and researchers used those existing lotteries as the source of random assignment. This is precedent that scarcity-driven lotteries are administratively feasible and defensible. (Source: Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist, Dynarski, Kane & Pathak, *QJE* 2011 — https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/The%20Quarterly%20Journal%20of%20Economics-2011-Abdulkadir.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** The lottery-as-evaluation design has been replicated across networks and cities (KIPP Lynn, *AER* 2010; Boston "no-excuses" charters raising AP-taking and college enrollment, Angrist et al. 2016; scale-up replication in Cohodes, Setren & Walters 2021) — demonstrating the approach generalizes and produces stable estimates when programs expand. (Source: Blueprint Labs / MIT summary — https://blueprintlabs.mit.edu/research/do-elite-exam-schools-add-value/)
- **[INFERENCE]** Feasibility parallel for our design: like these charters, our program has genuine scarcity (more qualified applicants than seats) and can run a lottery *among qualified applicants* — the same structure that made the charter estimates credible — while the qualifying bar preserves the capability floor the superbuilders care about.
- **[VERIFIED]** *Sample-size / power benchmark for a lottery evaluation (closes the C7 gap flagged in Sweep 1):* the federal gold-standard lottery study estimated charter impacts from **2,330 lottery applicants** (winners vs. losers) across 36 oversubscribed charter middle schools in 15 states — a concrete N at which admission-lottery random assignment yielded publishable, adequately-powered achievement estimates. (Source: Gleason, Clark, Tuttle & Dwoyer, *The Evaluation of Charter School Impacts: Final Report*, NCEE 2010-4029, IES/Mathematica — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED510573.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Feasibility caveat to plan around:* oversubscription is rarer than assumed — only ~**10–15%** of charter schools were oversubscribed enough to support a lottery study, and oversubscribed schools differed systematically (longer days, more likely to offer gifted programs, higher teacher salaries). Implication for us: a lottery is credible *only* under genuine excess demand, which our qualified-applicant scarcity supplies, but the design must confirm oversubscription before relying on it. (Source: Gleason et al. 2010 / national charter lottery methodology literature — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272775711001051)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Recent scale reference:* a 2023 kindergarten lottery evaluation drew on **2,310 applicants** across 14 oversubscribed lotteries at 9 Core Knowledge charter schools — confirming that single-network, single-grade lottery evaluations at a scale comparable to a district program remain feasible and are still being published. (Source: Core Knowledge kindergarten lottery evaluation, EdWorkingPaper AI23-755 — https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai23-755.pdf)

## C8 — A high-ceiling outcome can measure growth across the gifted range

- **[VERIFIED]** MAP Growth reports on a continuous, grade-independent vertical RIT (Rasch Unit) scale explicitly "designed to measure academic growth over time" and to compare achievement "independent of grade" — the mechanism that lets it register above-grade-level performance rather than topping out at grade-level content. (Source: NWEA MAP Help, Growth and Norms — https://teach.mapnwea.org/impl/maphelp/Content/Data/GrowthInsights.htm)
- **[VERIFIED]** 2025 MAP Growth norms are built on very large samples — expected-growth norms from **13.8 million students** — giving stable above-grade reference points for interpreting high scorers. (Source: NWEA 2025 MAP Growth Norms quick reference / Technical Manual — https://www.nwea.org/resource-center/white-paper/88182/MAP-Growth-Norms_NWEA_Technical-Manual.pdf/)
- **[VERIFIED]** Ceiling-effect risk is real and must be managed: NWEA's own documentation notes high-starting students "generally do not raise their score as much," and expected growth *compresses* in upper grades (e.g., fall-to-winter expected growth 6 RIT in grades 5–6 vs. only 4 RIT in grades 7–8) — so raw RIT gain understates gifted growth, and conditional growth percentiles (growth vs. peers with the same starting score) are the appropriate high-ceiling metric. (Source: NWEA, Growth and Norms — https://teach.mapnwea.org/impl/maphelp/Content/Data/GrowthInsights.htm)
- **[VERIFIED]** Above-level testing is the validated precedent for measuring the gifted range without ceiling effects: SMPY used the SAT with under-13s precisely because "the SAT's high ceiling for this age group distinguishes the able from the exceptionally able." Age-13 above-level scores predicted doctorates, patents, tenure at top-50 universities, and income decades later — and *more ability mattered even within the top 1%*, i.e., the high-ceiling measure captured meaningful variation a grade-level test would have flattened. (Source: Lubinski & Benbow, SMPY 35-year review, 2006; Wikipedia SMPY overview — https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-my/wp-content/uploads/sites/826/2013/02/14084655/DoingPsychScience2006.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Primary-source ability-gradient figures (closes the C8 gap flagged in Sweep 1):* the high-ceiling age-13 SAT discriminates *within* the top 1%. Splitting **N = 2,329** top-1% participants into quartiles by age-13 SAT-M+SAT-V composite (Q1 mean 858 → Q4 mean 1231), the top vs. bottom quartile odds ratios 25+ years later were: doctorates **OR = 3.56**, STEM publication (≥1) **4.55**, patents (≥1) **2.31**, literary publications (≥1) **3.01**, income in 95th percentile **4.97** — all with 95% CIs excluding 1.0. A grade-level test topping out at the 99th percentile would have flattened this entire spread. (Source: Kell, Lubinski & Benbow, *Psychological Science* 25(12):2217–2232, 2014, Fig. 19.2 — https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2014-kell.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED]** *Profoundly-gifted band (top 1 in 10,000; primary source):* within the **320** individuals identified at age 13 as top-1-in-10,000 (age-13 cutoffs SAT-M ≥ 700, SAT-V ≥ 630, pre-recentering), **44% held doctorates** (vs. 2% of the U.S. population and 23% of the broader top 1%), **15% held ≥1 patent** (vs. ~1% of the U.S. population), and by age 38 **>11% held university tenure (>7% at research-intensive institutions)** — demonstrating a high-ceiling age-13 measure forecasts extreme-range outcomes a ceiling-bound test cannot. (Source: Kell, Lubinski & Benbow, *Psychological Science* 2014 — https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2014-kell.pdf)

## Guardrails & claim boundaries (H7, H8, R10)

- **[VERIFIED] (H8 — broadening access need not harm current high performers):** A large study of gifted ability grouping found "gifted grouping does not help or hurt the achievement growth of gifted students nor does it help or hurt the achievement growth of non-gifted students" — i.e., neutral effects on top performers. (Source: Card & Giuliano / UConn NCRGE working paper on ability grouping — https://ncrge.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/982/2019/04/The-Effects-of-Ability-Grouping-of-Gifted-Students-on-Gifted-and-Non-Gifted-Achievement-Growth.pdf)
- **[VERIFIED] (H8):** Broadening access via a gifted/high-achiever program produced large gains for underrepresented students *with no spillover harm to others*: minority participants gained ~0.5 SD in 4th-grade reading and math (persisting through 6th grade), and "no evidence of negative or positive spillovers on nonparticipants." (Source: Card & Giuliano, gifted/high-achiever classroom study — cited in NCRGE/EBSCO ability-grouping literature; see also Fordham Institute review — https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/research/think-again-are-education-programs-high-achievers-inherently-inequitable)
- **[VERIFIED] (H8, contested-evidence flag):** The "untracking doesn't harm high achievers" literature is genuinely contested — a 100-year meta-analysis found a *positive* overall effect of ability grouping (Steenbergen-Hu, Makel & Olszewski-Kubilius, 2016), and critics note Slavin's influential heterogeneous-grouping work omitted the top ~5% of students. Honest framing: our design should *monitor* high-performer outcomes, not assume zero effect. (Source: Steenbergen-Hu et al., *Review of Educational Research*, 2016, summarized — https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/education/ability-grouping)
- **[VERIFIED] (H7 — equity monitoring):** Card & Giuliano provide the monitoring template: track gifted-identification rates *by subgroup* (FRL/ELL, Black, Hispanic) against a comparison group, using difference-in-difference (their school-level effect was +1.6 ppt, SE 0.3). Subgroup identification rate is a measurable, defensible equity indicator. (Source: Card & Giuliano, *PNAS* 2016 — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113)
- **[INFERENCE] (R10 — claim boundaries):** The evidence pile keeps four claim types distinct: *access* (who gets screened/identified — Card & Giuliano, Grissom & Redding), *reliability* (does the measure classify consistently — SEM/regression-to-mean findings), *predictive validity* (does the signal forecast later achievement — SMPY, Wai et al.), and *causal program impact* (RCT/RD/lottery — Elite Illusion, Boston charters). A number defends only its own claim type; e.g., SMPY predictive validity is *not* evidence of program impact, and a high raw-outcome cohort is *not* evidence of access equity.
