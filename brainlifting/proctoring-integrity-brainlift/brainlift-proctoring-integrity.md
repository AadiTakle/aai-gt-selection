# BrainLift: What Can Actually Be **Observed** When a Child Takes a High-Stakes Reasoning Test at Home

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose

A computer-delivered adaptive reasoning assessment taken by a child, possibly at home, has to answer a question no proctoring technology was designed for: not *is the right person alone in the room*, but *did this score come from this child's reasoning*? This BrainLift asks what published evidence actually establishes about that question — how much of the threat any proctoring modality can physically observe, what a statistical flag is worth once real base rates are applied, how an adaptive item bank leaks under its own selection rule, what surveillance costs the examinee and what the law now permits when the examinee is a minor, and which of the two threats proctoring cannot see — a frontier model beside the keyboard, or a helpful adult beside the child — the evidence supports treating as dominant. The organising discipline is that *deterrence*, *detection*, *identification*, and *evidence sufficient to act on a person* are four different claims resting on four different evidence bases, and this literature conflates them constantly. It argues from external evidence and general principles only, and is written to be read with no knowledge of any particular system or implementation.

### In Scope

- **Proctoring modalities and their measured efficacy** — the taxonomy, the meta-analytic size of the proctoring effect on ability scores, independently measured detection sensitivity and specificity, documented evasion, and deterrence effects that require no monitoring at all.
- **Statistical and psychometric detection of test fraud** — person-fit, answer-similarity, preknowledge, erasure, and response-time indices; their measured power; the base-rate arithmetic that converts a flag into a posterior probability; and the evidentiary standards governing action on a flag.
- **Item and item-bank security under adaptive delivery** — exposure control and what it costs in precision, the concentration produced by maximum-information selection, organised item theft, drift-based leak detection, and automatic item generation as an escape from bank security.
- **Fairness, privacy, and law when the examinee is a minor** — measured demographic differential in the detection machinery, the disability flagging pathway, what courts and data-protection regulators have actually decided, the statutory regimes aimed specifically at children, and the environmental preconditions remote proctoring imposes.
- **The two threats proctoring was not built for** — frontier-model capability on abstract reasoning delivered as text versus as images, whether model assistance is detectable from response data, coaching as the legal inflation channel, and adult presence during a child's test.
- **Provenance discipline throughout** — what is peer-reviewed, what is vendor-published, what is secondhand, what was legally decided versus merely alleged, and what was searched for and not found.

### Out of Scope

- **The structure and sequencing of the test experience** — adaptive routing, item ordering, session design, and retake policy are owned by the companion `test-structure` BrainLift. This one asks what can be *observed* about a test session, not how the session should be shaped.
- **Instrument-level measurement validity and tail-quality metrics** — owned by the companion `gifted-assessment-quality` BrainLift.
- **The statistical methodology for validating a whole instrument** against an established test — reliability machinery, classification and decision accuracy, agreement and linking, DIF and invariance — is owned by the companion `test-evaluation` BrainLift.
- **Which measured signals earn their place in an ability estimate** — the incremental validity of response time, process telemetry, distractor choice, confidence, and learning rate is owned by the companion `metric-evidence` BrainLift. This one treats those same signals only as *integrity* evidence, which is a different question with a different standard of proof.
- **Causal identification** — separating a programme's effect from selection into it is owned by the companion `gt-school-counterfactual` BrainLift.
- **Any mapping of these findings onto a particular system, configuration, policy, or plan** — belongs in a separate design document, not this BrainLift.

### Category 1: Proctoring modalities and measured efficacy

*General idea: "Proctoring" is not one intervention but a family of them — a live human watching through a webcam, a recording reviewed after the fact, an algorithm flagging behaviour for later human adjudication, a browser that locks the machine down, and the null condition of nobody watching — and they differ enormously in what they are physically capable of observing. This category assembles what has actually been measured about that family: how much scores move when supervision is added or removed, how accurate automated flagging is when anyone independent tests it, whether the deterrent effect of being watched is separable from the detection effect of being caught, and what being surveilled while tested does to performance. The evidence is unusually contentious: vendor efficacy narratives and independent peer-reviewed evaluations reach nearly opposite conclusions about the same products, the one meta-analytic estimate of the proctoring effect is far smaller than practitioner intuition suggests, and the literature is close to silent on whether any of it transfers to young children tested at home.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: The modality taxonomy and the measured size of the proctoring effect on scores**
  - **Source:** Harerimana, Mchunu & Mtshali (2026); Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs (2020); Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri (2021); Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Proctoring modalities separate by dependence on human versus machine oversight and by synchronous versus asynchronous format, yielding live online proctoring, automated/algorithmic proctoring, record-and-review, biometric identity verification, lockdown browsers, and hybrid automated-plus-human designs. *(Harerimana, Mchunu & Mtshali, 2026, *International Journal for Educational Integrity* 22, article 19, DOI 10.1007/s40979-026-00229-7.)* `T2`
      - A systematic search of six databases for 2015–2025 returned 795 records but only six studies met inclusion criteria for remote proctoring in nursing-student assessment, all published between 2021 and 2024. *(Harerimana, Mchunu & Mtshali, 2026, DOI 10.1007/s40979-026-00229-7.)* `T2`
      - A three-level random-effects meta-analysis of 109 effect sizes from 49 studies (total N = 100,434) found unproctored ability-test scores higher than proctored ones by a pooled Δ = 0.20, 95% CI [0.10, 0.31]. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, *European Journal of Psychological Assessment* 36(1):174–184, DOI 10.1027/1015-5759/a000494.)* `T2`
      - That meta-analysis found significantly smaller unproctored-score inflation for measures whose content is difficult to research on the internet. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, DOI 10.1027/1015-5759/a000494.)* `T2`
      - Its moderator test for implemented countermeasures against cheating — honesty contracts, follow-up verification testing, and similar measures written into testing guidelines — returned no significant effect. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, DOI 10.1027/1015-5759/a000494.)* `T2`
      - A subsample of 5 studies contributing 15 effect sizes (total N = 1,280) indicated considerable rank-order change between proctored and unproctored administration, ρ = .58, 95% CI [.38, .78]. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, preprint record, DOI 10.31234/osf.io/wxj4y.)* `T2` `[rests on only 5 studies; weakly estimated, and reported in the preprint rather than the published mean-difference result]`
      - Across two field experiments, students whose exams were not proctored scored "over 11% higher on average" than those whose exams were proctored. *(Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri, 2021, *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics* 90:101653, DOI 10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653.)* `T2`
      - That score gap was "much smaller when a web-based proctoring tool was used instead of an in-class live proctor," making the modality rather than the presence of proctoring the dominant variable. *(Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri, 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653.)* `T2`
      - The same authors present evidence that peer collaboration rather than test anxiety is the primary mechanism behind the proctored/unproctored difference, while stating their data do not rule out anxiety. *(Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri, 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653.)* `T2`
      - A later working-paper treatment by an overlapping author team frames anxiety as explaining part of a 7.8% proctor-induced score reduction. *(RedNIE Working Paper 320, 2024.)* `[working paper; not peer reviewed]`
      - In a webcam-proctored versus unproctored experiment, proctored participants scored lower only on the test whose answers were searchable online and not on the logical-reasoning test whose answers were not, and the authors characterise the decrease as very small. *(Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend, 2014, *Journal of Business and Psychology* 29(4):555–572, DOI 10.1007/s10869-014-9343-z.)* `T2`
      - In the same experiment proctored participants reported higher perceived pressure and greater privacy concerns, and more of them withdrew from testing than in the unproctored condition. *(Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend, 2014, DOI 10.1007/s10869-014-9343-z.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The best-powered estimate of the proctoring effect on ability-test scores is one fifth of a standard deviation, not the collapse implied by "unproctored tests are worthless." Two moderators matter more than that headline: inflation shrinks when the content cannot be looked up, and the countermeasures the industry recommends showed no measurable effect at all. The thinly estimated rank-order result is the more alarming one for any ranked decision, and the modalities are not substitutes — a live in-person proctor moved scores far more than a web-based tool in the same research programme. Proctoring also changed *who finished*, which is a selection problem rather than a measurement one.
    - **Link to source:** [Scoping review of remote proctoring modalities](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40979-026-00229-7), [Meta-analysis of proctored versus unproctored ability scores](https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759/a000494), [Field experiment on proctoring modality](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653), [Non-peer-reviewed working-paper version emphasising anxiety](https://ideas.repec.org/p/aoz/wpaper/320.html), [Cheating, reactions, and performance under remote proctoring](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-014-9343-z)

- **Subcategory 1.2: Measured detection performance of automated proctoring, and who adjudicates a flag**
  - **Source:** Bergmans, Bouali, Luttikhuis & Rensink (2021); Burgess, Ginsberg, Felten & Cohney (2022); Simko, Hutchinson, Isaac, Fries, Sherr & Aviv (2024); Heinrich (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a controlled trial of 30 computer-science students where 6 were instructed to cheat, automated analysis flagged none of the 6 cheaters and an independent human reviewer caught 1 of 6. *(Bergmans, Bouali, Luttikhuis & Rensink, 2021, *Proceedings of CSEDU 2021*, pp. 279–290, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
      - False positives in that trial were 0% for the automated detection and 4% for the human review, which the authors describe as "relatively high specificity … which, however, is quite useless in the light of the disastrous sensitivity." *(Bergmans et al., 2021, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
      - The authors conclude the system's sensitivity "should therefore be put at very close to zero" and that using online proctoring "is best compared to taking a placebo: it has some positive influence, not because it works but because people believe that it works." *(Bergmans et al., 2021, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
      - Despite that measured sensitivity, a clear majority of the same students believed use of the system would prevent cheating. *(Bergmans et al., 2021, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
      - The trial's stated limits are n = 30 with six cheaters, a single product, and a computer-science cohort whose technical ability the authors describe as far above average. *(Bergmans et al., 2021, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2` `[scope limit stated by the authors themselves]`
      - Reverse-engineering four proctoring suites used in 93% of U.S. law schools and 100% of remote state bar exams, the authors report that "despite promises of high-security, all their anti-cheating measures can be trivially bypassed and can pose significant user security risks." *(Burgess, Ginsberg, Felten & Cohney, 2022, *31st USENIX Security Symposium*.)* `T2`
      - The facial-recognition classifier used by the market-leading suite — a publicly available pre-trained model rather than a purpose-built one — showed an average false non-match rate of 11.47% on datasets the authors selected to resemble live exam capture conditions. *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - False non-match rates varied by the subject's labelled race, with subjects labelled 'Black' showing higher rates than those labelled 'White' on the two largest datasets for all classifiers except one. *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that vendors can pick classifiers with the lowest false non-match rate but that those classifiers "all present much higher FMRs, suggesting they provide little more than security theater." *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - They identify a structural limit rather than an implementation bug: because the software runs on hardware the test-taker administers, "the student has full administrative access and will always be able to bypass security features and monitoring schemes given enough time." *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - That paper attributes the Δ = 0.20 proctored/unproctored meta-analytic result to "Teclehaimanot et al." when the finding is Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs (2020). *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2` `[citation error in an otherwise sound source; the misattribution should not be propagated]`
      - Qualitative analysis of 137 YouTube and TikTok videos and 4,297 comments documented a community sharing both non-technical evasion methods such as sticky notes and "deeply technical (e.g., custom virtual machines) methods of evading proctoring." *(Simko, Hutchinson, Isaac, Fries, Sherr & Aviv, 2024, *CCS '24*, pp. 2681–2695, DOI 10.1145/3658644.3691638.)* `T2`
      - Because rule violations are not easily identifiable — the review's example is whether a student "look[ed] up in contemplation or did they look at someone providing information" — "the raising of flags needs to be overcautious, necessitating a large proportion of false positives." *(Heinrich, 2025, *Open Praxis* 17(3), DOI 10.55982/openpraxis.17.3.836.)* `T2`
      - Large-scale study of online proctoring accuracy is described as "something currently not possible" because flag data are not reported in any standardised, aggregatable form. *(Heinrich, 2025, DOI 10.55982/openpraxis.17.3.836, citing Dawson 2023.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The only published controlled sensitivity test of a named commercial automated proctoring product found sensitivity near zero against technically capable cheaters while specificity was perfect. Security research locates the reason structurally rather than in immature models — the adversary owns the machine, so no feature set converts remote proctoring into a hard control — and evasion instructions circulate as consumer-platform tutorials rather than as specialist knowledge. High false-positive rates are meanwhile a design choice, because a flag is a referral for human judgement, and nobody outside the vendors can currently audit flag accuracy at scale.
    - **Link to source:** [Controlled trial of automated proctoring efficacy](https://doi.org/10.5220/0010399602790290), [Open author copy](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2021/103996/103996.pdf), [Bias and vulnerability in remote proctoring software](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-burgess.pdf), [Community-developed proctoring evasion techniques](https://doi.org/10.1145/3658644.3691638), [Systematic-narrative review of online proctoring systems](https://doi.org/10.55982/openpraxis.17.3.836)

- **Subcategory 1.3: Human detection sensitivity, measured on a different cheating channel**
  - **Source:** Dawson & Sutherland-Smith (2017); Dawson & Sutherland-Smith (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Seven experienced markers blind-marking 20 assignments of which 6 were purchased detected the purchased work 62% of the time and correctly identified genuine student work 96% of the time. *(Dawson & Sutherland-Smith, 2017, *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 43(2):286–293, DOI 10.1080/02602938.2017.1336746.)* `T2`
      - In a pre-post training study across four disciplines, marker sensitivity rose from 58% to 82% (statistically significant) while specificity rose from 83% to 87% (not significant). *(Dawson & Sutherland-Smith, 2019, *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 44(5):715–725, DOI 10.1080/02602938.2018.1531109.)* `T2`
      - The authors describe the 2017 result as a pilot whose findings "should be taken with caution as they are from one course unit in one discipline." *(Dawson & Sutherland-Smith, 2017, DOI 10.1080/02602938.2017.1336746.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The only well-quantified human detection sensitivities in the integrity literature come from outsourced written work rather than live exam surveillance, and they run 58–82% — far above the near-zero automated sensitivity measured against live technical cheating. Training moved sensitivity 24 points without costing specificity, which is the opposite of the trade-off that tuning an automated flagger imposes. These figures are not transferable to webcam proctoring and should not be quoted as if they were.
    - **Link to source:** [Can markers detect contract cheating (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2017.1336746), [Training and detection accuracy (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2018.1531109)

- **Subcategory 1.4: What vendors and adopting institutions claim about the same systems**
  - **Source:** Proctoring-vendor corporate publications; California State University Northridge institutional evaluation
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A proctoring vendor's corporate blog states that once proctoring was introduced there was "no difference between scores from face-to-face exams and online exams," attributing this to work by a named accounting professor. *(Proctorio, "Setting the Record Straight: On the Necessity of (Online) Proctoring," proctorio.com.)* `[vendor-sourced; not peer reviewed; no independent publication of the underlying result was located]`

      - A third vendor guide claims AI proctoring accuracy of 90–95% versus 75–85% for human proctors, and that "most institutions clear 60-70% of AI flags after human review." *(Eklavvya, "AI Proctoring Guide," eklavvya.com.)* `[vendor-sourced; the clearance figure is attributed only to unnamed "industry studies"]`

- **Subcategory 1.5: Deterrence without detection**
  - **Source:** Corrigan-Gibbs, Gupta, Northcutt, Cutrell & Thies (2015a, 2015b); Mukherjee, Distler, Lenzini & Koenig (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a 409-student online course with cheating measured directly via a honeypot rather than by self-report, a pre-task warning produced a significant decrease in cheating while an honour code produced a smaller, non-significant effect. *(Corrigan-Gibbs, Gupta, Northcutt, Cutrell & Thies, 2015a, *Learning at Scale '15*, pp. 223–228, DOI 10.1145/2724660.2728663.)* `T2`
      - Across a pair of online exams spanning 632 students in India and a Mechanical Turk task with 2,378 participants, a stern warning spelling out the consequences of cheating produced an approximately twofold reduction in cheating, consistent across both experiments and both geographies. *(Corrigan-Gibbs et al., 2015b, *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction*, DOI 10.1145/2810239.)* `T2`
      - The same studies confirm that honour codes, "despite frequent use in massive open online courses, lead to only a small and insignificant reduction in online cheating behaviors." *(Corrigan-Gibbs et al., 2015b, DOI 10.1145/2810239.)* `T2`
      - In a between-subjects experiment with N = 997 in a realistic online test simulation, an honour-code reminder cut the odds of cheating by 50% (p = 0.006) and a warning message by 44% (p = 0.016). *(Mukherjee, Distler, Lenzini & Koenig, 2023, *Computers & Education*, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
      - In that same experiment a monitoring message telling test-takers they were being watched produced a 32% reduction in cheating odds that was not statistically significant (p = 0.103). *(Mukherjee et al., 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
      - Wald tests in that study showed no significant differences between the three interventions, and none of them significantly affected user experience or test-taking self-efficacy. *(Mukherjee et al., 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
      - The decision to cheat was influenced by how strongly an intervention conveyed that cheating is wrong and by "test-takers' perception of getting caught if they cheated." *(Mukherjee et al., 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Text shown before a task, with no monitoring behind it, roughly halved directly measured cheating across four samples on two continents — a larger measured effect on behaviour than the independently measured detection sensitivity of automated proctoring, at no cost and with no surveillance. The two research programmes disagree about which prime works: one finds honour codes near-useless and warnings effective, the other finds the honour-code reminder strongest and announced monitoring the only non-significant arm. Both converge on the mechanism being the examinee's perceived probability of being caught, which is a belief rather than a detection capability.
    - **Link to source:** [Honour codes versus warnings in an online course](https://doi.org/10.1145/2724660.2728663), [Deterring cheating in online environments](https://doi.org/10.1145/2810239), [Privacy-non-invasive anti-cheating interventions](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925)

- **Subcategory 1.6: What surveillance costs the measurement**

### Category 2: Statistical detection and the base-rate problem

*General idea: A second family of cheat-detection methods ignores the testing room entirely and looks for fraud in the response data — person-fit indices asking whether a response pattern is plausible under a measurement model, similarity indices asking whether two answer strings could have been produced independently, gated mixture models asking whether performance on exposed items outruns performance on fresh items, and response-time statistics treating aberrantly-fast-and-correct answering as the fingerprint of preknowledge. The methodological literature is mature and largely favourable: null distributions have been derived, Type I error is controllable, and power against well-specified alternatives can be respectable. The literature on what a flag is *worth* is far less comfortable. Measured cheating prevalence on real examinations sits in the low single digits, indices are calibrated to a nominal Type I error rate rather than to a posterior probability of guilt, and the resulting positive predictive value of a flag at realistic prevalence is frequently below 50% and sometimes below 15% — which collides with a standards regime granting examinees notice, access to the evidence, and an opportunity to rebut, and with a documented record of flags driving consequential accusations on thin corroboration.*

- **Subcategory 2.1: Person-fit indices — what they detect, and where their power collapses**
  - **Source:** Snijders (2001); Meijer, Molenaar & Sijtsma (1994); Meijer & Sijtsma (1995); van Krimpen-Stoop & Meijer (1996); Karabatsos (2003); Sinharay (2017); Xia & Zheng (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The standardized log-likelihood person-fit index lz is asymptotically standard normal only when true ability is used, and substituting an ability estimate shrinks its variance below 1. *(Snijders, 2001, *Psychometrika* 66(3):331–342, DOI 10.1007/BF02294437.)* `T2`
      - Snijders derived the corrected index lz\*, which restores the standard normal null distribution under estimated ability but applies only to dichotomously scored items. *(Snijders, 2001, DOI 10.1007/BF02294437.)* `T2`
      - Using critical values from the standard normal for lz under estimated ability yields less power than the corrected index because the true variance is smaller than 1. *(Meijer & Sijtsma, 2001, as characterized in Xia & Zheng, 2018, *Applied Psychological Measurement*, PMC6023093.)* `T2`
      - lz provided a high detection rate for cheating-type aberrance only among examinees of low or high ability, not in the middle of the ability range. *(Drasgow et al., 1987, as reviewed in Meijer & Sijtsma, 1995, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame0803_5.)* `T2`
      - On 17-item tests with mean item reliability .34, the nonparametric U3 index detected 73%–80.5% of a-priori aberrant patterns across two replications. *(Meijer, Molenaar & Sijtsma, 1994, DOI 10.1177/014662169401800202.)* `T2`
      - Detection rates rose with item discrimination, test length, and the ratio of aberrant to non-aberrant examinees, and simulees cheating on the hardest items were detected more easily than those guessing throughout. *(Meijer, Molenaar & Sijtsma, 1994, DOI 10.1177/014662169401800202.)* `T2`
      - Increasing the number of misfitting patterns in the calibration sample *decreased* the power of ZU3, because aberrant examinees contaminate the item parameters used to judge them. *(van Krimpen-Stoop & Meijer, 1996, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000204.)* `T2`
      - Karabatsos compared 36 person-fit statistics by ROC area and ranked the nonparametric HT best overall, followed by C, MCI and U3, all four outperforming 25 parametric indices. *(Karabatsos, 2003, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1604_2.)* `T2`
      - Sinharay replicated those simulations and found the parametric lz and ECI4z to be as powerful as HT and U3 once the comparison was performed on equal footing, attributing the original ranking to the comparison design. *(Sinharay, 2017, DOI 10.1080/08957347.2017.1353990.)* `T2` `[contested: directly overturns the preceding fact]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Person-fit indices detect the aberrance they were built for, but only when it is large, and their power collapses precisely in the middle of the ability range where most examinees sit. A structural problem compounds it: the item parameters used to judge an examinee are estimated from a sample that includes the cheaters, so more cheating buys less power. The field also disagrees internally about which family wins, with a headline nonparametric ranking directly overturned by a re-analysis of the same simulations.
    - **Link to source:** [Asymptotic null distribution with estimated person parameter](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02294437), [Test and person characteristics in nonparametric appropriateness measurement](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662169401800202), [Review of aberrant item score pattern detection](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame0803_5), [Deviant patterns and the power of a person-fit statistic](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662169602000204), [Comparing 36 person-fit statistics](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1604_2), [Are nonparametric person-fit statistics more powerful?](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957347.2017.1353990), [Asymptotically normal person-fit indices](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6023093/)

- **Subcategory 2.2: Answer-similarity and collusion indices**
  - **Source:** Wollack (1997); Wollack, Cohen & Serlin (2001); Zopluoglu & Davenport (2012); Maynes (2016); Wollack & Maynes (2016); Eckerly (2021); Wollack (2004)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Wollack's nominal-response-model copying index ω held Type I error at or below the nominal level in all simulated conditions while the classical g2 index yielded substantially inflated rates. *(Wollack, 1997, DOI 10.1177/01466216970214002.)* `T2`
      - ω showed good power only when at least 20% of items were copied on an 80-item test and at least 30% on a 40-item test. *(Wollack, 1997, DOI 10.1177/01466216970214002.)* `T2`
      - Applying copying indices pairwise falsely detected examinees almost three times as often as the nominal α level, and familywise power for ω was reasonable only when at least 30% of items were copied. *(Wollack, Cohen & Serlin, 2001, DOI 10.1177/01466210122032118.)* `T2`
      - Type I error control for g2 failed so badly under familywise conditions that its power could not be meaningfully evaluated at all. *(Wollack, Cohen & Serlin, 2001, DOI 10.1177/01466210122032118.)* `T2`
      - Across 1,440 simulated conditions neither the generalized binomial test nor ω inflated Type I error, with GBT slightly more powerful, and the amount of copying plus source ability dominated all other effects. *(Zopluoglu & Davenport, 2012, DOI 10.1177/0013164412442941.)* `T2`
      - An answer-similarity index estimates the probability that a pair would show the observed or greater response similarity under the assumption that the two worked independently. *(Maynes, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-3.)* `T2`
      - Collusion groups are detected by converting pairwise similarity probabilities into a distance matrix and running nearest-neighbour clustering. *(Wollack & Maynes, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-6.)* `T2`
      - The cluster method is sensitive to the choice of clustering procedure, and its probability statements still apply only to pairs rather than to the clusters actually being accused. *(Eckerly, 2021, DOI 10.1177/01466216211013109.)* `T2`
      - A copying index yields different values depending on which examinee is treated as copier and which as source, so by itself it cannot identify which member of a pair is culpable. *(Wollack, 2004, *The Bar Examiner* 73(2).)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The copying indices with controlled Type I error need roughly a third of the items copied before they are reliably powered, and applying them pairwise across a cohort inflates false detection to nearly three times nominal unless the multiplicity is handled. Two limits are structural rather than fixable by better estimation: the probability statement attaches to a pair while the accusation is often made against a group, and the index cannot say which member of a flagged pair copied from which.
    - **Link to source:** [A nominal response model approach for detecting answer copying](https://doi.org/10.1177/01466216970214002), [Defining error rates and power for detecting answer copying](https://doi.org/10.1177/01466210122032118), [Empirical power and Type I error of GBT and ω](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164412442941), [Detecting potential collusion using similarity analysis](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315743097-3), [Detection of test collusion using cluster analysis](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315743097-6), [Answer similarity analysis at the group level](https://doi.org/10.1177/01466216211013109), [Detecting answer copying on high-stakes exams](https://testing.wisc.edu/May2004BE_JamesWollack.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.3: Preknowledge, erasure, and tampering models**
  - **Source:** Shu, Henson & Luecht (2013); McLeod, Lewis & Thissen (2003); Wollack, Cohen & Eckerly (2015); Sinharay & Johnson (2017); Sinharay (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The Deterministic Gated IRT Model decomposes observed performance into a true-ability and a cheating-ability distribution by conditioning on whether each item was exposed or unexposed. *(Shu, Henson & Luecht, 2013, DOI 10.1007/s11336-012-9311-3.)* `T2`
      - An earlier Bayesian approach detected preknowledge in adaptive testing by comparing performance on suspect and non-suspect item subsets. *(McLeod, Lewis & Thissen, 2003, DOI 10.1177/0146621602250534.)* `T2`
      - The erasure detection index compares observed wrong-to-right erasures to the number expected by chance conditional on ability and number of erased items, and with a continuity correction held Type I error at or below nominal in every condition studied. *(Wollack, Cohen & Eckerly, 2015, DOI 10.1177/0013164414568716.)* `T2`
      - Without the continuity correction the erasure detection index often has inflated Type I error, and with it the index loses power. *(Sinharay & Johnson, 2017, DOI 10.1177/0013164416632287.)* `T2` `[contested: directly qualifies the preceding fact]`
      - Erasure analysis in several U.S. states used only the average wrong-to-right erasure count for a school or district, a statistic whose power has been shown to be rather low. *(Sinharay, 2018, DOI 10.1080/15366367.2018.1437308.)* `T2`
      - 33 states reported using erasure analysis to detect possible fraudulent test-taking behaviour in K–12 assessments in 2013. *(Government Accountability Office, 2013, as cited in Sinharay, 2018, ERIC ED582037.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Model-based preknowledge detection works by splitting performance on items the examinee could have seen from performance on items they could not, which requires already knowing which items were exposed. The erasure family shows the recurring trade directly: the version with controlled Type I error is the version that loses power, and the variant most widely deployed by states was the low-power school-average count rather than the examinee-level index.
    - **Link to source:** [Deterministic gated IRT model for item compromise](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-012-9311-3), [Bayesian detection of item preknowledge in CAT](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621602250534), [Detecting test tampering using item response theory](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164414568716), [Three new methods for analysis of answer changes](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164416632287), [Application of Bayesian methods for detecting fraudulent behavior](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED582037.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.4: Response-time signatures at both extremes**
  - **Source:** Qian, Staniewska & Reckase (2016); Sinharay (2020); Sinharay & Johnson (2019); Wise & Kong (2005); Soland, Kuhfeld & Rios (2021); Wise (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Item preknowledge is operationalized as an unexpectedly short response time paired with a correct response. *(Qian, Staniewska & Reckase, 2016, DOI 10.1111/emip.12102.)* `T2`
      - In a 111-item non-adaptive licensure examination, response-time analysis flagged 2 items as potentially exposed and 2 of 1,172 candidates as showing indications of preknowledge. *(Qian et al., 2016, DOI 10.1111/emip.12102.)* `T2`
      - On a companion adaptive licensure examination the same method found no indication of item preknowledge or compromised items at all. *(Qian et al., 2016, DOI 10.1111/emip.12102.)* `T2`
      - On one flagged item, 26 of 34 rapid responses (76.5%) were correct, far above the chance rate that benign rapid guessing would produce. *(Qian et al., 2016, DOI 10.1111/emip.12102.)* `T2`
      - A supporting simulation found that when examinees knew 10% of items or fewer, the response-time procedure detected 67 of 100 true preknowledge cases. *(Qian & Staniewska, 2013, as reported in Qian et al., 2016, DOI 10.1111/emip.12102.)* `[secondhand; reported through the later paper]`
      - Sinharay's Λs statistic quantifies the difference in speed between compromised and non-compromised items and is provably standard normal under the null of no preknowledge. *(Sinharay, 2020, DOI 10.1177/0146621620909893.)* `T2`
      - On real data at a nominal 1% level, the response-time person-fit statistic χpf produced Type I error rates of 0.062–0.063 — roughly six times nominal — while Λs produced 0.005–0.009. *(Sinharay, 2020, PMC7433384, Table 1.)* `T2`
      - Bayesian response-time residuals were also inflated at a nominal 1% level, reaching 0.055 when 10 items were compromised. *(Sinharay, 2020, PMC7433384, Table 1.)* `T2`
      - Λs power exceeded 0.8 only when the speed shift was 2 or 3 units and at least 4 items were compromised. *(Sinharay, 2020, DOI 10.1177/0146621620909893.)* `T2`
      - Across a 3×3 crossing of percent-items-compromised by percent-examinees-with-preknowledge, Λs was expected to yield low power in 4 cells and outright unreliable results in 4 more, leaving one cell of large power. *(Sinharay, 2020, PMC7433384, Table 3.)* `T2`
      - Detection statistics for test fraud are typically applied at conservative levels such as 1% rather than the customary 5%. *(Wollack, Cohen & Eckerly, 2015, as cited in Sinharay, 2020, PMC7433384.)* `T2`
      - Sinharay cautions that the response-time statistic "should not be used as a sole measure to detect test fraud" and should serve as secondary evidence in high-stakes contexts. *(Sinharay, 2020, PMC7433384.)* `T2`
      - van der Linden and Guo warned against the mechanical use of response-time statistics in high-stakes cheating detection because of their false-alarm rates. *(van der Linden & Guo, 2008, as characterized in Sinharay, 2020, PMC7433384.)* `T2`
      - A statistic combining item scores and response times flagged examinees detected by neither the score-only nor the time-only statistic, with a false-alarm rate always below nominal in contaminated data sets. *(Sinharay & Johnson, 2019, DOI 10.1111/bmsp.12187.)* `T2`
      - Response Time Effort is the proportion of items on which an examinee exceeded an item-specific time threshold rather than responding too quickly to have read the item. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - The validity criterion for a rapid-guessing threshold is that responses below it should be correct at approximately chance rate. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - The operational normative threshold sets the rapid-guessing cutoff at 10% of an item's mean response duration capped at 10 seconds, validated by showing flagged responses were correct at near-chance rates. *(Wise & Ma, 2012, as described in Soland, Kuhfeld & Rios, 2021, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00100-w.)* `T2`
      - Four different threshold-setting methods — fixed, surface-feature, visual-inspection, and two-state mixture model — produced only minor differences in resulting effort scores. *(Wise, 2006, ERIC ED490202.)* `[conference/technical report]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Response time carries information score aberrance does not: fast-and-correct is a sharper preknowledge signature than score patterns alone, and a combined statistic catches examinees that neither component catches alone. But the same real-data comparison validating the targeted statistic showed a general-purpose response-time person-fit statistic firing at six times its nominal rate, and the targeted statistic is well-powered in only a narrow band of conditions. The same short latency reads as disengagement or as fraud depending entirely on whether the fast responses turn out to be correct.
    - **Link to source:** [Using response time to detect item preknowledge](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12102), [Open copy](https://www.ncsbn.org/public-files/Qian_et_al-2016-Educational_Measurement-_Issues_and_Practice.pdf), [Detection of item preknowledge using response times](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621620909893), [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7433384/), [Item scores and response times combined](https://doi.org/10.1111/bmsp.12187), [Response time effort](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2), [Comparing response time threshold setting methods](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-021-00100-w), [Response time threshold setting methods (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED490202.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.5: The base-rate arithmetic — what a flag is actually worth**
  - **Source:** Skorupski & Wainer (2016); Sinharay (2018); Severo, Silva-Pereira, Ferreira, Monteiro & Pereira (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - With 70,000 examinees, 5% cheaters, and a statistic that is 99% sensitive and 99% specific, 3,465 true and 665 false positives yield 4,130 flags and a positive predictive value of 0.84 — a 16% false-positive rate among flags, not the nominal 1%. *(Skorupski & Wainer, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-18.)* `T2`
      - The same statistic at 1% prevalence produces 693 true and 693 false positives out of 1,386 flags — a positive predictive value of exactly 0.50. *(Skorupski & Wainer, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-18.)* `T2`
      - At 1% prevalence and a threshold of 2 standard errors (α ≈ 0.023), the posterior probability of cheating given a flag is 0.18 when the cheater distribution is centred 2 SD out and 0.31 when it is centred 5 SD out. *(Skorupski & Wainer, 2016, Table 18.3, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-18.)* `T2`
      - Raising the threshold to 3 SD (α ≈ 1.35×10⁻³) lifts the posterior probability of cheating to 0.54–0.88, and to 4 SD (α ≈ 3.17×10⁻⁵) lifts it to 0.88–0.99. *(Skorupski & Wainer, 2016, Table 18.3, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-18.)* `T2`
      - The detection threshold matters more than the separation between cheater and non-cheater distributions, because with 99% of the distribution non-cheating only a threshold excluding practically all non-cheaters makes a flag reliable. *(Skorupski & Wainer, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-18.)* `T2`
      - At a 4 SD threshold the posterior probability of cheating is 0.996–0.998 across assumed prevalence priors from 0.001 to 0.5, so a sufficiently extreme threshold makes the prevalence assumption nearly irrelevant. *(Skorupski & Wainer, 2016, Table 18.4, DOI 10.4324/9781315743097-18.)* `T2`
      - In a real licensure examination with 5,640 within-centre examinee pairs, the posterior probability of answer copying exceeded 0.95 only for ω values above about 3.13, the 99.9th percentile of the standard normal. *(Sinharay, 2018, ERIC ED582037.)* `T2`
      - Sinharay concluded from that real-data analysis that "flagging an examinee based on a p-value of, for example, 0.01, is quite likely to yield a false accusation." *(Sinharay, 2018, ERIC ED582037.)* `T2`
      - A peer-reviewed gated-model simulation calibrated to real medical-school examinations reported positive predictive values of 12.71%–14.84% at 5% true cheating prevalence and 29.54%–34.07% at 10% prevalence, using a 0.9 classification cut-off. *(Severo, Silva-Pereira, Ferreira, Monteiro & Pereira, 2019, Table 2, DOI 10.1186/s12909-019-1710-z.)* `T2`
      - Those positive predictive values arose from specificity of 77.8%–78.2% and sensitivity of 60.3%–69.8% in the 5% prevalence conditions. *(Severo et al., 2019, Table 2, DOI 10.1186/s12909-019-1710-z.)* `T2`
      - Positive predictive value only reached 85.3%–88.4% at 35% prevalence and 98.7%–99.1% at 70% prevalence, so the index is trustworthy only where cheating is already the norm. *(Severo et al., 2019, Table 2, DOI 10.1186/s12909-019-1710-z.)* `T2`
      - The measured true prevalence of item preknowledge in those same examinations was 1.2%–3.7%, below the lowest simulated prevalence condition, with apparent prevalence of 0.0%–3.4%. *(Severo et al., 2019, DOI 10.1186/s12909-019-1710-z.)* `T2`
      - Self-reported item preknowledge in the same medical-student population was about 25% and self-reported answer copying at least once ranged from 52% to 67%. *(Severo et al., 2019, DOI 10.1186/s12909-019-1710-z.)* `T2`
      - Across 11 examinations of the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health the detected prevalence of answer copying was 0.1%. *(As cited in Severo et al., 2019, DOI 10.1186/s12909-019-1710-z.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** At the prevalence actually measured on real examinations a flag is close to a coin toss: exactly 0.50 positive predictive value in the canonical 1%-prevalence worked example even with 99% sensitivity and specificity, and 12.7%–14.8% in a peer-reviewed preknowledge simulation at 5% prevalence. Getting the posterior probability of cheating above 0.95 takes thresholds around 3.1–4 standard deviations, far more extreme than the α = 0.01 operational programmes commonly use. Statistically estimated prevalence and self-reported prevalence differ by an order of magnitude in the same population, so even the prior that drives the arithmetic is contested.
    - **Link to source:** [The case for Bayesian methods when investigating test fraud](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315743097-18), [Open chapter text](https://centerforassessment.github.io/Colloquium_2019/img/Papers/Skorupski%20&%20Wainer.pdf), [Application of Bayesian methods for detecting fraudulent behavior](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED582037.pdf), [Item pre-knowledge true prevalence in clinical anatomy](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-019-1710-z), [Detecting test fraud using Bayes factors](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41237-020-00113-9)

- **Subcategory 2.6: What the professional standards require before a flag becomes an action**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014); International Test Commission (2014); Dwyer & Hecht (1995); Wollack (2004)

### Category 3: Item and item-bank security under adaptive delivery

*General idea: Most cheat-detection literature asks how to catch a person; this category asks how the test itself gets stolen. An adaptive test built on maximum-information selection is structurally biased toward leaking its most valuable items first, because the algorithm keeps returning to the same small, highly discriminating subset — so a handful of organised memorisers can compromise the operationally useful core of a bank while leaving most of it untouched. Published exposure-control algorithms exist to flatten that distribution, and the measured cost in precision ranges from essentially zero to a 26% increase in RMSE depending on method and pool, with a comparable further cost attributable to changing the selection rule rather than to exposure control at all. The proposed escape from bank security altogether is automatic item generation — if items are minted from templates or models on demand, there is no fixed bank to steal — but the psychometric bill is real and specific, and the figural case cuts both ways: figural items are the cheapest domain to generate, yet the claim that they resist theft has no published support and generated figural forms have repeatedly failed to behave as parallel forms on retest.*

- **Subcategory 3.1: What exposure control buys and what it charges**
  - **Source:** Georgiadou, Triantafillou & Economides (2007); Kingsbury & Zara (1989); Sympson & Hetter (1985) as described in Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan (2015); Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan (2015); Karagianni & Tsaousis (2026); Leroux, Lopez, Hembry & Dodd (2013); Leroux et al. (2019); Lee & Dodd (2012); Lim & Han (2025); Davis (2002)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Exposure-control strategies partition into randomisation, conditional-probability, stratified, and hybrid families, all of which work by degrading the "always pick the single most informative item" rule. *(Georgiadou, Triantafillou & Economides, 2007, *JTLA* 5(8).)* `T2`
      - Randomesque selection administers one item drawn at random from the top-*k* most informative candidates and, unlike the earlier 5-4-3-2-1 strategy, never reverts to pure maximum-information selection. *(Kingsbury & Zara, 1989, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame0204_6.)* `T2`
      - Sympson–Hetter separates the probability an item is *selected* from the probability it is *administered*, using control parameters derived from prior simulation runs. *(Sympson & Hetter, 1985, as described in Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Sympson–Hetter control parameters are pool-specific and must be recomputed whenever the pool changes, an operational cost the randomisation and hybrid families do not incur. *(Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Stocking and Lewis characterised the exposure/precision relation as a balloon — squeeze one side and the other swells. *(Quoted in Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - In a 500-item pool delivering 25-item adaptive tests, maximum-Fisher-information selection with no exposure control left 293 items (58.6%) never administered in any of 25 replications. *(Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Under that same no-control condition the maximum observed exposure rate reached 1.0, meaning some items were shown to every single examinee. *(Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Adding Sympson–Hetter to maximum-information selection raised RMSE from 0.1748 to 0.1900 in a medium-difficulty pool and from 0.2168 to 0.2734 (+26%) in a hard pool. *(Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Switching from maximum-information to a-stratified selection raised RMSE from 0.1748 to 0.2186 (+25%) before any exposure control was applied at all. *(Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - No condition in that study reached the ideal test-overlap rate of 0.05, the best achieved being 0.097 under a-stratification plus Fade-Away. *(Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `T2`
      - In a 160-item abstract-reasoning bank delivering 30-item adaptive tests to 10,000 simulees, bias ranged only 0.002–0.007 and standard error of estimation only 0.26–0.27 across six exposure-control methods *and* the no-control baseline. *(Karagianni & Tsaousis, 2026, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2026.1769909.)* `T2`
      - In that same study progressive-restricted control used 100% of the bank at 18% mean exposure, while every other method including no control used only 73.13–76.88%. *(Karagianni & Tsaousis, 2026, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2026.1769909.)* `T2`
      - Under no exposure control 44% of administered items exceeded the 0.20 target exposure rate, versus 31% under progressive-restricted. *(Karagianni & Tsaousis, 2026, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2026.1769909.)* `T2`
      - Under a 3PL model, randomesque, Sympson–Hetter and no-control procedures administered at most 52% of a large bank and at most 80% of a small one, while progressive-restricted-standard-error administered almost all items at comparable precision. *(Leroux, Lopez, Hembry & Dodd, 2013, DOI 10.1177/0013164413486802.)* `T2`
      - With a 172-item pool and a predicted-standard-error-reduction stopping rule, no control left 68% of the pool unadministered, randomesque-3 left 62%, randomesque-6 left 56%, and progressive-restricted-standard-error left 0%. *(Leroux et al., 2019, DOI 10.1177/0146621618824856.)* `T2`
      - Maximum-information selection left between 13% and 33% of a polytomous pool unused depending on pool difficulty and ability distribution, while progressive-restricted left 0%. *(Lee & Dodd, 2012, DOI 10.1177/0013164411411296.)* `T2`
      - In a variable-length shadow-test design, maximum-Fisher-information selection left 35.6% of the bank unused, overexposed 10.3% of items, and produced a 33.6% test-overlap rate, all of which a stratification modification cut to 5.0%, 7.2% and 21.4%. *(Lim & Han, 2025, DOI 10.3352/jeehp.2025.22.35.)* `T2`
      - Across three polytomous IRT models, randomesque and within-.10-logits with six-item candidate groups gave the best exposure control per unit of precision lost, while a-stratified and enhanced a-stratified "turned in surprisingly poor performances across all variables." *(Davis, 2002, doctoral dissertation, University of Texas at Austin.)* `[dissertation]`
      - In that same study Sympson–Hetter and conditional Sympson–Hetter did hold exposure to target but were "difficult and time consuming to implement" and disappointing on pool utilisation. *(Davis, 2002.)* `[dissertation]`
      - An early study reported 141 of 260 items never administered under maximum-information selection. *(Hulin, Drasgow & Parsons, 1983, as cited in Boztunç Öztürk & Doğan, 2015.)* `[secondhand; unverified at source]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Plain maximum-information selection strands between 13% and 68% of a bank unused while pushing some items to 100% exposure, and hybrid progressive-restricted methods achieve full pool utilisation. The price of that fix is not a fixed number: it ranges from statistically indistinguishable in two studies to a 26% RMSE increase in a hard pool. A comparable cost — roughly 25% of RMSE — comes from switching the selection rule itself, so studies that change the rule and add exposure control at once overstate what exposure control alone charges. Nobody in this literature reports reaching a theoretically ideal overlap rate.
    - **Link to source:** [Review of item exposure control strategies 1983–2005](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ838610.pdf), [Procedures for selecting items for computerized adaptive tests](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame0204_6), [Investigating item exposure control methods in CAT](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1057460.pdf), [Balancing exposure and efficiency](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2026.1769909), [Comparison of exposure control procedures under the 3PL model](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164413486802), [Exposure control with variable-length CAT under the partial credit model](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621618824856), [Exposure controls, pool characteristics, and population distributions](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164411411296), [Improving item pool utilization with a shadow-test approach](https://doi.org/10.3352/jeehp.2025.22.35), [Strategies for controlling item exposure with polytomous items](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/items/ecc2b4f8-347b-4e79-89b5-b35d84486b8a)

- **Subcategory 3.2: Whether a bigger or split pool can buy security**
  - **Source:** Wainer (2000); Stocking (1994); Chang & Zhang (2002); Zhang & Chang (2005)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Wainer argued from item-usage distributions that pool security rises only linearly as pool size rises exponentially, making "just build a bigger bank" economically infeasible. *(Wainer, 2000, *JEBS* 25(2):203–224, DOI 10.3102/10769986025002203.)* `T2`
      - Retrospective analysis of five operational pools found pools worth six to eight linear tests sufficient for adaptive tests half the length of a parallel linear test, but insufficient for exposure control under continuous administration. *(Stocking, 1994, ETS Research Report Series 1994(1), DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.1994.tb01578.x.)*
      - Test-overlap-rate lower bounds derived from the hypergeometric family let a programme compare an algorithm's observed overlap against the best theoretically achievable, with a large gap indicating the design needs work. *(Chang & Zhang, 2002, *Psychometrika* 67(3):387–398, DOI 10.1007/BF02294991.)* `T2`
      - Splitting a bank into multiple randomly assigned pools reduces the expected number of compromised items an examinee encounters relative to a single whole pool, but only under characterised conditions. *(Zhang & Chang, 2005, ETS Research Report 05-19, DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.2005.tb01996.x.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** One camp holds that pool size cannot be the lever because security scales linearly against exponential cost; the other supplies overlap-rate lower bounds, pool-sizing guidance, and multiple-pool arithmetic that treat pool engineering as workable. They agree on a narrower point that matters more for any always-available test: continuous administration is the regime in which any fixed pool becomes insufficient.
    - **Link to source:** [Rescuing computerized testing by breaking Zipf's law](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986025002203), [Three practical issues for modern adaptive testing item pools](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.1994.tb01578.x), [Hypergeometric family and item overlap rates in CAT](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02294991), [Enhancing test security by using multiple item pools](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.2005.tb01996.x)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Why maximum-information selection is structurally vulnerable to organised theft**
  - **Source:** Yi, Zhang & Chang (2008); Yi, Zhang & Chang (2006); Chang & Zhang (2003) as reported therein
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Maximum-information selection with Sympson–Hetter control administered only 69% of a 480-item pool, shrinking the *effective* pool from 480 to 332 items. *(Yi, Zhang & Chang, 2008, *APM* 32(7):543–558, DOI 10.1177/0146621607311336.)* `T2`
      - That concentration drove the observed average test-overlap rate to 17.5% under maximum-information-plus-Sympson–Hetter versus 10.6% under a-stratified selection with the same control. *(Yi, Zhang & Chang, 2008, DOI 10.1177/0146621607311336.)* `T2`
      - With 30 thieves each memorising 10 items from among the first 1,000 examinees, a later examinee met on average 27.8 of 40 items already compromised under maximum-information-plus-Sympson–Hetter, versus 20.1 under a-stratified and 17.3 under randomised selection. *(Yi, Zhang & Chang, 2008, DOI 10.1177/0146621607311336.)* `T2`
      - Maximum-information selection produced *fewer* distinct compromised items (170) than randomised selection (218) from the same 300 memorisation slots, yet caused more damage because the stolen items were precisely the ones being re-administered. *(Yi, Zhang & Chang, 2008, DOI 10.1177/0146621607311336.)* `T2`
      - The identical theft carried out among the last 5,000 examinees compromised only 10.7 items per examinee versus 27.8 among the first 1,000, making early-pool-life theft roughly 2.6 times as damaging. *(Yi, Zhang & Chang, 2008, DOI 10.1177/0146621607311336.)* `T2`
      - Of the three designs tested, maximum-information selection *with* Sympson–Hetter exposure control was the most vulnerable to organised item theft. *(Yi, Zhang & Chang, 2008, DOI 10.1177/0146621607311336.)* `T2`
      - Under randomised selection, 34 thieves memorising 20 items each compromise 50% of a 1,000-item pool but only 17 are needed for a 500-item pool. *(Chang & Zhang, 2003, as reported in Yi, Zhang & Chang, 2008.)* `[conference paper; secondhand]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The adaptive-specific failure mode is visible directly in the numbers: maximum-information selection produced fewer stolen items than random selection and substantially more damage, because the items it leaks are the ones it keeps re-serving. Capping per-item exposure does not prevent the algorithm from concentrating on a small effective pool, which is why the design combining maximum information with the standard conditional exposure control was the most vulnerable one tested. Timing dominates magnitude — theft during a pool's first tenth of life was roughly 2.6 times as damaging as the same theft later.
    - **Link to source:** [Severity of organized item theft in CAT: a simulation study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621607311336), [Severity of organized item theft in CAT: an empirical study](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.2006.tb02028.x)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Field evidence of bank compromise, and drift-based leak detection**
  - **Source:** Foster (2016); Foster & Zervos (2006) and Maynes (2009) as reported therein; International Test Commission (2014); van der Linden & Glas (2000); Zimmermann, Klusmann & Hampe (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An entire technology-delivered test session, including every question, can be captured automatically by a digital recording system attached to a computer output port. *(International Test Commission, 2014, Guidelines on the Security of Tests.)*
      - Organised memorisation of items for later reconstruction and distribution is the practice the testing industry designates "harvesting." *(International Test Commission, 2014.)*
      - Hundreds of certification test files purchased from braindump sites matched the original published test files at 99–100% content overlap. *(Foster & Zervos, 2006, as reported in Foster, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315871493-13.)* `[conference poster; vendor-sourced; not independently replicated]`
      - One vendor analysis inferred that more than 80% of 598 takers of a popular certification exam used braindump content, and that the pass rate would have been 7% rather than the observed 93% without it. *(Maynes, 2009, as reported in Foster, 2016.)* `[company claim; single exam; not independently replicated]`
      - Server-side theft is the most devastating variety because the complete operational pool is stolen at once rather than item by item. *(Foster, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315871493-13.)*
      - A disclosed item is expected to show drift in its item parameters, which motivates applying statistical quality-control charts to parameters re-estimated from live adaptive data. *(van der Linden & Glas, 2000, *JEBS* 25(4):373–389, DOI 10.3102/10769986025004373.)* `T2`
      - In simulated preknowledge on an operational medical-admissions test, a drop in discrimination combined with a rise in local item dependence detected compromised items with sensitivity 1.0 and specificity .95 when 11 of 80 items were preknown to 10% of takers. *(Zimmermann, Klusmann & Hampe, 2016, *PLOS ONE* 11(12):e0167545, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0167545.)* `T2`
      - In that same study, cheating groups smaller than 5% of test takers were not detected reliably by any indicator. *(Zimmermann, Klusmann & Hampe, 2016, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0167545.)* `T2`
      - Item difficulty can also drop because a cohort was better prepared or the curriculum shifted, so parameter drift alone is not proof of leakage. *(Zimmermann, Klusmann & Hampe, 2016, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0167545.)* `T2`
      - Drift-based leak detection has a bootstrapping problem: the anchor set used to equate two administrations must itself be uncompromised, which cannot be established before equating. *(Zimmermann, Klusmann & Hampe, 2016, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0167545.)* `T2`
      - Item designs that resist theft are rare, and the intuition that items demanding more than factual recall are harder to steal and share has no research support. *(Foster, 2016, DOI 10.4324/9781315871493-13.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Detecting a leak from the data works well above roughly a 5% cheating base rate and fails below it, and the method's own authors note that the equating anchor must already be known clean for it to work at all — so an absence of detected drift is weak evidence of an intact bank. The most-cited real-world compromise figures come from vendor and conference sources rather than independent peer review. The belief that harder-to-verbalise item types resist theft has no research support, and a recorder attached to the output port captures the session regardless of item modality.
    - **Link to source:** [Testing technology and its effects on test security](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315871493-13), [ITC Guidelines on the Security of Tests](https://www.intestcom.org/files/guideline_test_security.pdf), [Detection of known items with a statistical quality control method](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986025004373), [Using local dependence to detect cheating](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0167545)

- **Subcategory 3.5: Automatic item generation as a security strategy, and its psychometric bill**
  - **Source:** Sommer & Arendasy (2025); Bejar (2002) as characterised therein; Sinharay, Johnson & Williamson (2003); Glas & van der Linden (2003); Kosh, Simpson, Bickel, Kellogg & Sanford-Moore (2019); National Academies (2022); Runge et al. (2024) and Attali et al. (2022) as reported in Sommer & Arendasy (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Isomorphs vary only incidental surface features and are hypothesised to carry statistically identical parameters, whereas variants vary radicals and are expected to differ. *(Bejar, 2002, as characterised in Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
      - Calibrating item *models* rather than individual items saves money only when within-family parameter variation is low and adequately modelled, which the reviewers state "is not always the case." *(Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
      - Bayesian hierarchical family expected response functions were developed precisely because items from one family are statistically dependent and cannot be calibrated as independent items. *(Sinharay, Johnson & Williamson, 2003, DOI 10.3102/10769986028004295.)* `T2`
      - Substituting predicted for empirically calibrated item parameters has negligible effect on person estimates only when radicals explain R² ≥ 0.80 of item-parameter variance. *(Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
      - Cognitive-design-system generators achieved correlations of R = 0.70 to 0.90 between predicted and estimated difficulty, that is, 49–80% of item-difficulty variance explained. *(Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
      - Automatic min-max generators achieved R = 0.89 to 0.96 across sets of 120–320 items with reported item loss of ≤1% even under the Rasch model. *(Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
      - One transformer-generated item set had radicals explaining only R² = 0.29 of difficulty variance and R² = 0.10 of discrimination variance. *(Runge et al., 2024, as reported in Sommer & Arendasy, 2025.)* `T2`
      - Another transformer-generated set retained only 454 of 789 items (57.5%) after expert-panel review, and the surviving items' classical difficulty and discrimination statistics "varied considerably." *(Attali et al., 2022, as reported in Sommer & Arendasy, 2025.)* `T2`
      - Deployed generators have implemented only *k* = 6 to roughly *k* = 60 distinct item models, which caps the number of psychometrically distinct items obtainable regardless of how many instances are minted. *(Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
      - If a single item model leaks, every item ever generated from it is compromised and must be withdrawn, making item-model generation *more* exposed to organised theft than element-based generation where theft proceeds item by item. *(Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
      - Administering two isomorphs of one model within a session induces local dependence and biases the person-parameter estimate, so generation requires exposure control at the model level in addition to the item level. *(Glas & van der Linden, 2003, DOI 10.1177/0146621603027004001; Sommer & Arendasy, 2025.)* `T2`
      - Automatic generation beat manual item writing on cost only above 173–247 items within a single fine-grained content area. *(Kosh, Simpson, Bickel, Kellogg & Sanford-Moore, 2019, DOI 10.1111/emip.12237.)* `T2`
      - Item creation and review for one selected-response item on a large national assessment cost $1,000–$2,500, with an all-item-types weighted average near $3,700. *(National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2022, *A Pragmatic Future for NAEP*, ch. 4.)*
      - Generation-based rotating item pools have been argued to reduce exposure and enhance security, but the reviewers state that more studies calibrating generated items are needed before isomorph parameter equivalence can be assumed. *(Sommer & Arendasy, 2025, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence13080102.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The security promise of generation is that there is no fixed bank to steal, and the bill is paid in calibration. Predicted parameters are only safe to substitute for calibrated ones above a demanding explained-variance threshold that transformer-generated sets have not met, and deployed generators implement only a few dozen distinct item models regardless of how many instances they mint. A leaked item model compromises its entire progeny at once, which is the opposite of the item-by-item leakage generation is meant to replace.
    - **Link to source:** [Automatic- and transformer-based automatic item generation: a critical review](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence13080102), [Calibrating item families and family expected response functions](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986028004295), [Computerized adaptive testing with item cloning](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621603027004001), [A cost–benefit analysis of automatic item generation](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12237), [A Pragmatic Future for NAEP, ch. 4 "Item Development"](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26427/chapter/6)

- **Subcategory 3.6: Whether generated figural items behave as parallel forms**
  - **Source:** Zorowitz, Chierchia, Blakemore & Daw (2024); Chierchia et al. (2019); Blum & Holling (2018); Freund, Hofer & Holling (2008); Matzen et al. (2010); Harris, McMillan, Listyg, Matzen & Carter (2020); Arendasy & Sommer (2013); Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018); Freund & Holling (2011); Loesche et al. (2015) as reported in Schneider et al. (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a matrix-reasoning bank whose clones differ only in shape set, clone-level residual variance accounted for 35.1% of total difficulty variance across clones, estimated at 0.620 (95% HDI 0.538–0.701). *(Zorowitz, Chierchia, Blakemore & Daw, 2024, *Behavior Research Methods* 56:1104–1122, DOI 10.3758/s13428-023-02067-8.)* `T2`
      - That residual corresponded to a mean absolute difficulty difference of 0.705 logits between clones of the same item, leading the authors to conclude clones "cannot be assumed to be exchangeable." *(Zorowitz et al., 2024, DOI 10.3758/s13428-023-02067-8.)* `T2`
      - In the same bank, distractor type alone produced a 19.9% accuracy reduction on otherwise identical items. *(Zorowitz et al., 2024, versus Chierchia et al., 2019.)* `T2` `[non-replication: this result contradicts an earlier study of the same bank]`
      - Clone-level variance was credible for difficulty but not for discrimination, so non-equivalence in that bank was parameter-specific rather than global. *(Zorowitz et al., 2024, DOI 10.3758/s13428-023-02067-8.)* `T2`
      - For 23 automatically generated figural analogies administered to 307 people, Rasch and rule-based difficulty estimates correlated r = 0.74 (r² = 0.55), and the rule-based model fitted significantly worse than the descriptive model. *(Blum & Holling, 2018, *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:1286, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01286.)* `T2`
      - For 25 computer-generated figural matrix items administered to 169 people, Rasch and rule-based difficulty estimates correlated r = 0.71. *(Freund, Hofer & Holling, 2008, *APM* 32(3):195–210, DOI 10.1177/0146621607306972.)* `T2`
      - Raven-like matrix generation software can produce effectively unlimited items, but many items used in its own norming study required manual alteration to fix distractors. *(Matzen et al., 2010, *Behavior Research Methods* 42(2):525–541, DOI 10.3758/brm.42.2.525.)* `T2`
      - A later psychometric review of that generator noted the time to generate and curate new items may itself hinder adoption, and published fixed pregenerated item sets with IRT parameters instead. *(Harris, McMillan, Listyg, Matzen & Carter, 2020, DOI 10.25035/pad.2020.03.006.)* `T2`
      - With 358 respondents, retest gains on generated *isomorphic* alternate forms were as pronounced as on identical forms, while psychometrically matched forms — same parameters, different radicals — produced smaller gains. *(Arendasy & Sommer, 2013, *Intelligence* 41(3):181–192, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2013.02.004.)* `T2`
      - Alternate forms whose items differ only in non-salient surface features produce retest gains almost identical to identical forms. *(Arendasy & Sommer, 2013; Matton et al., 2011; Morley et al., 2004, as summarised in Sommer & Arendasy, 2025.)* `T2`
      - Meta-analytic retest gains were SMCR = 0.37 for identical material and 0.23 for alternate forms across 174 samples and 153,185 participants, with no further gain after the third administration. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T2`
      - With 189 people taking two tests of figural matrices generated to a strict construction rationale, item difficulty parameters were not invariant across time, and identical versus parallel generated forms differed little at the individual level. *(Freund & Holling, 2011, *Intelligence* 39(4):233–243, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2011.02.009.)* `T2`
      - A 6:41-minute video teaching five figural-matrix rules raised Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices scores at d = 0.52, 0.56 and 0.81 in three experiments, but at d = 0.14 and non-significantly in a fourth. *(Loesche et al., 2015, as reported in Schneider, Becker, Krieger, Spinath & Sparfeldt, 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2020.101473.)* `[one of four experiments failed; reported secondhand]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The exchangeability assumption on which the whole generation-as-security argument rests is contradicted by the strongest figural evidence available: clones differing only in shape set carried 35.1% of difficulty variance at the clone level and a mean 0.705-logit spread, and rule-based difficulty prediction lands around r = 0.71–0.74, well below the threshold at which predicted parameters are held harmless. Generated isomorphs also fail to blunt retest gains any better than reusing the identical item, so the devices that defeat verbatim item theft do not defeat familiarity. "Effectively unlimited items" and "usable items" are not the same quantity — both the original norming work and a later review report substantial manual distractor curation.
    - **Link to source:** [IRT analysis of the matrix reasoning item bank](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-023-02067-8), [Automatic generation of figural analogies](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01286), [Psychometric properties of computer-generated figural matrix items](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621607306972), [Recreating Raven's: systematically generating matrix problems](https://doi.org/10.3758/brm.42.2.525), [Measuring intelligence with the Sandia matrices](https://doi.org/10.25035/pad.2020.03.006), [Retest effects across methods of constructing alternate forms](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2013.02.004), [Modeling retest and training effects with generated figural matrices](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2011.02.009), [Retest effects in cognitive ability tests: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003), [Teaching figural matrix rules in a short video](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2020.101473)

### Category 4: Fairness, privacy, and the law when the examinee is a minor

*General idea: Automated proctoring imposes costs that are measurable, unevenly distributed, and legally live. The detection machinery has documented differential error by skin tone, sex, and age — including elevated error on children specifically — and it flags disability-related movement, gaze, and assistive-technology use as suspicion. The environmental preconditions it requires (a private quiet room, reliable bandwidth, camera-capable hardware, tolerance of a stranger's view into the home) are themselves distributed by income. Courts and data-protection regulators have now actually decided several of these questions and have not decided them the same way. Children's data regimes add a further layer: one jurisdiction prohibits inferring emotions in education outright, and children's-privacy rules require parental consent from the very adult who, in a home administration, is also the most plausible source of improper assistance. That last point is where the literature is thinnest, and the thinness is itself a finding.*

- **Subcategory 4.1: Differential error — three different measurements that are routinely conflated**
  - **Source:** Grother, Ngan & Hanaoka (NIST, 2019); Buolamwini & Gebru (2018); Yoder-Himes et al. (2022); Srinivas, Ricanek, Michalski, Bolme & King (2019); a student researcher's reverse-engineering analysis as reported by Motherboard and The Verge (2021); the analysed vendor's public response
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across the 189 algorithms NIST evaluated, false positive rates varied across demographic groups by factors of 10 to beyond 100, while false negative differentials usually varied by factors below 3. *(Grother, Ngan & Hanaoka, 2019, NIST Interagency Report 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST found false positives 2 to 5 times higher in women than men, with the multiple varying by algorithm, country of origin, and age. *(NIST IR 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST found elevated false positives in both the elderly and in children, largest in the oldest adults and youngest children and smallest in middle-aged adults, consistently across country-of-birth, datasets, and algorithms. *(NIST IR 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST states that children are "disadvantaged in many automated border control systems either by being excluded by policy, or by encountering higher false negatives," even without significant time lapse between two photographs. *(NIST IR 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST explicitly declined to relate its measured recognition errors to skin tone or any other phenotype, noting that estimating skin tone would itself require an algorithm that may exhibit demographic differentials. *(NIST IR 8280, §"What we did not do," DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2` `[scope limit: NIST measured by sex, age, race and country of birth — not by skin tone]`
      - NIST attributes elevated false negatives partly to image capture, giving "inadequate lighting or under-exposure of dark-skinned individuals" as an example of a photography condition that induces a demographic effect. *(NIST, Face Recognition Technology Evaluation demographics page, updated 2025-03-05.)* `T2`
      - Testing three commercial gender-classification systems on a Fitzpatrick-balanced dataset, darker-skinned females were the most misclassified group at error rates up to 34.7%, against a maximum error rate of 0.8% for lighter-skinned males. *(Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018, PMLR 81:77–91.)* `T2`
      - In that study all three classifiers performed better on lighter than darker faces by an 11.8%–19.2% error-rate difference, and worst on darker female faces at 20.8%–34.7% error. *(Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018, PMLR 81:77–91.)* `T2` `[scope limit: this measured gender classification, not face verification or face detection]`
      - Examining automated proctoring output for approximately 357 students at one institution, students with darker skin tones averaged 6.07 flags per assessment versus 1.91 for medium and 1.19 for lighter skin tones. *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, *Frontiers in Education* 7:881449, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - In the same dataset the software reported average facial detection of 78%, 87%, and 92% of assessment time for students with darker, medium, and lighter skin tones respectively (p < 0.001). *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - Students with darker skin tones were 2.5 and 5.6 times more likely to receive a "low facial detection" flag than medium- and lighter-skinned students respectively. *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - Black students were flagged for a mean of 2.80 minutes per assessment versus 0.85 minutes for White students, with the overall racial-group difference significant at p = 0.0037. *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - The authors tested and rejected connection quality as the explanation, finding that video frame-rate-reduction flags did not differ significantly by skin tone or race and internet-interruption flags did not differ by race. *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - Across eight face recognition systems — five commercial, two government, one open source — every algorithm tested showed a negative bias, meaning considerably degraded performance, for children compared with adults. *(Srinivas, Ricanek, Michalski, Bolme & King, 2019, IEEE/CVF CVPR Workshops, pp. 2269–2277, DOI 10.1109/CVPRW.2019.00280.)* `T2`
      - A student researcher reverse-engineered a widely deployed proctoring tool's browser extension, found its face-detection files identical to published open-source models, and measured those models failing to detect faces in images labelled as Black 57% of the time across nearly 11,000 images from a public face dataset. *(Analysis reported in Feathers, Motherboard/VICE, 8 April 2021; the outlet states a second security researcher validated and recreated the analysis.)* `[independent but not peer reviewed; outlets report the researcher's first name inconsistently]`
      - In the same testing the highest detection hit rate for any ethnicity was under 75%. *(As reported in The Verge, 8 April 2021.)* `[journalism reporting an independent analysis]`
      - The vendor publicly confirms it uses HAAR-based features and Canny edge detection from a widely used open-source computer-vision library to track the presence of a face, and responded that the analysis concerned face *detection* rather than identity recognition. *(Proctorio, "Setting the Record Straight: OpenCV," company blog.)* `[company statement]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Three different things have been measured here and must not be merged. NIST measured face *recognition* error by sex, age, race and country of birth, found false-positive differentials of 10–100× plus elevated error at both age extremes including children, and explicitly refused to attribute any of it to skin tone; the Gender Shades audit measured *gender classification* by Fitzpatrick skin type and found a 0.8%-to-34.7% spread; and one peer-reviewed audit measured an actual proctoring product's *face-detection* output and found darker-skinned students flagged roughly five times as often, with connection quality tested and ruled out. The widely quoted 57% figure is independent reverse-engineering corroborated by a second researcher and a journalist, not a peer-reviewed evaluation.
    - **Link to source:** [NIST IR 8280, demographic effects in face recognition](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.IR.8280), [Gender Shades](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/buolamwini18a.html), [Audit of proctoring flag rates by skin tone](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2022.881449), [Face recognition performance on children](https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPRW.2019.00280), [Motherboard report of the reverse-engineering analysis](https://www.vice.com/en/article/proctorio-is-using-racist-algorithms-to-detect-faces/), [Vendor response](https://proctorio.com/about/blog/setting-record-straight-opencv)

- **Subcategory 4.2: Disability and the flagging pathway**
  - **Source:** Brown / Center for Democracy & Technology (2020, 2022); National Disabled Law Students Association (2020); a September 2020 complaint reported in the legal press
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A disability-rights policy analysis argues that automated proctoring flags disability-linked behaviour as suspicious — motor tics, pacing, reading aloud, screen reader and dictation use, longer bathroom breaks — and can out a student as disabled to anyone with access to the recording. *(Brown, "How Automated Test Proctoring Software Discriminates Against Disabled Students," Center for Democracy & Technology, 16 November 2020; expanded in CDT, *Ableism and Disability Discrimination in New Surveillance Technologies*, May 2022.)* `[advocacy analysis, not empirical measurement]`
      - In a self-selected survey of 281 respondents, of whom 260 were registered for a remotely proctored 2020 bar examination and 113 identified as disabled, ambient noise was the most cited concern (114 respondents), followed by internet stability (68) and motion restrictions (62). *(National Disabled Law Students Association, *Report on Concerns Regarding Online Administration of Bar Exams*, August 2020.)* `[convenience sample drawn from the organisation's own networks; measures anticipated concerns, not observed flagging outcomes]`
      - Respondents in that survey specifically anticipated being flagged for fidgeting, stretching, stimming, gaze aversion associated with learning disabilities, and inability to hold gaze on-screen for an entire section. *(NDLSA, August 2020.)* `[reported concerns, not adjudicated findings]`
      - Three law graduates with disabilities filed suit in September 2020 against a state bar and the national bar examiners, alleging that being required to test in person rather than receive remote accommodations violated state and federal disability law, and challenging a requirement to remain in front of a webcam for entire test sections. *(Complaint reported in The Recorder / Law.com, 15 September 2020.)* `[alleged, not adjudicated; the disposition of this case was not verified]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The disability record is asymmetric in a way that limits how much weight it can carry. The mechanism is well-articulated and the anticipated harms are documented in a large self-selected survey, but no located study measures flag rates against disability status the way one study measured them against skin tone. What has been alleged in litigation should not be read as what has been decided.
    - **Link to source:** [How automated test proctoring software discriminates against disabled students](https://cdt.org/insights/how-automated-test-proctoring-software-discriminates-against-disabled-students/), [NDLSA report on online bar exam concerns](https://ndlsa.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/NDLSA_Online-Exam-Concerns-Report1.pdf)

- **Subcategory 4.3: What courts and regulators have actually decided**
  - **Source:** *Ogletree v. Cleveland State University* (N.D.     - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A US federal district court held that a state university's practice of conducting warrantless room scans of students' homes before remote tests is unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, concluding the student's privacy interest in his home outweighed the university's interest in scanning it. *(Ogletree v.       - That court reasoned in part that room scans are not necessary to preserve test integrity because there are numerous ways students could cheat regardless of whether a scan is performed. *(Ogletree, Amended Opinion and Order, 20 December 2022.)* `T2`

      - The record in that case included the plaintiff's statement that he had confidential settlement documents in the room he was required to scan. *(Ogletree, Amended Opinion and Order, 20 December 2022.)* `T2`
      - Italy's data protection authority found that a university using proctoring software for written exams processed students' facial biometric data with no lawful basis under GDPR Article 9, and imposed a €200,000 fine plus a processing limitation. *(Garante, Provvedimento n. 317 of 16 September 2021, docweb 9703988.)* `T2`
      - That authority held the students' consent could not be relied on, both because the processing served the issuing of legally recognised diplomas and because consent could not be considered freely given given the imbalance between student and controller. *(Garante, Provvedimento n. 317/2021; EDPB summary.)* `T2`

      - A putative biometric-privacy class action against another proctoring vendor was dismissed with prejudice by joint stipulation in 2022, the plaintiff stating that after extensive discovery he found no evidence the vendor collects or possesses information sufficient to identify a test-taker by name, location, or contact information. *(Stipulation of dismissal, Hines v.     - **DOK 2 — Summary:** What has actually been decided does not point one way. - **Subcategory 4.4: The statutory regimes aimed specifically at children**
  - **Source:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act); Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR); FTC COPPA Rule amendments, 90 Fed. Reg. 16918 (2025); ICO Age Appropriate Design Code
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - EU law prohibits placing on the market, putting into service, or using AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons. *(Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 5(1)(f).)* `T2`
      - The same regulation separately classifies as high-risk "AI systems intended to be used for monitoring and detecting prohibited behaviour of students during tests in the context of or within educational and vocational training institutions at all levels." *(Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Annex III, point 3(d).)* `T2`
      - Its recitals state that education AI systems, including those for monitoring and detecting prohibited student behaviour during tests, may be particularly intrusive and may perpetuate historical patterns of discrimination against women, certain age groups, persons with disabilities, and persons of certain racial or ethnic origins. *(Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Recital 56.)* `T2`
      - That regulation applies from 2 August 2026, but the chapters containing the Article 5 prohibitions applied from 2 February 2025. *(Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 113.)* `T2`
      - GDPR defines biometric data as data resulting from specific technical processing of physical or behavioural characteristics "which allow or confirm the unique identification of that natural person, such as facial images," and prohibits processing it "for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person" absent an exception. *(Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 4(14) and 9(1).)* `T2`

      - GDPR recites that children merit specific protection with regard to their personal data because they may be less aware of the risks, consequences, safeguards, and their own rights, particularly for profiling and personality-profile creation. *(Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Recital 38.)* `T2`

      - The FTC's 2025 COPPA Rule amendments added biometric identifiers usable for automated or semi-automated recognition of an individual to the definition of "personal information," effective 23 June 2025 with a compliance date of 22 April 2026. *(Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule, final rule amendments, 90 Fed.     - **DOK 2 — Summary:** On children specifically, the strongest instrument is prospective rather than litigated. - **Subcategory 4.5: What the examinee pays — anxiety, environment, and bandwidth**
  - **Source:** Conijn, Kleingeld, Matzat & Snijders (2022); Woldeab & Brothen (2019); Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend (2014); ATP & ITC *Guidelines for Technology-Based Assessment* (2022); Pew Research Center (2024, 2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across four survey waves covering 1,760 students in 105 courses at one faculty, multi-level analysis found proctoring had no effect on temptation to cheat, perceived exam difficulty, or performance, but students reported significantly higher test anxiety. *(Conijn, Kleingeld, Matzat & Snijders, 2022, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 38(6):1521–1534, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12651.)* `T2`

### Category 5: AI capability and adult assistance

*General idea: Proctoring technology was built to answer one question — is the right person alone in the room, and are they looking at anything they shouldn't be? Neither of the two threats that matter most to an at-home children's reasoning assessment is visible to that question. A frontier model running on a phone eighteen inches outside the webcam frame produces no browser event and no gaze pattern distinguishable from thinking, and a parent sitting beside the child produces no anomaly at all, because a supportive adult is the expected condition of at-home child testing rather than a violation of it. The published evidence complicates the alarm in a specific way: on abstract grid puzzles delivered as text, frontier models now exceed average human performance by a wide margin, while on image-based matrix and figural-analogy problems — the actual format of nonverbal cognitive tests — the same models sit at or barely above chance, with roughly fifty times as many failures attributed to perception as to reasoning. Meanwhile the strongest measured score inflation in this literature comes not from cheating at all but from coaching, which is legal, widespread, and in one selection-test study substantial enough to shift means while leaving rank order intact. All model-capability figures below are dated because they age quickly.*

- **Subcategory 5.1: Frontier model performance on abstract reasoning delivered as text**
  - **Source:** ARC Prize verified results and technical report (2025–2026); the published ARC-AGI task collections and task-format guide; independent commentary on the human baseline
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - ARC Prize's independently verified testing lists a frontier model at maximum reasoning effort scoring 92.5% on ARC-AGI-2 and 96.5% on ARC-AGI-1, published 2026-07-09. *(ARC Prize, verified results page.)*

- **Subcategory 5.2: The same models on image-delivered figural items, and where the failures sit**
  - **Source:** Khezresmaeilzadeh et al. (2026); Haznitrama, Ardi & Oh (2026); Cao et al. (2024/2025); Du, Zou & Cheng (2026); Yan et al. (2025); a two-stage description-first pipeline study (ACL 2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On an abstract puzzle set delivered as images — sequence completion, matrix prediction, odd-one-out, figure rotation, 3D visualization — models averaged 27.52% against a 25% random-guess baseline and a 90.36% human baseline, measured February 2026. *(Khezresmaeilzadeh et al., 2026, arXiv:2602.05382.)*

      - On a matrix-reasoning benchmark modelled on Raven's and the WISC, one model reached 33.96% and a later one 52.29% against an 81% adult human baseline. *(Cao et al., 2024/2025, arXiv:2406.10424.)*

The near-chance figures come from independent academic benchmarks while the headline text figure comes from the benchmark organisation's own verified testing, and one benchmark locates the failure in decomposition rather than perception.

  - **Source:** Bloemers, Oud & van Dam (2016); Guo & Drasgow (2010); Aguado et al. (2018); Nye et al. (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**

- **Subcategory 5.4: Whether model assistance can be detected from response data**
  - **Source:** Liang et al. (2023); Mehta et al. (2025/2026); Bungaro et al. (2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Seven widely used GPT detectors falsely flagged 61.3% of 91 human-written TOEFL essays as AI-generated while classifying US eighth-grade essays near-perfectly. *(Liang et al., 2023, *Patterns*, DOI 10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779.)* `T2`

- **Subcategory 5.6: Coaching as the legal, measured inflation channel**
  - **Source:** Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007); Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018); an operation-specific latent-growth study (2025); te Nijenhuis, van Vianen & van der Flier (2007); te Nijenhuis, Voskuijl & Schijve (2001); Bunting & Mooney (2001); New York City Independent Budget Office (2022); a commercial preparation provider's self-report
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A meta-analysis of 107 samples and 134,436 participants found an adjusted overall retest effect of d = .26 on cognitive ability tests, larger when practice was accompanied by coaching and when identical forms were reused. *(Hausknecht et al., 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373.)* `T2`
      - A later meta-analysis of 174 samples from 122 studies (N = 153,185) confirmed significant retest effects and found no further score gains after the third administration. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence*, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T2`
      - A 2025 latent-growth study (N = 203) reported linear improvement of about 10.45 IQ points per assessment across three sessions, roughly two-thirds of a standard deviation. *("Investigating retest effects in cognitive ability tests: an operation-specific approach," *Intelligence*, 2025.)* `T2`
      - A meta-analysis of 64 test-retest studies using IQ batteries (N = 26,990) reported a correlation of −1.00 between subtest g-loadings and score gains, interpreted as score gains containing no g. *(te Nijenhuis, van Vianen & van der Flier, 2007, *Intelligence*, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2006.07.006.)* `T2` `[an extreme value obtained via the method of correlated vectors; treat as contested]`

      - In 552 children aged 10–11 taking seven parallel forms of an academic selection test, familiarisation and practice produced no significant change in means, three hours of coaching produced a statistically significant shift in means while individuals maintained their rank order, and sustained coaching over nine months produced substantial gains. *(Bunting & Mooney, 2001, *Educational Psychology*, DOI 10.1080/01443410120065450.)* `T2`
      - A municipal budget office found that under test-based gifted admissions fewer than one quarter of test-takers were eligible, and Black and Hispanic students were less than half as likely as White students to score high enough for both citywide and district programs. *(New York City Independent Budget Office, 2022.)*

For a ranked selection decision the distinction that matters is the one an eleven-plus study isolated: three hours of coaching moved the mean while leaving rank order intact.

- **Subcategory 5.7: The adult in the room, and whether remote administration is equivalent at all**
  - **Source:** Yantz et al. (2009); Kehrer et al. (2000); Pearson telepractice guidance; Hamner et al. (2021); Manning et al. (2024); Ruffini et al. (2021); Harder et al. (2023); Moreau et al. (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In 53 children aged 6–8 given a nonverbal intelligence test with and without a parent observing, parental observation status significantly interacted with observation order to affect performance, mainly through a steeper improvement slope when the parent observed first. *(Yantz et al., 2009, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist*, DOI 10.1080/13854040801894722.)* `T2`
      - With a significant-other observer present, 30 test-takers showed performance deficits on attention, speed of information processing, and verbal fluency, and none on cognitive flexibility or motor speed. *(Kehrer et al., 2000, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist*, DOI 10.1076/1385-4046(200002)14:1;1-8;ft067.)* `T2`
      - A test publisher's remote-administration guidance instructs that a non-professional in-home facilitator must remain neutral, not provide additional encouragement, and not answer on behalf of the child, and warns that children may look to the facilitator for guidance in ways that will influence performance. *(Pearson, telepractice guidance for the NEPSY-II.)* `[publisher guidance, not an empirical finding]`
      - In 893 clinically referred children and adolescents aged 4–17, only two of seven remotely versus in-person administered subtests differed significantly — Visual Puzzles (d = .33) and Math Concepts (d = .18) — with Matrix Reasoning showing no significant difference. *(Hamner et al., 2021, *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society*, DOI 10.1017/S1355617721001053.)* `T2`
      - In 476 youths aged 6–17 assessed at home by telehealth versus in person, nine of twelve neuropsychological measures showed no differences and three verbal tests were about one scaled score point lower in person. *(Manning et al., 2024, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist*, DOI 10.1080/13854046.2024.2403734.)* `T2`
      - A systematic review of 23 studies of cognitive tele-assessment in children reported similarity in performance scores against in-person assessment while stressing that methodological precautions are required for technical and procedural challenges. *(Ruffini et al., 2021, *Child Neuropsychology*, DOI 10.1080/09297049.2021.2005011.)* `T2`
      - A separate systematic review of pediatric tele-neuropsychology concluded the approach is feasible and acceptable but that performance validity was not tested in the included studies, samples were small and mostly white, and there is a critical need for tests specifically normed for remote administration. *(Harder et al., 2023, PMC10231293.)* `T2`
      - In 19 youths with neurodevelopmental disorders given a cognitive battery in person versus in-home remotely, results were generally equivalent except that Fluid Reasoning and Auditory Processing scores differed significantly across modalities. *(Moreau et al., 2023, *Canadian Journal of School Psychology*, DOI 10.1177/08295735231199858.)* `T2` `[a very small sample and a single study]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Adult presence during children's testing has a measurable but modality-specific and directionally inconsistent effect, and publishers already treat an in-home non-professional facilitator as a validity risk requiring explicit instruction not to help. Remote administration of children's cognitive tests looks broadly equivalent to in-person in clinical samples, with two caveats bearing directly on nonverbal reasoning: the one study reporting a fluid-reasoning discrepancy had N = 19, and every equivalence study reviewed was clinician-supervised and low-stakes rather than unsupervised and high-stakes. One systematic review adds that performance validity was never tested in any included study.
