# BrainLift: Placement and Dosing — What a Learner Profile Is Fit to Decide About Where a Student Starts and How Their Time Is Split

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose
This BrainLift asks what a measured profile of a student — reasoning ability, achievement level, and rate of learning — is actually **fit to decide**. It holds two decisions apart: where a student should start in a subject, and how a fixed budget of learning time should be divided between subjects and scheduled inside them. Four claims sit underneath those decisions and rest on four separate evidence bases: that a starting level is estimable enough to be worth estimating, that ability or rate carries something achievement does not, that a profile licenses giving one student a different share of time than another, and that the schedule itself should differ by student. A profile can be fit to decide one and unfit to decide the next. Failing one does not weaken the others; it just removes the warrant for that one decision.

### In Scope
- Aptitude defined as time-to-learn, and what mastery learning delivered against that definition.
- The record on matching instruction to a measured cognitive profile, including the one moderator that replicated.
- Where the variance in learning actually sits: starting knowledge, rate, or neither.
- What a placement estimate delivers, and how fast a system's own observations overtake it.
- Moving time between subjects under a fixed budget: what gets displaced, who benefits, and how fast the gains decay.
- Scheduling the time once it is allocated, and whether the right schedule differs by student.
- Provenance: what is peer-reviewed, what is a working paper or publisher report, which numbers are predicted rather than observed, and what was searched for and not found.

### Out of Scope
- Whether a learning rate is estimable and stable at all (`learning-rate-scoring`), whether measured reasoning moves (`reasoning-growth`), instrument validity at the tail (`gifted-assessment-quality`), routing and session design (`test-structure`), validating a new test against an old one (`test-evaluation`), which signals earn a place in a composite (`metric-evidence`), causal identification of program effect (`gt-school-counterfactual`), and whether the work is the student's own (`proctoring-integrity`).
- Curriculum design. The units of instruction and their prerequisites are treated as given; the question here is where a student enters.
- Comparing a named system or vendor, and mapping any of this onto a build, configuration, or policy. Both belong in a separate design document.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

**SPOV 1: Stop building profiles that describe students. A profile earns its place only by locating a student against content, skill by skill. Any rule that changes instruction based on a student trait should be treated as invalid until it shows a crossover interaction, and none has.**

- **Elaboration:** The burden of proof belongs on the rule, and that is the point. Common practice treats a differentiation rule as reasonable until someone disproves it. The record says to assume it fails until it passes. Four separate literatures asked whether a student trait moderates instruction and four gave the same answer. The research program built to find these interactions concluded that some replicate and none are usable in practice, and Cronbach explained why: any interaction can be moderated by an untested higher-order one, so we "enter a hall of mirrors that extends to infinity" (2.1). The strictest test ever set, deliberately weakened below the version people usually claim, found one qualifying study in an enormous literature (2.2). The special-education version ran nationally and produced a positive predictive value near .22, with its own evaluators warning it "may result in many children receiving instruction that is not optimally matched to their specific needs"; the replacement was skill-by-treatment at g = 0.43 and 0.48 against cognition-by-treatment at 0.17 (2.3). The one moderator that did replicate is prior knowledge in the domain, never ability. Bjork's own words are that "the level of difficulty that is optimal will vary with the degree of a learner's prior learning," and searching four of his papers and both cognitive-load reviews turned up no version conditioned on general ability (2.4). So prior knowledge in the specific subject sets dosing, guidance, and difficulty, and where anyone has actually tested a per-student schedule the two available studies contradict each other on weak learners with neither interaction reaching significance (6.2). Reasoning and rate scores get no vote on how a subject is taught; the cleanest single test of that is a six-year growth study where intelligence predicted the starting point at 4.72 and the slope at 0.24, p = .88 (3.4). They can still help locate where the student sits, since rate varies about seven times more across skills than across students (3.1, Insight 1). Aptitude-treatment optimists counter that the old studies were underpowered and that modern systems can test interactions at a scale Cronbach never had.

**SPOV 2: The finding everyone cites to retire learning-rate measurement is weakest exactly where it gets applied, and the practice everyone defends is also wrong. A rate is only measurable per subject, and only above an observation threshold nobody has established.**

- **Elaboration:** Both camps overread their own evidence. Against the retirement camp: the near-constant-rate result is not robust to which observations enter the model. Capping practice at the first ten opportunities inflates student-level rate variance by a median of 118%, and by 233% in the short-practice group, while the starting-knowledge estimate moves by −1.5% (3.2). The mechanisms named as candidates are mastery-based exit, adaptive routing, and disengagement, which describes how a self-paced mastery system normally runs, and the median practice sequence in that corpus is 6.5 opportunities. A co-author of the original paper also reports that individualizing a model's learning-speed parameter beat individualizing its starting parameter by roughly ten to one on the largest dataset available (4.4). Against the measurement camp: a rate is not a general property of a person. Fitted slopes in the same people did not correlate between verbal and spatial material, and those authors concluded there is no "material-independent learning ability that influences rate of learning" (3.3). The original paper's own domain split puts student rate spread at 0.045 in language against 0.008 in science, so how much signal even exists depends on the subject (3.1). Slopes also retest at a median of .34 against .53 for starting level (3.3). Estimate the rate per subject, from enough observations to leave the truncation regime (Insights 2–3). No source states that number, so a per-subject rate from a short block is inflated against an unknown threshold and has to be labelled that way. Defenders of the original result counter that it replicated to the third decimal on 821,890 fresh observations, and that a sensitivity analysis is not a refutation.

**SPOV 3: Acceleration is not a treatment for a tail, and a placement test should not be graded on misclassification. Grade it on two things only: whether it can see past a grade-level ceiling, and whether it puts more students into harder material.**

- **Elaboration:** The accuracy framing survives because it is easy to measure. The evidence says it is the wrong mechanism. In the one randomized trial that moved students both ways on a better placement rule, students moved up gained about 8 points on college-level completion and students moved down lost about 5 to 8. Those are mirror images. If the rule were genuinely more accurate, moving a student down to their supposedly correct level should have helped them. The evaluators concluded that "greater access to college-level courses rather than greater placement accuracy may be the mechanism" (4.2). Accuracy also loses on its own terms. Purpose-built placement tests explained about 1% of the variation in course outcomes against 11% for an ordinary high school record, a rich algorithm recovered only about 15% of severe error, and the errors run three-to-one and six-to-one toward holding back students who would have passed (4.1). The window any up-front estimate operates in is two to three observations wide, because a population prior predicts at chance on a student's first attempt at a skill and hits its permanent plateau by the third, and a prior built from a single in-system answer beat standard knowledge tracing in 30 of 42 topics (4.3, 4.4). An up-front measurement can do one thing in-system observation cannot: see above the ceiling. Half the top slice of a talent-search cohort already knew more algebra before studying it than half their classmates knew after a full year, and no grade-level test finds that at any precision (4.5). This also applies to every student: in the randomized tracking experiment every quantile gained, 0.19 SD at the top against 0.16 at the bottom and statistically indistinguishable, and a century-scale review reports grouping effects that "did not vary for high-, medium-, and low-ability students" (4.6, Insight 6). Placement traditionalists counter that misclassification is the only property you can actually audit, and that "widens access" is a policy preference dressed up as a measurement standard.

**SPOV 4: Added instructional time is the weakest lever available, so it should be the last one pulled. Fix the difficulty band and fix the schedule before moving a single minute between subjects.**

- **Elaboration:** This inverts the instinct every remediation policy runs on, that a student who is behind should get more of the subject. Measured carefully, that lever is worth about 0.17 SD while it is being applied, loses half of it within a year of the extra time stopping and two thirds within two, and produces no detectable effect on whether the student ever completes the later courses in that subject (5.2). It also degrades as you lean on it, turning negative past roughly 500 weekly minutes, and it returns 0.025 SD in a well-run classroom against 0.011 in a poorly run one, so the same reallocation is a different intervention depending on what receives it (5.2). It is never free. The hour came out of art and music, and students the policy never targeted saw grades fall by .10 to .18 points and failure rates rise four points (5.1). It is also badly aimed: the students who gained most from extra math time were the weak readers, put there by verbally delivered instruction rather than by anything about mathematics (5.1, Insight 5). The two cheaper levers have better evidence. The only causal experiment on where a marginal minute goes says concentrate on material of intermediate difficulty (5.3), and the scheduling literature delivers roughly eleven points of recall from spacing and d = 0.83 from interleaving at nominally no extra minutes — though the interleaving authors note that time on task was never measured, so that figure is an upper bound (6.1, 6.3). Both operate at a grain the evidence actually covers, while nothing has ever tested allocation between subjects (5.3, Insight 8). So the order is difficulty band first, schedule second, minutes last, and when minutes do move they move on level and on that subject's own growth profile rather than on one formula applied across four subjects (3.5, Insight 4). Urgency advocates counter that a student two grade levels behind cannot wait for scheduling optimizations, and that volume is the only lever that moves fast enough to matter.

## Experts

### Kenneth Koedinger (with Carvalho, Liu, McLaughlin); Yudelson, Gordon
- **Who:** Learning-at-scale researchers fitting growth models to millions of student practice records; Koedinger directs LearnLab and co-founded Carnegie Learning.
- **Focus:** Knowledge-component models, intelligent tutoring systems, and whether the individual difference that matters is starting knowledge or speed.
- **Why Follow:** They hold that starting knowledge is the actionable difference and rate is nearly a constant. Koedinger is also a co-author on the finding that individualizing a model's starting estimate does nothing while individualizing its speed estimate helps, which cuts against his own headline.
- **Where:** [Koedinger et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2221311120) | [Yudelson, Koedinger & Gordon (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_18) | [CMU HCII profile](https://www.hcii.cmu.edu/people/ken-koedinger)

### Kathleen McDermott, Zerr; Zhang, Salthouse, Tucker-Drob
- **Who:** Memory and individual-differences researchers measuring learning speed as trials-to-criterion rather than accuracy at a fixed point.
- **Focus:** Whether learning speed is a stable, ability-linked trait, and whether it generalizes across the kind of material being learned.
- **Why Follow:** They are the counterweight to the near-constant-rate camp, and they disagree with each other. McDermott's group gets a rate measure that retests at .70 across three years and tracks IQ at .43; Zhang's group finds ability predicts starting level and not slope, and that slopes do not transfer between verbal and spatial material.
- **Where:** [Zerr et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618772540) | [Zhang et al. (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2007.01.004) | [Memory & Cognition Lab](https://memory.wustl.edu/)

### John Sweller, Kalyuga; Robert and Elizabeth Bjork
- **Who:** Cognitive load theorists and the desirable-difficulties research program.
- **Focus:** How much guidance and difficulty a learner should get, and what determines the answer.
- **Why Follow:** They disagree about how hard to make things and agree on the variable that decides it. Sweller's camp shows that techniques helping novices reverse and start harming learners as expertise grows; Bjork's camp shows that conditions depressing performance during instruction often produce durable learning. Both condition the answer on prior knowledge in the domain, and neither conditions it on general ability.
- **Where:** [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4) | [Bjork & Bjork (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2020.09.003) | [Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/)

### Lee Cronbach, Snow; Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer; Stuebing, Fletcher, Burns
- **Who:** The methodologists who ran the aptitude-treatment interaction program, the team that set the strictest test for instructional matching, and the researchers who evaluated the special-education version of it.
- **Focus:** Whether instruction can be matched to a measured cognitive profile at all, and what a matching claim has to produce to be believed.
- **Why Follow:** They supply the negative record any profile-driven design inherits whether it cites them or not, including Cronbach's structural argument that interaction findings can never be safely general.
- **Where:** [Cronbach (1975)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076829) | [Pashler et al. (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x) | [Stuebing et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/02796015.2012.12087373) | [Burns et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1037/spq0000117)

## DOK 3: Insights

**Insight 1 - Every moderator that replicated in this literature is a property of the material, or of the student's knowledge of that material. Every moderator that failed is a property of the student as a general trait.**
- The pattern holds across four bodies of evidence with different methods, populations, and outcome measures. In the aptitude-treatment program, the survivor is domain-specific prior knowledge, while ability and learning style produced one qualifying study out of an enormous pile (2.2, 2.4). In special education the same idea was tested nationally and replaced: cognitive-processing measures returned g = 0.17 for designing interventions against 0.43 and 0.48 for measures of the academic skill itself (2.3). In the practice data, rate varies about seven times more across knowledge components than across students (3.1). In the scheduling literature, material moderators replicate cleanly enough that interleaving reverses to g = −0.39 for word learning, while prior knowledge was not even coded as a moderator (6.3), and the canonical spacing sources never tested a learner moderator at all across 271 comparisons (6.1). Kalyuga saw part of this himself and said so in print, tying the expertise-reversal results back to the older aptitude-treatment work (2.4), so the convergence is visible from inside one of these traditions. The question worth asking is therefore which components a student is slow on. Proponents of learner modeling counter that four nulls on crude trait measures do not rule out a good one.

**Insight 2 - The evidence that rate barely varies between students was collected on practice that mastery systems truncate, and truncation inflates that one parameter while leaving the others alone.**
- Three facts no single source combines. Capping practice sequences at ten opportunities raises student-level rate variance by a median of 118%, and 233% in the short-practice group, while moving the starting-knowledge estimate by −1.5% (3.2). Truncation hits the one parameter the whole argument turns on. Mastery-based exit and adaptive routing are the named candidate mechanisms, and both describe how a self-paced system normally behaves; the median practice sequence in that corpus runs 6.5 opportunities. And when a model individualized both parameters on the largest dataset available, speed beat starting knowledge roughly ten to one, reported by a co-author of the original result (4.4). The finding still holds inside the practice regime it was measured in. That regime is the one a mastery platform creates. The original authors counter that they tested data limitations directly and ruled them out.

**Insight 3 - A learning rate only makes sense per subject, and only above an observation threshold nobody has published. The global rate is a category error and the short-window rate manufactures the differences it reports.**
- Separating the two objections is what makes a per-subject rate defensible. The claim that rate is a general trait fails on its own evidence: fitted slopes in the same people did not correlate between verbal and spatial material, and those authors concluded there is no "material-independent learning ability that influences rate of learning" (3.3). Koedinger's own domain split says the same thing from the other side, with student rate spread at 0.045 in language against 0.017 in math and 0.008 in science, so the amount of usable signal is about five times larger in language than in science (3.1). The same split appears in school achievement over six years, where intelligence predicted the starting point at 4.72 and the growth slope at 0.24 with p = .88 in 3,530 students (3.4). A rate crossed with a subject is a better-behaved object than a rate alone. But the second objection stands. Slopes retest at .34 against .53 for level (3.3), and truncation inflation is worst in exactly the short sequences a practical assessment would produce (3.2). Nothing states the threshold, so a per-subject rate from a short block is inflated and has to be labelled an estimate. Practitioners counter that waiting on a threshold nobody has published means never measuring rate at all.

**Insight 4 - A single time-allocation rule cannot be subject-neutral. The same "how far behind" input predicts much faster growth in reading and almost none in math, so one rule quietly becomes two different policies.**
- Expected fall-to-spring gain across the 25th, 50th and 75th starting percentiles runs 9.611, 8.156 and 6.701 in Grade 4 reading against 11.060, 10.963 and 10.866 in Grade 4 math, and Grade 8 repeats the shape (3.5). Starting behind in reading predicts substantially faster growth; starting behind in math predicts almost nothing extra. Any rule that reads "how far behind" and returns "more time" therefore carries a different implied theory in each subject, and nobody chose either one. The tradition that licensed differentiated time never supplied the conversion either: Carroll conceded that the field had "no adequate procedures for estimating how long a given unit of instruction will take to be learned by students with different aptitudes" (1.1), so any such rule invents its own arithmetic. It compounds with the decay evidence: extra time in one subject loses about two thirds of its gain within two years (5.2), so the subject where students are already expected to close ground is the worst place to spend a decaying resource. Growth-norm skeptics counter that reading measures compress at the floor and ceiling more than math ones, and that a publisher's convenience sample can manufacture a compensatory pattern on its own.

**Insight 5 - Reading gates the return on time spent in other subjects, but only up to a functional threshold. Past that point the subjects stop being independent budget lines for a different reason: the format of the instruction decides who it fails.**
- In the double-dose math policy the students who gained most had below-average *reading* skill, which the authors attribute to instruction that "focused on verbal exposition of mathematical concepts," while the students with the weakest incoming *math* skill gained least and saw their algebra grades fall (5.1). The moderator for extra math time lived in a different subject, put there by how the teaching was delivered. Below a functional reading level, reading sits upstream of everything and caps what an extra hour in any other subject can return. Above it, reading stops gating and the leftover dependence belongs to delivery format instead of to the student. The threshold itself is an open assumption: a working estimate of roughly a fourth-grade reading level is mine, no located source establishes it, and it has to be tested rather than assumed. Content designers counter that if format is the moderator, the fix is to rewrite the instruction rather than to move the time.

**Insight 6 - The benefit of acceleration never concentrates in a subgroup anyone can identify in advance, so selecting who gets accelerated has no evidential basis and the only decision left is where each student enters.**
- Three findings point the same way and no source connects them. When an algorithm moved students in both directions, the harm from moving one down matched the gain from moving one up, which is why the evaluators named access over accuracy (4.2). In the randomized tracking experiment every quantile gained, 0.19 SD at the top against 0.16 at the bottom and statistically indistinguishable (4.6). And the century-scale review reports grouping effects that "did not vary for high-, medium-, and low-ability students" (4.6). If a benefit is real and keeps refusing to concentrate, a screen that decides eligibility is sorting on something the outcome does not respect. The grain does vary: per-subject and within-class grouping return 0.19 to 0.37 while sorting students by general ability returns 0.04 to 0.06 (4.6), the same content-over-trait axis as Insight 1. Selection advocates counter that fixed capacity forces a choice, and that "everyone benefits" is not a plan when seats are scarce.

**Insight 7 - Two different inflations get conflated in this field: choosing your own outcome measure, and choosing your own sample. They need separate guards, because the standard defense answers only one of them.**
- On measure choice, mastery learning is 0.52 on tests the researchers built, 0.29 on standardized tests, and 0.10 for the school-based variant on standardized tests, and one study gave the same students both kinds in the same week for +.64 against +.04 (1.3). The two-sigma tutoring benchmark re-measured at 0.79 and then at 0.288 (1.4). On sample choice, the personalized-learning estimate fell from 0.27 SD to about three percentile points on a cohort with less implementation experience and fewer charter schools, and the authors state plainly that neither design separates the program from the schools that chose to adopt it (5.4). The tradition also missed its own prediction, since Bloom expected the aptitude-achievement correlation to approach zero and got .35, which he attributed to range restriction (1.2). Keeping the two apart matters because the best defense of a locally built test — that it covers exactly what was taught while a standardized test covers a year of untouched curriculum — has no purchase whatsoever on sample choice. So the two guards are separate: require an outcome measure the program did not build, and require a comparison group that did not select itself in. Program advocates counter that demanding both an outside measure and an unselected comparison sets a bar almost no education research clears.

**Insight 8 - The evidence sits one level below the decision. Nothing has tested allocation between subjects, so that split is an assumption; what has been tested is which difficulty band inside a subject, and the schedule to run it on.**
- The only causal allocation experiment found the best outcome came from concentrating on medium-difficulty material, beating both the hardest and the easiest, with measured uptake curves explaining why, and it ran on individual items inside one task with nothing extending it to subjects (5.3). The scheduling literature answers what schedule in great detail, including that the optimal gap scales with how long the material has to last, and never tests for whom, across 271 comparisons dominated by adults at retention intervals under a day (6.1). The two studies that did test the learner axis agree only that high performers gain nothing, and contradict each other on weak students, at 81 and 61 participants with neither interaction significant (6.2). So the defensible rule splits time between subjects on level plus that subject's own expected-growth profile, since the same starting percentile means different things in reading and in math (3.5, Insight 4), and keeps rate out of the split, where it is worth about one opportunity against ten (3.1). Rate earns its place in the within-subject decision instead, which keeps this consistent with SPOV 2. Practitioners counter that a week has to be divided regardless, so an absent finding does not remove the decision, it only hides who made it.

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

### Overall Summary

Aptitude was originally defined as the time a student needs to reach mastery, so a time budget derived from a measured profile follows straight from the definition. The definition never supplied a number. Its own author conceded twenty-five years later that the field had no way to estimate how long a unit takes a given student. Mastery learning's headline effects hold on tests built by the researchers running the study and fall to roughly zero on standardized ones, and the two-sigma tutoring benchmark it aimed at re-measures at 0.288 to 0.79. Matching instruction to a cognitive profile has been tried three times under three names and failed three times; the only moderator that replicated is prior knowledge in the specific domain. Starting knowledge varies about twenty percentage points across the middle half of students while rate varies about one point per practice opportunity, though the rate estimate doubles under practice truncation and rate does not transfer across materials. Placement tests misplace a quarter to a third of students, lose to ordinary school records, and buy their gains through access rather than accuracy, and a system's own data reaches full predictive power by a student's third attempt at a skill. Added time in one subject produces about 0.17 SD while it lasts, decays to a third within two years, turns negative past roughly 500 weekly minutes, and comes out of art and music while the other core subjects keep their hours. Spacing and interleaving both work; whether the right schedule differs by student is untested in the canonical sources and contradictory in the two small studies that looked.

### Category 1: Aptitude Defined as Time-to-Learn — The Definition That Makes Time a Function of Measured Ability

- **Subcategory 1.1: Aptitude was defined as the time a student needs, so a differentiated time budget follows from the definition**
  - **Source:** Carroll (1989), the author's own 25-year review of his 1963 model
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The definition, in Carroll's words: "Aptitude is the name given to the variable or variables that determine the amount of time a student needs to learn a given task, unit of instruction, or curriculum to an acceptable criterion of mastery under optimal conditions of instruction and student motivation. High aptitude is indicated when a student needs a relatively small amount of time to learn; low aptitude is indicated when a student needs much more than average time to learn."
      - Three of the model's five variables are measured in time: aptitude, **opportunity to learn** (the time a schedule allows), and **perseverance** (the time a student is willing to spend). "The time actually spent on learning, in any given situation, is the shortest of the three time variables."
      - The other two variables act on the time requirement rather than beside it. Weak instruction increases the time needed; a student's limited ability to understand instruction increases it too.
      - Twenty-five years on, Carroll's verdict on what held: "All or most of its components have been amply confirmed as influential in school learning, although it has not always been possible to say exactly how they are effective or how they can be controlled or manipulated."
      - His verdict on what did not: "the precise relations postulated among the variables of the model have not been adequately investigated," and "Educational psychology as a science still has no adequate procedures for estimating how long a given unit of instruction will take to be learned by students with different aptitudes."
      - He also distanced himself from the program built on his model: "I doubt that true equality of attainment can ever be realized."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Aptitude *was* time-to-mastery, so varying a student's time by measured aptitude restates the definition. That is a much stronger starting position than an interaction claim has to earn, and it should be granted at full strength. Then comes the author's own concession: the components were confirmed, the quantitative relations were never investigated, and nobody could convert an aptitude score into hours. The definition tells you to vary time. It cannot tell you by how much.
    - **Link to source:** [The Carroll Model: A 25-Year Retrospective and Prospective View](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X018001026)

- **Subcategory 1.2: Mastery learning made a falsifiable prediction from that definition, and it came out partway**
  - **Source:** Bloom (1968); Bloom (1984)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Bloom's opening claim in 1968: "Most students, perhaps over 90 percent, can master what teachers have to teach them, and it is the task of instruction to find the means which will enable students to master the subject under consideration."
      - He expected the spread in time-to-learn to shrink rather than just be accommodated, predicting the ratio between slower and faster learners "may be reduced from about 6 to 1 to perhaps 3 to 1."
      - His prediction: with instruction and time made appropriate to each student, "the relationship between aptitude and achievement should approach zero," against correlations of "+.70 or higher" under uniform instruction.
      - In 1984 he reported tutoring at about two standard deviations above the control class average and mastery learning at about one.
      - The prediction as it actually landed: correlations "changed from +.60 under conventional to +.35 under mastery learning and +.25 under tutoring." Bloom immediately qualified it, noting the correlations "were so low because of the restricted range of scores under these learning conditions."
      - The two-sigma figure rests on two doctoral dissertations, and the exposure was short: "the instructional treatment was limited to 11 periods of instruction over a 3-week block of time."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The tradition predicted that giving each student the time they need drives the aptitude-achievement correlation toward zero. It went from .60 to .35, and Bloom attributed even that to range restriction rather than to aptitude ceasing to matter. The famous number is also the least secure part: two unpublished dissertations, eleven class periods, and a mastery-learning arm at half the size.
    - **Link to source:** [Learning for Mastery](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED053419) | [The 2 Sigma Problem](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X013006004)

- **Subcategory 1.3: Mastery learning's effect size is 0.52 or roughly zero, depending on whose test you use**
  - **Source:** Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990); Slavin (1987)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 103 studies, "The average effect size in the 103 studies was 0.52," with a standard error of 0.033.
      - The same meta-analysis splits it by whose test was used: locally developed tests averaged 0.57 across 88 studies, standardized tests averaged 0.29 across 11, and the school-based Learning-for-Mastery variant on standardized tests averaged 0.10 across 2.
      - Across 13 studies reporting by ability level, the effect was larger for less able students (0.61) than more able ones (0.40), but "The difference, however, is not significant (t = 1.23, p > .10)."
      - Under stricter rules — real schools, at least four weeks, evidence of equated groups — Slavin found "the effects of mastery learning on standardized achievement measures are extremely small, at best. The median effect size across all seven studies is essentially zero (ES = +.04)."
      - On researcher-built measures in the same review, the median across eight studies was +.255.
      - One study shows the split cleanly on its own: Anderson, Scott & Hutlock (1976) produced +.64 on the researcher's math posttest and +.04 on the commercial California Achievement Test given to the same students.
      - Studies that gave mastery learning extra time produced a median immediate gain of +.31, but "none of three retention measures found significant differences (median ES = −.03)."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The reviewers agree on the data and disagree about which measure carries the headline. Both report large effects on tests built by the people running the intervention and near-nothing on standardized ones. The extra-time result carries the sharper warning for any time-allocation rule: +.31 immediately, nothing retained.
    - **Link to source:** [Effectiveness of Mastery Learning Programs](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265) | [Mastery Learning Reconsidered](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543057002175)

- **Subcategory 1.4: The two-sigma benchmark the whole tradition aimed at re-measures at a third of its size**
  - **Source:** VanLehn (2011); Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - VanLehn states the received figures before testing them: answer-based tutoring systems, intelligent tutoring systems, and adult human tutors "are believed to be d = 0.3, 1.0, and 2.0 respectively."
      - His result: "This review did not confirm these beliefs. Instead, it found that the effect size of human tutoring was much lower: d = 0.79. Moreover, the effect size of intelligent tutoring systems was 0.76, so they are nearly as effective as human tutoring."
      - A meta-analysis of experimental PreK–12 tutoring evidence reports "an overall pooled ES of 0.288 SD (SE = 0.029, p < .001)."
      - It also reports where the effect concentrates: largest for programs using teachers or paraprofessionals, in earlier grades, at least three days a week, and held during school.
      - No study reproducing two sigma was located, and no failed replication attempt was located either. The record shows an absence of attempts, which is a different claim from attempts that failed.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two sigma was the number that made "give every student the time they need" look capable of closing most of the distribution. Re-measured twice, the most intensive individualized instruction available lands at 0.79 and 0.288. One provenance point: nobody tried to replicate the original and failed. An absence of attempts supports a weaker conclusion than a failed attempt would.
    - **Link to source:** [The Relative Effectiveness of Human Tutoring and Intelligent Tutoring Systems](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369) | [The Promise of Tutoring for PreK–12 Learning](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312231208687)

### Category 2: Matching Instruction to a Cognitive Profile — Three Attempts, Three Failures, and One Survivor

- **Subcategory 2.1: The program built to find these interactions concluded none were usable, and gave a structural reason**
  - **Source:** Cronbach (1975); Snow (1980), restating the 1977 handbook he co-wrote
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An **aptitude-treatment interaction**, or ATI, is a finding that one instructional method works better for students with one profile and a different method works better for another. It is the formal name for "match the teaching to the learner."
      - Cronbach's structural objection: "An ATI result can be taken as a general conclusion only if it is not in turn moderated by further variables. If Aptitude × Treatment × Sex interact, for example, then the Aptitude × Treatment effect does not tell the story. Once we attend to interactions, we enter a hall of mirrors that extends to infinity."
      - What the literature actually produced: "In attempting to generalize from the literature, Snow and I have been thwarted by the inconsistent findings coming from roughly similar inquiries. Successive studies employing the same treatment variable find different outcome-on-aptitude slopes."
      - He retracted his own earlier program: "Important as ATIs are proving to be, the line of investigation I advocated in 1957 no longer seems sufficient."
      - The handbook's verdict, as Snow restated it three years later: "Cronbach and Snow (1977) concluded that the existence of ATI as phenomena has been clearly established. But, while some ATI findings are plausible and some are replicable, few are well understood and none are yet applicable to instructional practice."
      - The sentence most often quoted from the 1977 handbook, usually cited to p. 492, could not be verified. The book is under controlled lending, and the page cited by the secondary source carrying the quote does not contain it. Snow's verified 1980 restatement is used instead.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The people who ran the search concluded the phenomena are real and none of them usable. That is a harder verdict than "evidence is mixed." Cronbach's reason does not expire with better data: any interaction can be moderated by an untested higher-order one, so an interaction result is never safely general. That argument applies to any scheme reading a treatment off a profile.
    - **Link to source:** [Beyond the two disciplines of scientific psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076829) | [Aptitude Research Project final report (ERIC ED204407)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED204407.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.2: The strictest test of a matching claim was deliberately weakened, and one study in a vast literature came close**
  - **Source:** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The criterion has four parts: sort learners by the measure, randomly assign learners within each group to at least two methods, give everyone the same achievement test, and show that the best method differs between groups.
      - The statistical signature required is a **crossover interaction** — the lines for the two groups have to cross when you plot outcome against method, not merely differ in height.
      - The authors weakened the test below the version usually claimed: "To provide the most liberal criterion in our search for evidence supporting the learning-styles hypothesis, we cast the hypothesis so that it requires only the style-by-method crossover interaction described previously. It does not require that the optimal method for each group would somehow match or conform to each group's learning style."
      - The yield: "despite the vast size of the literature on learning styles and classroom instruction, we found only one study that could be described as even potentially meeting the criteria described earlier, and as we report in the following text, even that study provided less than compelling evidence."
      - Studies with the right design found the opposite. On one of them: "Massa and Mayer found no support for any of these interactions despite exhaustive analysis of nearly 20 individual-difference measures."
      - The same authors keep the broader question open: "In summary, ATIs evidently do occur, but it has not been easy to determine exactly when they occur."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the clearest statement of what a matching claim has to produce, and the bar is low on purpose. One study out of an enormous literature came close, and the well-designed ones went the other way. The design criterion transfers unchanged to any claim that students with profile A should get treatment A, whatever the profile measures.
    - **Link to source:** [Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x)

- **Subcategory 2.3: The same idea ran at national scale under a different name and was abandoned**
  - **Source:** Stuebing, Fletcher, Branum-Martin, Francis & VanDerHeyden (2012); Burns et al. (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A **pattern of strengths and weaknesses**, or PSW, approach identifies a learning disability from the shape of a child's cognitive profile rather than from their academic performance, then selects instruction to match. It is the special-education version of profile-driven allocation.
      - Simulating three operational PSW methods: "all three methods had excellent specificity and negative predictive values, but low to moderate sensitivity and very low positive predictive values." **Positive predictive value** is the share of children flagged who genuinely have the condition.
      - The authors' conclusion names the instructional cost: "general application did not improve the efficiency of the decision model, may not be cost effective because of low base rates, and may result in many children receiving instruction that is not optimally matched to their specific needs."
      - The size of the problem, as restated by a later paper in the same program: "Low base rates ensured that NPV was high (median NPV = .99, range: .94 – .99). However, PPV was low across methods (median PPV = .22; range: .01 – .56)." These figures come through a citing paper rather than the primary tables.
      - Burns and colleagues screened 1,126 articles, kept 37, and found "a small effect (g = 0.17) for measures of cognitive functioning, but moderate effects of g = 0.43 and g = 0.48 for measures of reading fluency and phonemic/phonological awareness."
      - Their conclusion names the replacement: "the data do not support the use of cognitive measures to develop interventions but instead favor more direct measures of academic skills (e.g., reading fluency) in a skill-by-treatment interaction."
      - They also flag the thinness of the base: "There were few studies that examined measures of cognitive functioning within the intervention process."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The closest precedent to profile-driven allocation was implemented nationally and produced decisions that were right when they said no and mostly wrong when they said yes, at a positive predictive value near .22. The surviving substitution is to match on the academic skill instead, which carried effects two to three times larger.
    - **Link to source:** [Technical Adequacy of Three Methods for Identifying Specific Learning Disabilities](https://doi.org/10.1080/02796015.2012.12087373) | [Meta-analysis of academic interventions derived from neuropsychological data](https://doi.org/10.1037/spq0000117)

- **Subcategory 2.4: One moderator replicated, and it is prior knowledge in the subject rather than ability**
  - **Source:** Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003); Kalyuga (2007); Bjork & Bjork (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The **expertise reversal effect**: "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners."
      - Its scope: "a large number of cognitive load theory (CLT) effects that can be used to recommend instructional designs are, in fact, only applicable to learners with very limited experience. With additional experience, specific experimental effects can first disappear and then reverse."
      - A worked case in reading: additions to a high-school biology text meant to improve coherence "were beneficial only for low-knowledge readers. High-knowledge readers benefitted most from using the original, minimally coherent format text."
      - The novice side of the same mechanism: an equivalent reversal occurs "if novices must attempt to process very complex, high-element interactivity material or attempt to imagine complex material beyond working memory limits."
      - Bjork and Bjork on the limit of their own principle: "Many difficulties are undesirable during instruction and forever after... If, however, the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties."
      - Stated as a graded rule rather than a footnote: "The level of difficulty that is optimal, therefore, will vary with the degree of a learner's prior learning."
      - Kalyuga later tied the effect back to the older tradition himself: "patterns of empirical findings on the effect fit well those in studies of Aptitude Treatment Interactions (ATI) that were originally initiated in mid-60s." He also states that "learner knowledge base is a single most important cognitive characteristic that influences learning and performance."
      - In every passage located, both programs condition the moderator on background knowledge, skills, or prior learning in the domain. A targeted search of four Bjork publications and both Kalyuga reviews found no version conditioned on general ability, aptitude, or IQ.
      - Neither review reports a pooled effect size or a study count against stated inclusion criteria. Both are narrative reviews, and no meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect was located.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The one survivor of the whole program is specific about which variable does the work. Guidance, coherence, and difficulty must be set relative to how much the student already knows in that domain, and getting it wrong reverses the effect. Two traditions that disagree about almost everything else say it in nearly identical terms, and neither conditions it on ability.
    - **Link to source:** [The Expertise Reversal Effect](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4) | [Expertise Reversal Effect and Its Implications for Learner-Tailored Instruction](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3) | [Desirable difficulties in theory and practice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2020.09.003)

### Category 3: Where the Variance Actually Sits — Starting Knowledge, Rate, or Neither

- **Subcategory 3.1: Students differ enormously in what they know and barely at all in how fast they improve**
  - **Source:** Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The analysis covers "1.3 million observations across 27 datasets of student interactions with online practice systems in the context of elementary to college courses in math, science, and language" — six language, 16 math, five science, across eight elementary, eight middle and high school, and 11 college settings.
      - Starting knowledge at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles was 55.21%, 66.05%, and 75.17% correct. Learning rate at those same percentiles was 1.70%, 2.25%, and 2.56% improvement per practice opportunity.
      - Stated directly: "the interquartile range in variation for student learning rate... is only about 1% per opportunity (2.56 to 1.70%), whereas the variation in initial knowledge is about 20% (75.17 to 55.21%)."
      - Median student rate spread across datasets was 0.018 log odds. The same figure computed across **knowledge components** — the individual skills a subject decomposes into — was "orders of magnitude larger at 0.102" with a spread "seven times larger at 0.132."
      - Practice opportunities to reach 80% correct, split by starting knowledge: 13.13 for the lower half against 3.66 for the upper half. Split by rate: 7.89 for a slower learner against 6.94 for a faster one, so "a typical slower learner needs only one extra opportunity to keep pace with a typical faster learner."
      - Domain by domain, student rate spread was 0.045 in language, 0.017 in math, and 0.008 in science.
      - The result depends on the quality of the skill decomposition. With a poor one, "the model with learning rate (iAFM) never outperforms the model without it."
      - The authors' own limit: "not everyone has favorable learning conditions nor will everyone choose to engage in the substantial number of practice opportunities required."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** One candidate input to a placement decision is roughly twenty times the size of the other. Closing a starting-knowledge gap takes about ten extra practice opportunities; closing a rate gap takes about one. Read literally, place on level and stop differentiating on rate. Two conditions travel with it, and both sit on the design side: the decomposition has to be good, and the students were already practicing under favorable conditions.
    - **Link to source:** [An astonishing regularity in student learning rate](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2221311120)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The result replicates on new data and doubles when practice sequences are cut short**
  - **Source:** Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali (2024); Lee, Lichand, Barnard, Klotz, Thille, Kim & Domingue (2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An independent replication on "over 15,000 students and 821,890 observations across 6 math topics" reported that "Following the same exclusion criteria... we recovered the same interquartile range (i.e., middle 50%, IQR) reported in the original paper to the thousandth decimal place."
      - In that new data the largest student rate spread was 0.116, against a largest original value of 0.102.
      - A re-analysis refitting the same model to 26 of the 27 original datasets first confirmed the baseline: the figure "agrees with the original to within 0.003 for 24 of 26 datasets," median 0.020 against 0.018 reported.
      - Capping each student-skill practice sequence at its first ten opportunities moved the two components in opposite directions. Starting knowledge changed by a median of −1.5%. Rate changed by a median of **+118%**, spread +44% to +297%, inflating in 18 of 20 eligible datasets.
      - Comparing short against long practice sequences inside the same datasets, rate variance was larger in the short group in all 9 datasets that fit, median difference **+233%**, with 8 of 9 exceeding the original 0.018 baseline.
      - The structural fact that makes this bite: "The median pair length across the 26 datasets is 6.5 opportunities."
      - Three candidate mechanisms are named and explicitly not tested: mastery-based exit, adaptive routing, wheel-spinning or disengagement; misspecified curve shape, since "a linear approximation of a concave curve produces a steeper slope when fit through early opportunities"; and weak identification from sparse data.
      - The re-analysis authors' own limit: "The refits document sensitivity but do not test the mechanisms that produce it. Ten is one defensible truncation cap... but it is a single threshold."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Both are true because they test different things. The replication shows the model recovers near-zero rate variance given the same kind of data under the same rules. The re-analysis shows the estimate is not robust to which observations are included, and the asymmetry is the finding — truncation moves the rate parameter and leaves starting knowledge alone. The conditions producing it are ordinary mastery-system behavior.
    - **Link to source:** [Replicating an "Astonishing Regularity in Student Learning Rate"](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.12729850) | [Revisiting the Regularity of Student Learning Rate](https://doi.org/10.1145/3774398.3811619)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Rate is stable and ability-linked when measured as trials-to-criterion, and not a general property of a person**
  - **Source:** Zerr, Berg, Nelson, Fishell, Savalia & McDermott (2018); Zhang, Davis, Salthouse & Tucker-Drob (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Trials-to-criterion** counts how many test cycles a learner needs before recalling every item once. Measured that way on word pairs, it retested at r = .68 (95% CI [.61, .73]) across 30 hours in 281 people, and r = .70 (95% CI [.52, .83]) across a mean three-year gap in 46 people.
      - It tracked ability: the learning-efficiency composite correlated .43 with WASI-II Full Scale IQ, .44 with processing speed, and .43 with verbal memory. Trials-to-criterion correlated −.46 with IQ. A regression on all three ability measures reached R² = .33.
      - It was also discriminant: "none of the five personality measures included in the study were significantly correlated with Test 1, tests to criterion, final test, or overall learning-efficiency score (mean rs = .003, ps > .168)."
      - In two samples totalling 3,036 people across verbal and spatial learning tasks, starting-level parameters correlated across sessions with a median of .53 while slope parameters correlated with a "median .34," and the slopes were "all significantly different from zero, indicating that the slope parameters are not completely lacking in reliability."
      - Ability related to level and not rate: "In neither task was fluid or crystallized intelligence associated with learning," and fluid intelligence touched the verbal slope only in the 5–12 age group.
      - Rate did not generalize across materials: "the slope parameters were not correlated significantly with each other... there does not appear to be a material-independent learning ability that influences rate of learning."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Whether rate is usable depends on what is counted. Counted as trials-to-criterion, it is about as stable as a trait and tracks IQ at .43. Counted as a slope net of starting level, it is less reliable than the level beside it, ability predicts the level instead, and it does not transfer between verbal and spatial material in the same people. The stable version blends level, rate, and retention, which is the confound a slope exists to remove.
    - **Link to source:** [Learning Efficiency](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618772540) | [Correlates of individual, and age-related, differences in short-term learning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2007.01.004)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Intelligence predicts where a student stands and not how fast they grow**
  - **Source:** Murayama, Pekrun, Lichtenfeld & vom Hofe (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The design followed 3,530 students across six annual waves from Grade 5 to Grade 10, modelling math achievement as a growth curve with a starting point and a slope.
      - Intelligence predicted the starting point strongly: coefficient 4.72, standard error 0.27, p < .01. "Not surprisingly, intelligence had a strong positive relation with the intercept."
      - Intelligence did not predict the slope: coefficient 0.24, p = .88. "Intriguingly, in contrast to the relations with the intercept factor, intelligence did not predict the total amount of growth of math achievement over time."
      - Two things did predict the slope: perceived control, 3.78, p < .05, positively, and surface learning strategies, −5.99, p < .01, negatively. Deep learning strategies did not.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** In the longest school-achievement growth study here, intelligence split cleanly across the two parameters a placement decision cares about. It predicted the starting point at roughly twenty times its standard error and its relation to six years of growth was indistinguishable from zero. What did move the slope was perceived control and avoiding surface strategies, neither of which is a cognitive-ability measure.
    - **Link to source:** [Predicting Long-Term Growth in Students' Mathematics Achievement](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12036)

- **Subcategory 3.5: Once prior achievement is known, an ability test adds two to eight points of R²**
  - **Source:** Lavelle-Hill, Frenzel, Goetz, Lichtenfeld, Marsh, Pekrun, Sakaki, Smith & Murayama (2024); Thum & Kuhfeld (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using 105 predictors on 3,425 German secondary students over 5 to 9 years with held-out validation, prior achievement alone reached prediction R² of .58, .65, .65, and .75 across successive year-to-year intervals.
      - The extra R² contributed by everything else — self-reports, grades, and the cognitive tests together — beyond prior performance and school track was .07, .06, .08, and .02.
      - "In our data, IQ and prior achievement were correlated (Pearson r = .63). Combined with the relatively small predictive effect of IQ on the outcome variable, this pattern of findings suggests that the effect of IQ was mediated through prior achievement."
      - At longer gaps the increment stayed flat rather than growing: ".07 (lag = 1), .09 (lag = 2), .10 (lag = 3), and .07 (lag = 4)."
      - A large norming study builds growth expectations conditional on where a student started, scoring observed growth against growth predicted from starting score, grade, subject, and instructional weeks.
      - The relation between starting score and expected gain is strongly subject-dependent. Fall-to-spring expected gains at the 25th, 50th, and 75th starting percentiles were 9.611 / 8.156 / 6.701 for Grade 4 reading and 4.868 / 3.649 / 2.431 for Grade 8 reading, against 11.060 / 10.963 / 10.866 for Grade 4 math and 5.534 / 5.382 / 5.229 for Grade 8 math.
      - The report states its own sampling limit: partner schools "constitute an evolving convenience sample of the target population."
      - It gives no figure for how much growth variance starting score explains, and no reliability coefficient for gain scores. It reports conditional means with a constant conditional standard deviation instead.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Once prior achievement is in the model, everything else measured about a student including the cognitive tests adds two to eight points of R² against a base of fifty-eight to seventy-five, and the IQ effect appears to run through achievement rather than beside it. The norming data add an asymmetry no single allocation rule absorbs: in reading, lower-starting students are expected to gain much more, while in math expected gain is nearly flat.
    - **Link to source:** [How the predictors of math achievement change over time](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000863) | [NWEA 2020 MAP Growth Norms](https://teach.mapnwea.org/impl/normsResearchStudy.pdf)

### Category 4: What a Placement Estimate Delivers, and How Fast a System's Own Data Overtakes It

- **Subcategory 4.1: A single placement test misplaces a quarter to a third of students, and loses to an ordinary school record**
  - **Source:** Scott-Clayton (2012); Belfield & Crosta (2012)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A **severe error** is a student predicted to earn a B or better in the college-level course but placed into remediation, or a student placed into the college-level course who failed it. Across more than 42,000 first-time community college entrants, the severe error rate was 24.0% in math and 33.4% in English.
      - The errors run overwhelmingly one way. Severe *underplacement* was 18.3% against 5.8% overplacement in math, and 28.9% against 4.5% in English — roughly three to one and six to one.
      - "Math scores alone explain about 13 percent of the variation in first college-level math course grades, while reading/writing scores explain less than 2 percent of the variation in first college-level English grades."
      - Adding predictors did not repair it: a rich algorithm using test scores, high school background, and motivation proxies "could reduce severe placement errors by about 15 percent (from 24 to 21 percent in math, and from 33 to 28 percent in English)."
      - The underplacement figures are statistical extrapolations, not observations. By the author's own footnote they are "the mean of the predicted probabilities of each these eventualities. It is not based on specific assignment of students to each eventuality," because an underplaced student is never seen in the course they were kept out of.
      - In a separate statewide system, "The average R-squared value is 0.11 using high school GPA (compared to 0.01 for the placement tests), and it is the same size for both English and math." The school record won "in all 36 comparisons."
      - "Whereas the correlation across the eight placement tests and the six DE courses ranged between 0.08 and 0.18 on average, the correlation across HS GPA and the six DE courses ranged between 0.34 and 0.36."
      - Swapping the test for the record roughly halved severe error: English fell from 32.7% to 16.5% and from 26.8% to 12.3% depending on test battery.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Where placement accuracy is best measured, a purpose-built test misplaces a quarter to a third of students and a rich algorithm recovers about fifteen percent of that. Two features generalize. The dominant failure is holding back a student who would have passed, so expected cost is driven by unnecessary repetition. And an ordinary school record outpredicted the tests by an order of magnitude and halved severe error on its own.
    - **Link to source:** [Do High-Stakes Placement Exams Predict College Success?](https://ccrc.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/02/high-stakes-predict-success.pdf) | [Predicting Success in College](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED529827.pdf)

- **Subcategory 4.2: Moving a student down cost what moving one up gained, so the mechanism was access rather than accuracy**
  - **Source:** Barnett, Kopko, Cullinan & Belfield (2020); Kopko & Daniels Sarica (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a randomized trial across seven community colleges, 12,796 students were assigned either to business-as-usual single-test placement or to an algorithmic multiple-measures rule, then followed for nine terms.
      - How many students the rule moved: "In math, 16 percent of program group students were 'bumped up' to a college-level course; 10 percent were 'bumped down' to a remedial course. In English, 44 percent were bumped up and 7 percent were bumped down."
      - Students moved up did about as well as those already there: "the status quo pass rate (with grade C or higher) in college-level math was 63 percent; the bumped-up pass rate was 60 percent. The status quo pass rate in college-level English was 67 percent; the bumped-up pass rate was 65 percent."
      - Over nine terms, students in the bump-up zone gained about 15 percentage points in college-level math enrollment and about 8 in completion. In English, enrollment rose from 64% to 78% and completion by about 7 points.
      - Students in the bump-down zone lost about as much. College-level math enrollment fell from 78% to 62%, a 16-point drop, with 5 points off passing; English enrollment fell from 83% to 71% with 7 points off passing.
      - The evaluators drew the mechanism themselves: "The negative effect of bumping down students was similar in magnitude to the positive effect of bumping up students... students bumped down by the algorithm would have benefitted as much from college-level placement as those bumped up... These findings suggest that greater access to college-level courses rather than greater placement accuracy may be the mechanism by which MMA improves student success."
      - Across the full sample the gains stopped short of terminal outcomes: "We find no impact on student persistence or associate degree attainment."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the only randomized evidence that moves students both directions on a better algorithm, and the symmetry falsifies the obvious reading. If the algorithm added accuracy, moving a student down should have helped them. It hurt them by about what moving someone up gained, which is what you see when the operative variable is simply whether a student is let into the harder course.
    - **Link to source:** [The Long-Term Effectiveness of Multiple Measures Assessment](https://doi.org/10.1080/19345747.2025.2505172) | [Who Should Take College-Level Courses?](https://postsecondaryreadiness.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/multiple-measures-assessment-impact-findings.pdf)

- **Subcategory 4.3: A population prior predicts at chance on the first attempt and is finished by the third**
  - **Source:** Zhang, Das, Baker & Scruggs (2021); van der Velde, Sense, Borst & van Rijn (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using 274,590 responses from 4,151 students across 101 skills, predictive discrimination was reported separately by attempt number. **AUC** measures how well a model separates correct from incorrect answers, where 0.50 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect.
      - For Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, AUC was 0.49 on a student's first attempt at a skill, 0.63 on the second, and 0.68 on the third, staying between 0.66 and 0.70 after that.
      - The authors state what the first value means: "at this point, both BKT and PFA are performing at or below chance," and "Model performance is much more comparable by the time the student reaches their third attempt at a skill."
      - What the first prediction actually is: "the initial prediction made by BKT and PFA reflect the overall student performance across the entire (training) data set on that skill, instead of the individual student's knowledge level on the skill."
      - A model carrying more information into the first prediction did noticeably better there: its "AUC was 0.16 higher than BKT and 0.13 higher than PFA."
      - In the first of two preregistered experiments on giving an adaptive system better starting parameters, "Although the rate of forgetting predictions made by the Bayesian model were closer to observed values than the default prediction, this did not translate into an improvement in preregistered learning outcomes." The number of items studied did not differ.
      - In the second experiment, using material with more variable difficulty, delayed-recall accuracy "was about 6.8 percentage points higher (95% CI [3.4, 10.5])" after the informed block.
      - The authors' conditional conclusion: "We found that cold start mitigation improved learning outcomes, provided that there was sufficient variability in the difficulty of the study material."
      - No source located states, in any form, how many observations it takes before a system's own data outweighs its initial prior. No crossover point, prior half-life, or dominance threshold appears in this literature.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The closest measurement of what a pre-enrollment estimate is worth is the power of a population prior before any observation exists, and it is nil. One observation moves it to 0.63 and by the third it has reached its permanent plateau. No source states the point at which a system's own data overtakes its prior, so this stands in as a proxy, and it bounds the window to two or three observations per skill. Filling that window worked only when the content had enough spread for better scheduling to matter.
    - **Link to source:** [Knowledge Tracing Models' Predictive Performance when a Student Starts a Skill](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/EDM21_paper_126.pdf) | [Alleviating the Cold Start Problem in Adaptive Learning](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-021-00101-6)

- **Subcategory 4.4: Individualizing a starting estimate either helps a little or does nothing, and individualizing speed helps ten times more**
  - **Source:** Pardos & Heffernan (2010); Yudelson, Koedinger & Gordon (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 42 problem sets and 4,354 students, giving each student an individualized prior probability of knowing a skill beat standard knowledge tracing in 33 of 42 problem sets, with average correlation 0.3515 against 0.1933.
      - A prior derived from a *single* answer — assume they know it if the first response is correct, assume they don't if it is wrong — beat standard knowledge tracing in 30 of 42 problem sets. The authors concluded "much can be gained by grouping students into one of two priors based on their first response to a given skill."
      - Their own caveat on size: "while PPS has been shown to provide reliably better predictions, the increase in performance prediction accuracy may not be substantial."
      - On two datasets totalling 28.9 million rows from 9,353 students across 515 to 933 skills, the result reversed: "student-specific a priori probability of mastery (p(L0)) in model 2 has no effect on model performance. On the other hand, introduction of student specific speed of learning (p(T)) in model 3 results in a consistent and more pronounced advantage over models 1 and 2."
      - The magnitudes differ by an order of magnitude. Individualizing the starting estimate improved error by at most 0.00039; individualizing speed improved it by up to 0.00443. In one condition accuracy was slightly *worse* with an individualized starting estimate.
      - The authors flag the design difference that probably explains the conflict: "results in Table 2 are for student-stratified validation. Namely, individualized BKT models are making predictions on data from unseen students unable to use their learnt student-specific parameters."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two studies in the same tradition disagree, and the difference is a validation design rather than a fact about students. The positive result predicts held-out items for students whose earlier answers were seen; the null predicts unseen students, which is the condition a pre-enrollment estimate actually faces. Two details cut deeper: the best cheap prior came from one answer, and individualizing speed helped ten times more than individualizing level.
    - **Link to source:** [Modeling Individualization in a Bayesian Networks Implementation of Knowledge Tracing](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-13470-8_24) | [Individualized Bayesian Knowledge Tracing Models](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_18)

- **Subcategory 4.5: A grade-level test on an advanced student is uninformative by construction**
  - **Source:** Stanley (2000)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Above-level testing** means giving a student a test written for older students, so that a very advanced student does not simply answer everything correctly.
      - The finding: "SMPY found that half of all 7th graders who score 500-800 on SAT-M (about 14% of the talent-search participants score that well) know more algebra, as measured by the standardized algebra test, before they study the subject in school than do half of the students after completing a school year of it."
      - The operating rule, called **diagnostic testing followed by prescriptive instruction**: "Determine what the student does not know (diagnosis) and help him or her learn just that, without needless repetition."
      - The ceiling problem made concrete: a 12-year-old who scored 760 on SAT-M took a 40-item Algebra I test "on which a score of 32 correct is excellent for anyone who has completed a school year of that subject," and "The boy made no errors."
      - An instructional demonstration and the author's own verdict on it: "We taught 75 youths who reasoned exceptionally well mathematically the first year of algebra in 1 day!" with "About two thirds of the mentees reached our preset criterion of success, which we defined as the 85th percentile or higher" — followed by "this 1-day marathon was sort of a stunt. We did not expect such hastily acquired knowledge to 'stick.'"
      - On delivery: "Modern computer technology and adaptive testing can make that strategy feasible." His closing line: "Avoid trying to teach students what they already know."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Diagnosis is the strongest case for spending effort on an up-front estimate. A test an advanced student aces cannot say what to teach next, and half the top slice of a talent-search cohort already knew more algebra before instruction than half their peers did after a year. The fix is to measure above grade level; more precision at grade level changes nothing. Note that the author calls his own most dramatic result a stunt.
    - **Link to source:** [Helping students learn only what they don't already know](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-8971.6.1.216)

- **Subcategory 4.6: Grouping by current standing helped every quantile, and the payoff depends entirely on the grain**
  - **Source:** Duflo, Dupas & Kremer (2011); Steenbergen-Hu, Makel & Olszewski-Kubilius (2016); Steenbergen-Hu & Moon (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a randomized evaluation across 121 Kenyan primary schools, assigning students to classes by initial achievement rather than at random raised test scores by 0.139 SD (SE 0.078), or 0.176 SD (SE 0.077) with controls, after 18 months.
      - Both ends gained: "students in the top half of the preassignment distribution gained 0.19 standard deviations, and those in the bottom half gained 0.16 standard deviations. Students in all quantiles benefited from tracking."
      - The effect outlasted the program at 0.163 SD (SE 0.069) a year after it ended, against a class-size reduction in the same context producing 0.09 SD that "completely disappears within one year after the class size reduction stops."
      - The authors tie the result to a specific incentive context: Kenyan teachers "have incentives to focus on the students at the top of the distribution," inside "an educational system that is much more focused on the top of the distribution than in the United States."
      - A review of roughly a century of grouping research reports within-class grouping at g = 0.19 to 0.30, cross-grade subject grouping at 0.26, special grouping for gifted students at 0.37, and whole-class ability grouping at 0.04 to 0.06. It also states that "the effects did not vary for high-, medium-, and low-ability students." These are abstract-level figures; the full text was not accessed.
      - The same paper reports acceleration at g = 0.70 against non-accelerated same-age peers, 0.09 and non-significant against non-accelerated older peers, and 0.42 aggregated across forms.
      - The first-order meta-analysis by an author of that paper, covering 38 primary studies, reports achievement at g = 0.180 with a 95% confidence interval of [−.072, .431], an interval crossing zero, and social-emotional outcomes at 0.076, [−.025, .176]. "No strong evidence regarding the moderators of the effects was found."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Grouping precisely by current standing is not a null intervention, and unusually the randomized effect persisted a year after the program stopped while a class-size intervention in the same context did not. But the payoff depends on the grain: within-class or single-subject grouping returns 0.19 to 0.37 while sorting whole classes by general ability returns 0.04 to 0.06. Read the acceleration figures carefully, since the same lead author reports 0.42 and 0.180 with an interval crossing zero.
    - **Link to source:** [Peer Effects, Teacher Incentives, and the Impact of Tracking](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.5.1739) | [What One Hundred Years of Research Says About Ability Grouping and Acceleration](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316675417) | [The Effects of Acceleration on High-Ability Learners](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986210383155)

### Category 5: Moving Time Between Subjects Under a Fixed Budget

- **Subcategory 5.1: Doubling math time took the hour from art and music, and the students it helped most were defined by reading skill**
  - **Source:** Cortes, Goodman & Nomi (2015); Nomi & Allensworth (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The displacement was measured rather than assumed: "The treatment doubled instructional time in math, replaced largely elective courses, such as music and art, so that total coursework was unchanged."
      - In detail: "Relatively few of those replaced courses were in the core subjects of science, English and social studies. Many more were music and art courses and, to a lesser extent, vocational education and foreign language courses. Double dose algebra did not replace physical education or health courses."
      - Achievement effects were 0.18 SD on the ACT math score and 0.24 SD on an 11th-grade assessment. Under a sensitivity check for selective attrition, estimates "now fall into the 0.08-0.14 range, about half of the previous estimates."
      - Attainment effects ran larger than test-score effects: "Double-dosing raises the four-year graduation rates by 9.8 percentage points, driven largely by a 13.7 percentage point increases for poor readers." The authors note that "Test score effects under-predict attainment effects."
      - Who benefited was set by a skill the intervention was not targeting: "Perhaps because the intervention focused on verbal exposition of mathematical concepts, the impact was largest for students with below average reading skills, emphasizing the need to target interventions toward appropriately skilled students."
      - Stratified on incoming *math* skill instead, the weakest students did worst: "Students with very low initial abilities benefited less than students close to the national median," and "The very lowest-ability students showed declines in algebra grades (by .08 points)."
      - Students the policy never targeted were made worse off: "Across the three above-norm (high-ability) groups, grades dropped by .10 to .18 points from 2002 to 2003... and failure rates increased by four percentage points."
      - The treatment was not time alone: "For below-norm students who were targeted by the policy, the intervention was not just receiving extra instructional time, but receiving extra instructional time within homogenous low-ability classes."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The best-measured case of moving time toward a subject a group was weak in produces results no simple rule anticipates. The hour came from electives, so the cost fell outside the tested subjects. The group that gained most was defined by reading skill in a math intervention, because the extra teaching was verbal. The lowest-skill students in the target subject gained least, and untargeted students got worse.
    - **Link to source:** [Intensive Math Instruction and Educational Attainment](https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.50.1.108) | ["Double-Dose" Algebra as an Alternative Strategy to Remediation](https://doi.org/10.1080/19345740802676739)

- **Subcategory 5.2: Added time decays to a third within two years, and turns negative past about 500 weekly minutes**
  - **Source:** Taylor (2014); Rivkin & Schiman (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using two cutoffs in middle-school math scheduling, "Math achievement grows much faster under the requirement, 0.16–0.18 student standard deviations" — 0.176 SD at the 50th-percentile cut and 0.166 SD at a second cut near the 24th percentile.
      - The gain did not hold: "one year after returning to a regular one-class schedule, the initial gains decay by as much as half, and two years later just one-third of the initial treatment effect remains."
      - It did not convert into course progression either: "treated students were no more likely to have completed Algebra I by the end of 9th grade or to have completed Algebra II by the end of high school."
      - The author names the cost: "One cost is notable, more time in math crowds out instruction in other subjects."
      - Using variation within the same student across subjects, additional weekly minutes raised achievement with coefficients of 0.068, 0.025, and 0.017 across three specifications.
      - Returns diminish and reverse: "the results in both fixed effect specifications strongly support the hypothesis of diminishing returns. Importantly, the return to additional minutes diminishes quite slowly, becoming negative at over 500 minutes per week."
      - The return depends on the room the time is spent in: "an additional hour of weekly instruction time raises achievement by more than twice as much at the 75th percentile (0.025 standard deviations) than at the 25th percentile (0.011 standard deviations)" of classroom environment quality.
      - Their conclusion: "Schools with behaviors that place them in the lower tail in terms of classroom environment therefore are likely to realize little or no benefit from increases in instructional time."
      - They found no significant interaction with their measured *instruction* quality index and decline to accept their own null: "we believe that the more compelling interpretation is that our indirect measures fail to capture salient differences."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Added time buys about 0.17 SD while it lasts, and it is a stock rather than a permanent shift: half gone a year after the extra time stops, two thirds within two, with no effect on later course progression. Returns turn negative past roughly 500 weekly minutes in one subject, and an added hour is worth more than twice as much in a good environment as a poor one.
    - **Link to source:** [Spending more of the school day in math class](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2014.06.002) | [Instruction Time, Classroom Quality, and Academic Achievement](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12315)

- **Subcategory 5.3: The one experiment that manipulated allocation found medium-difficulty material won**
  - **Source:** Metcalfe & Kornell (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Given free choice, learners did not work on their weakest material: "In contrast to the dominant discrepancy reduction model, which favors the most difficult items, people, given free choice, devoted most time to medium-difficulty items and studied the easiest items first."
      - The causal test: "When study time was experimentally manipulated, best performance resulted when most time was given to the medium-difficulty items."
      - The measured mechanism: "Empirically determined information uptake functions revealed steep initial learning for easy items with little subsequent increase. For medium-difficulty items, initial gains were smaller but more sustained, suggesting that the strategy people had used, when given free choice, was largely appropriate."
      - The model made a further prediction that held: "On the basis of the information uptake functions, a negative spacing effect was predicted and observed in the final experiment."
      - Every experiment in this literature works on individual items inside a single task. No study was located that experimentally contrasted giving time to a student's weakest *subject* against giving it to material just beyond current mastery.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** One experiment manipulated allocation instead of watching what learners chose, and the best outcome came from concentrating on intermediate material. Measured uptake curves explain why: easy material is learned fast and stops repaying attention, while intermediate material gains more slowly and keeps gaining. The limit is grain. Nothing extends this to dividing a week between subjects.
    - **Link to source:** [The Dynamics of Learning and Allocation of Study Time to a Region of Proximal Learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.132.4.530)

- **Subcategory 5.4: The personalized-learning estimate fell from 0.27 SD to three percentile points on a different cohort**
  - **Source:** Pane, Steiner, Baird & Hamilton (2015); Pane, Steiner, Baird, Hamilton & Pane (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The first report: "The two-year effect sizes for this student population across all schools were 0.27 in mathematics and 0.19 in reading."
      - "There were positive results across all grade levels, although the effects tended to be larger in the elementary grades and were not statistically significant in the high school grades."
      - The authors' own central caveat: "it is important to use caution in interpreting these results as causal effects of personalized learning. Although the analyses used the best estimation strategies possible given the nature of the data and limits to implementing a strong experimental design, it was not possible to separate personalized learning effects from other potential school effects."
      - Two years later, on a different cohort: "We estimate study students gained about 3 percentile points in mathematics relative to a comparison group of similar students. In reading, there was a similar trend, though it was not statistically significant. Low-performing and high-performing students appeared to benefit."
      - On implementation as observed rather than designed: "none of the schools looking as radically different from traditional schools as theory might predict," and several practices "did not appear to differ from practices in schools nationally, such as student discussions with teachers on progress and goals; keeping up-to-date documentation of student strengths, weaknesses, and goals; and student choice of topics and materials."
      - The authors attribute the gap to sample composition rather than a corrected estimate, noting the earlier schools had more implementation experience and were mostly charter schools, so the two studies are not directly comparable.
      - Their own standard for settling it: "these results must be confirmed using more-rigorous experimental study designs."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The most-cited evidence for individualized, self-paced delivery comes from the evaluations commissioned to study it, and read in sequence they show how much of an apparent effect is the sample. Both sets of authors say plainly that neither design separates the intervention from the schools that adopted it. The implementation finding travels furthest: the schools did not look as different from ordinary schools as the theory predicted, and the practices closest to profile-driven allocation were no more common than nationally.
    - **Link to source:** [Continued Progress: Promising Evidence on Personalized Learning](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR1365.html) | [Informing Progress: Insights on Personalized Learning Implementation and Effects](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR2042.html)

### Category 6: Scheduling the Time Once It Is Allocated

- **Subcategory 6.1: Spacing is one of the most robust effects here, and its canonical sources never test whether it depends on the learner**
  - **Source:** Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006); Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Spacing** means separating study sessions in time; **massing** means putting them back to back. The synthesis covered 317 experiments in 184 articles out of 427 reviewed, yielding 271 direct massed-versus-spaced comparisons.
      - Across all retention intervals, mean recall was 36.7% massed against 47.3% spaced, a 10.6-point gap across 14,811 participants, t(540) = 6.6, p < .001.
      - "Only 12 of 271 comparisons of massed and spaced performance showed no effect or a negative effect from spacing, making the spacing effect quite robust."
      - The corpus skews short and adult: "The vast majority of mean performance differences (80 percent) used a retention interval of less than one day," and of roughly 839 comparisons, 714 came from young adults aged 18–35. The age table is a count table, and no spacing-by-age contrast is tested.
      - Neither canonical source reports any moderator analysis by student ability, prior knowledge, learning speed, or aptitude. A full-text search of both for ability, individual-difference, moderator, prior-knowledge and aptitude terms returns only the note that children and older adults were included.
      - On the size of the gap between sessions, across more than 1,350 people with gaps up to 3.5 months: "The optimal gap increased as test delay increased. However, when measured as a proportion of test delay, the optimal gap declined from about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year test delay."
      - In absolute terms the optimal gaps were "approximately 1.6, 7, 10, and 25 days (24%, 19%, 14%, and 7% of the RIs)," and choosing the optimal gap over no gap produced "10, 59, 111, and 77% improvement in recall" at retention intervals of 7, 35, 70, and 350 days.
      - Their conclusion: "The interaction of gap and test delay implies that many educational practices are highly inefficient."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The main effect is about as secure as anything here: spacing won 259 of 271 comparisons at roughly eleven points of recall, and getting the gap right rather than wrong is worth tens of percent. The actionable structure is that the optimal gap scales with how long the material has to last, non-proportionally. The one thing a student-specific schedule needs is missing. Nobody tested it, and an untested question supports a weaker conclusion than a tested null.
    - **Link to source:** [Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) | [Spacing Effects in Learning: A Temporal Ridgeline of Optimal Retention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)

- **Subcategory 6.2: The two studies that did test learner moderation of spacing contradict each other on weak students**
  - **Source:** Barzagar Nazari & Ebersbach (2019); Ruitenburg, Guldemont, Kirschner, Jarodzka & Camp (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - With 81 seventh-graders practicing statistics either massed on one day or spread over three, there was no evidence of an effect at two weeks but "strong evidence for a positive effect of distributed practice" at six weeks.
      - The moderation finding: "Exploratory analyses indicated that especially students in the medium performance range benefitted from distributed practice," with no significant effect in either the low or the high range.
      - The authors' own limit: "These assumptions, however, are based on exploratory analyses and need further empirical confirmation."
      - With 61 fifth-graders on math problem solving, the main spacing effect on one-week-delayed performance was β = 19.14, z = 2.36, p = 0.018.
      - The interaction of initial performance with schedule was not significant, β = −0.24, z = −1.28, p = 0.202, but simple-slope analysis found a spacing effect "for participants with low (β = 29.18, z = 2.87, p = 0.004) and average (β = 19.29, z = 2.42, p = 0.015) initial practice performance, but not for those with high initial [practice performance]."
      - Those authors flag that their result contradicted their own hypothesis: "We expected distributed practice to be less beneficial also for students with a low initial performance level."
      - Their statistical caveat applies to the whole question: "notoriously large samples are needed to identify such moderations as significant... but is a limitation of our study."
      - They confirm the high-performer null was not a measurement artifact: "the disappearance of the spacing effect for students with high initial practice performance was not due to a ceiling effect."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two small classroom studies tested the exact question a student-specific schedule needs, and they agree on one thing only: high performers get no spacing benefit. On weak students they point opposite ways, one finding nothing at the bottom and the other finding the largest effect there and saying so contradicted its own prediction. Neither interaction reached significance, at 81 and 61 students.
    - **Link to source:** [Distributed practice in mathematics: Recommendable especially for students on a medium performance level?](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tine.2019.100122) | [Initial Practice Performance Moderates the Distributed Practice Effect](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.70032)

- **Subcategory 6.3: Interleaving reverses based on the material, not the student, and its time cost has never been measured**
  - **Source:** Brunmair & Richter (2019); Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Interleaving** means mixing problem types together rather than doing all of one type before moving on. Across 238 effect sizes in 158 samples from 59 papers and 8,466 participants, "We found a moderate overall positive effect of interleaving (k = 238; g = 0.42; p < .001, 95% CI [0.34, 0.50])."
      - The effect depends heavily on material and reverses for one type: paintings g = 0.67, naturalistic photographs 0.35, mathematical tasks 0.34, expository texts no significant effect, and **words g = −0.39**.
      - "Interleaving leads to poorer learning outcomes than blocking when items of different conceptual categories are presented," and the authors advise caution "especially for expository texts and words."
      - Prior knowledge and expertise were not coded as moderators. The coded moderators were population, mean age, material type, between- and within-category similarity, and material complexity, and mean age was available for only 97 of 238 effect sizes.
      - In a preregistered trial where 54 seventh-grade classes did interleaved or blocked assignments over four months and were tested a month later, "the interleaved group outscored the blocked group, 61% versus 38%, d = 0.83," 95% CI [0.68, 0.97], with "a positive effect for each of the 15 teachers, ds 0.23–1.48."
      - The authors state the time cost and that nobody has measured it: "Interleaved practice probably takes more time... every teacher reported that the interleaved assignments took more time than did blocked practice. To the extent that this was true, the observed interleaving effect would have been smaller if it had been measured per unit of time invested by the student. To our knowledge, no previous study of interleaved mathematics practice has measured time on task."
      - A prior-knowledge boundary condition: "Interleaved practice might be less effective or too difficult if students do not first receive at least a small amount of blocked practice when they encounter a new skill or concept... the data do not suggest that students should entirely avoid blocked practice."
      - A second condition: "Interleaved practice might be effective only if students receive corrective feedback."
      - They name student proficiency as untested: "the effect size likely depends on other factors. This list includes the usual possibilities, such as student proficiency, teacher buy-in, duration of the intervention, choice of material, and degree of transfer required."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Interleaving is among the better-evidenced scheduling moves here, and its documented reversals are driven by what is being learned rather than who is learning it, with word learning actually favoring blocking. Two author-stated caveats matter more than the effect size under a fixed budget: every teacher said interleaved work took longer and nobody has measured time on task, so the headline is an upper bound of unknown tightness.
    - **Link to source:** [Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209) | [A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)
