# DOK 1 — Who Gets Missed: The Size of the Unidentified Gifted Population and the Mechanism Behind It

**Research question.** What proportion of academically gifted children are never identified, and what
specifically causes them to be missed?

Fact extraction only. No interpretation, no editorializing. Every number and every quoted phrase below was
read out of the source named beside it. Exact quotes are in quotation marks. Anything I could not verify is
flagged inline **and** listed again in the final numbered section.

## Modelled vs. measured

Every fact below is tagged:

- **[MEASURED]** — a number observed on real children in a real district, state, or national dataset.
- **[MODELLED]** — a number produced by simulation, or by applying an assumed distribution to a population.
  These are estimates of what *would* happen, not counts of what *did*.
- **[META]** — a pooled estimate across studies.

## Q1 — The headline miss rate

There is no single agreed "X% of gifted children are never identified" number in the literature. What exists
are three distinct kinds of estimate, which answer different questions and should not be conflated. All three
are below.

### 1.1 The population-count estimate: Gentry et al. (2019), "System Failure: Access Denied"

**Source.** Gentry, M., Gray, A. M., Whiting, G. W., Maeda, Y., & Pereira, N. (2019). *System Failure: Access
Denied. Gifted Education in the United States: Laws, Access, Equity, and Missingness Across the Country by
Locale, Title I School Status, and Race.* Report Cards, Technical Report, and Website. Gifted Education
Research & Resource Institute (GERI), Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN; Jack Kent Cooke Foundation,
Lansdowne, VA.
[https://www.education.purdue.edu/geri/new-publications/gifted-education-in-the-united-states/](https://www.education.purdue.edu/geri/new-publications/gifted-education-in-the-united-states/)

**VERIFICATION STATUS — PARTIAL, and I flag it prominently.** This is a technical report, not a
peer-reviewed article, and it has no DOI. I could **not** retrieve the technical report PDF itself. Every
number below is quoted from a source that cites the report, not from the report's own pages:

- Gentry, M. (2020), "Access and Equity in Gifted Education in Arizona and Beyond," presentation slides
  hosted by the Arizona Association for Gifted and Talented,
  [arizonagifted.org PDF](https://arizonagifted.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Gentry-AZ-Reportcard-Slides.pdf)
  — authored by the report's lead author and reproducing its headline findings verbatim.
- Peters, S. J., Gentry, M., Whiting, G. W., & McBee, M. T. (2019) and the authors' own summary in *The
  Conversation*, "Schools often fail to identify gifted and talented students,"
  [theconversation.com/130506](https://theconversation.com/schools-often-fail-to-identify-gifted-and-talented-students-especially-if-they-are-black-latino-or-native-american-130506).

Treat the numbers below as author-reported, not as independently verified against the technical report.

**SAMPLE. [MEASURED base, MODELLED counterfactual]** Every US public school in the 2015–2016 Office for
Civil Rights CRDC census (with comparisons to 2000, 2012, 2014). K–12. This is a federal census, not a
sample. What is *modelled* is the counterfactual identification rate; the actual identification counts are
measured.

**Their definition of "missingness," quoted.** "Missingness is defined as the students missing from gifted
identification either because they attended schools that did not identify or because they were
underidentified in their schools that did identify."

**The headline numbers.**

- "At the upper boundary more students are missing from gifted education identification than are identified
  (3.6 million missing and 3.2 million identified)." (The Conversation piece gives the identified count as
  3.3 million for 2015–2016; the slides say 3.2 million. **These two author-sourced figures disagree by
  0.1 million and I do not know which the technical report states.**)
- "42% of [schools] in 2015–2016 [did] not identify a single student with gifts and talents."
- Missingness rate by racial group, as percentage of that group's would-be-identified population, given as a
  range (lower to upper boundary): **Black 63%–74%; Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander 59%–72%; Latinx
  53%–66%; American Indian/Alaska Native 48%–63%; Two or more races 29%–49%; White 29%–42%; Asian 20%–26%.**
- Worked example for one group, from the authors' own summary: "only 276,840 Black students were identified
  as gifted and talented in 2016. We estimate that as many as 771,728 would be identified this way if systems
  were working properly."
- "Students who attend Title I schools are identified [at] a rate of only 58% of their counterparts in
  Non-Title I schools."
- Representation indices reported for the same data: American Indian/Alaska Native 0.83, Black 0.57, Latinx
  0.67, Native Hawaiian 0.62.
- State report cards: "We gave 17 states failing grades because fewer than 60% of their public schools
  identified anyone as gifted and talented. Six more got a D."

**How the 3.6 million was computed**, per the lead author quoted in *The Hechinger Report*
([hechingerreport.org](https://hechingerreport.org/up-to-3-6-million-students-should-be-labeled-gifted-but-arent/)):
"To calculate the number of 3.6 million overlooked students, the researchers first applied the 10 percent
average to the roughly 4 of 10 schools that had identified no gifted students at all, Gentry said. Then they
adjusted that number for the thousands of Latinx, African American, Native American/Alaska Native and Native
Hawaiian/Pacific Islander students who would have been included if they were found to be gifted at the same
rate as their white and Asian peers." The same piece reports: "On average, in the six of 10 schools that have
identified gifted children, 10 percent of students were classified as gifted," and "Low-income [schools]
identified 8 percent of their students as gifted, compared to 13 percent of students at [wealthier] schools."

**What this estimate is and is not.** It is a *counterfactual applied to a federal census*: it asks how many
more students would carry the label if every school identified at the observed average rate and if
underrepresented groups were identified at the same rate as White and Asian students. It is **not** a
measurement of how many children have high ability and lack the label. It takes current identification rates
as the normative standard.

A peer-reviewed article by the same team reports the Black-student component with a DOI: Gentry, M., Whiting,
G. W., & Gray, A. M. (2022), "Systemic Inequities in Identification and Representation of Black Youth with
Gifts and Talents," DOI [10.1177/00420859221095000](https://doi.org/10.1177/00420859221095000), abstract:
"in every state and school locale, Black youth were underidentified on average by 50%. In 2016, 276,840 Black
students were identified as gifted, with as many as 771,728 (73.60%) missing from identification." **DOI
verification for this one is listed in the could-not-verify section — I did not resolve it against
Crossref.**

### 1.2 The psychometric estimate: sensitivity of a typical identification system is 30–40%

This is the estimate that most directly answers "what proportion of children who meet a district's own
standard never get the label." It comes from McBee, Peters & Miller (2016) — full detail in **Q3** below —
and is restated by a second author team in a separate paper:

**Source for the restatement.** McBee, M. T., & Makel, M. C. (2019). "The Quantitative Implications of
Definitions of Giftedness." *AERA Open*, 5(1). DOI
[10.1177/2332858419831007](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007).
**Crossref returned:** title "The Quantitative Implications of Definitions of Giftedness"; journal *AERA
Open*; issued 2019-01; authors McBee, Matthew T. / Makel, Matthew C.; 5(1). **MATCH.** Open access; ERIC
EJ1210517.

**The statement, quoted exactly.** "Recent research in the field suggests that the current number of
identified students is likely a substantial underestimate. For example, McBee, Peters, and Miller (2016)
found that **the sensitivity of a typical identification process is on the order of 30% to 40%** due to poor
psychometric performance. This implies that a substantially larger proportion of students actually qualifies
as gifted (with respect to true scores) under existing identification criteria."

**[MODELLED].** Sensitivity of 30–40% implies a false negative rate of 60–70%. This is a simulation result,
not a count of real children. See Q3 for the full parameter set it depends on.

For context in the same paper: "According to Office of Civil Rights data from 2013–2014, 6.7% of American
public school students are identified as gifted or talented."

### 1.3 The above-grade-level estimate: how many students are already working beyond their grade

This reframes the question from "who is gifted" to "who is not being challenged," and produces much larger
numbers. Reported inside McBee & Makel (2019), citing Peters, Rambo-Hernandez, Makel, Matthews & Plucker
(2017):

"Peters et al. (2017) examined three state administrative data sets as well as a national data set of scores
on the NWEA Measures of Academic Progress (MAP), a vertically equated computer-adaptive achievement measure
that has an exceptionally high ceiling. Using the state achievement test data, they found that **20% to 49%
of students were performing at least one grade level above placement in English/language arts, and 14% to 37%
in mathematics.** Using MAP data, they found that the percentage of students performing above grade level was
**14% in math and 35% in reading.** These percentages translate into millions of students. Against a standard
of which students need more challenge, the Peters et al. (2017) data suggest that the answer to the question
of how many students are gifted is likely a great many more than are currently being identified."

The underlying paper is Peters, Rambo-Hernandez, Makel, Matthews & Plucker, "Should Millions of Students Take
a Gap Year? Large Numbers of Students Start the School Year Above Grade Level," *Gifted Child Quarterly*,
61(3), 229–238, DOI 10.1177/0016986217701834. **I did not resolve this DOI against Crossref and did not read
the paper — listed in the could-not-verify section.** [MEASURED — these are real test scores on real
students, unlike 1.2.]

### 1.4 What no source I found provides

I did not find any study that measures, on a defined population of children, (a) how many meet a stated
ability criterion and (b) how many of those carry a gifted label, and reports the ratio. Every "miss rate"
above is either a counterfactual applied to administrative counts (1.1), a psychometric simulation (1.2), or
a redefinition of the target population (1.3). The closest thing to a direct measurement is Card & Giuliano's
Broward experiment (Q2), which measures the increase in identification when the referral gate is removed but
does not report a denominator of all truly eligible children.

## Q2 — Universal screening as a natural experiment

### 2.1 Card & Giuliano, Broward County — the core natural experiment

**Source.** Card, D., & Giuliano, L. (2016). "Universal screening increases the representation of low-income
and minority students in gifted education." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 113(48),
13678–13683. DOI [10.1073/pnas.1605043113](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113).
**Crossref returned:** title "Universal screening increases the representation of low-income and minority
students in gifted education"; journal *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*; issued 2016-11-15;
authors Card, David / Giuliano, Laura; 113(48):13678–13683. **MATCH.**
Working-paper version: Card & Giuliano, NBER Working Paper 21519 (2015), "Can Universal Screening Increase
the Representation of Low Income and Minority Students in Gifted Education?"
[nber.org/papers/w21519](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21519) — note the working paper title differs from the
published title.

**Note on district identity.** The paper never names the district; it says only "a large and diverse school
district in the state of Florida (hereafter 'the District')" with 140 larger elementary schools. It is widely
reported as Broward County. **I did not find the district named in the paper itself, so I do not assert it
here.** (Listed in the could-not-verify section.)

**SAMPLE. [MEASURED]** Third graders in "the District," Florida. Grade 3, ages ~8–9. Full analytic sample
n = 78,065 across the 2004–2007 third-grade cohorts (pre cohorts 2004–2005: n = 39,933; post cohorts
2006–2007: n = 38,132). Plan B subsample n = 37,947. Restricted to first-time third-grade enrollees at the
140 larger elementary schools, excluding charters and special schools. Figure 1 extends the district series
to 2011. **Ability range covered: the full district population** — this is a census of a grade, not a
high-ability sample. Racial composition pre-period: 35% White non-Hispanic, 34% Black non-Hispanic, 25%
Hispanic, 3% Asian; 45% FRL; 11% ELL.

**The design.** Universal NNAT (Naglieri Non-Verbal Ability Test) screening of all second graders was
introduced in spring 2005. Before that, "candidates for gifted status were identified through parent and
teacher referrals, mainly occurring in first and second grades."

**Eligibility standards held constant — the authors say so explicitly.** "Importantly, the IQ thresholds and
other requirements for gifted eligibility were unchanged. Any increase in the number of students identified
as gifted following the introduction of the program can thus be attributed to the screening effort, and not
to a relaxation of the standards for gifted status."

Thresholds: Florida law required IQ ≥ 130 for non-ELL/non-FRL students ("Plan A"); the district set 116 for
ELL or FRL students ("Plan B"). NNAT referral cutoffs were 130 (Plan A) and 115 (Plan B).

**Pre-screening baseline (the inequity being measured).** "Only 28% of gifted students in third grade were
Black or Hispanic, compared with 60% of all students in the District. Thirteen regular elementary schools in
the District had no gifted children in third grade in 2004 or 2005, but the gifted rate was nearly 10% at the
13 schools with the lowest fraction of FRL students."

**RESULT 1 — identification rates before and after. [MEASURED]** From Table 1 and Table 2 of the paper,
fraction identified as gifted by end of third grade:

| Plan A eligible (non-FRL, non-ELL) | 0.051 | 0.066 | 0.015 (0.002)** | 0.006 (0.005) n.s. | 1.108 (0.115) n.s. |
| Plan B eligible (FRL or ELL) | 0.014 | 0.043 | 0.029 (0.002)** | 0.025 (0.004)** | 2.743 (0.446)** |

SEs in parentheses, clustered by school. ** p < .01, * p < .05. School-level difference-in-differences
(District vs. matched comparison schools in other Florida districts): 0.016 (0.003)**.

**The odds-ratio statement in the authors' own words.** "With no change in the minimum standards for gifted
status, the screening program led to a 174% increase in the odds of being identified as gifted among all
disadvantaged students, with a 118% increase for Hispanics and a 74% increase for Blacks." Elsewhere: "The
overall impact for third graders translates into a 45% increase in the odds of being identified as gifted"
and "the impact for Whites is relatively small (12%) and differs significantly from the impact for Blacks and
Hispanics combined (P < 0.01)."

**RESULT 2 — IQ testing rates. [MEASURED]** Fraction IQ-tested by end of third grade rose from 0.16 to 0.24
overall; Plan A 0.18 → 0.23; Plan B 0.13 → 0.24. The authors note this "close[d] the testing gap between the
two groups." About 1,300 additional IQ tests were administered by district staff in summer/fall after the
first spring-2005 screening, with a similar number in 2006.

**RESULT 3 — who the newly identified students were ("compliers"). [MEASURED]** Compliers = students
identified as gifted post-screening who would not have been identified pre-screening; always takers =
students who would have been identified either way. From Table 3, full sample:

Within the Plan B subsample only, compliers vs. Plan B always takers: mean IQ 124.5 vs. 124.2, difference
0.38 (1.14), **not significant** — i.e. within the same eligibility rule, the newly found students had
essentially the same measured IQ as the students the old system was already finding, but 0.35 SD lower
achievement.

**RESULT 4 — the figure specifically asked for: share of newly identified students who already met the
strict threshold. [MEASURED]** In the authors' exact words, about the Plan B group: "Moreover, a full 20% of
the compliers have IQs of 130 or higher (vs. 25% of the always takers). This suggests that many high-ability
disadvantaged students are at risk for being overlooked in a traditional parent/teacher referral system."

So: **20% of the newly identified Plan B (low-income or English-learner) students scored at or above 130** —
the *non*-disadvantaged Plan A threshold — meaning they would have qualified under the strictest standard and
were still missed by parent/teacher referral.

A parallel statement for Plan A: "About 30% of the compliers have scores under 125 points, vs. 8% of the
always takers. Virtually all those with IQ < 125 were classified as Plan B at the time of IQ testing but are
Plan A by the end of third grade (mainly because they transition out of ELL status). However, apart from this
group, many of the newly identified Plan A gifted students had IQ scores well above the minimum 130-point
threshold. We thus conclude that the traditional referral system also misses some high-ability
nondisadvantaged students."

**RESULT 5 — school-level distribution. [MEASURED]** "all 140 larger elementary schools in the District had
at least one gifted third-grade student in 2006 or 2007, whereas, in 2004 and 2005, 13 schools had no gifted
students." And on pre-period concentration: "Half of gifted students in the pre cohort were at schools that
enrolled only 18% of the entire third-grade population, whereas half of all third-grade students in the
District were at schools that enrolled a total of only 16% of the gifted students."

**RESULT 6 — what happened when the district suspended screening. [MEASURED]** "because of financial
pressures caused by the Great Recession, the District cut funding for IQ testing in 2007 and suspended the
screening program in 2010. By 2011, the gifted share of third graders had returned to the level of
2004–2005."

The authors use this reversal as an identification check on an alternative explanation: "[While the screening
program may have raised parent and teacher awareness about the gifted program, the return of gifted rates to
their prescreening levels after the program was suspended in 2011 (Fig. 1) suggests that increased awareness
cannot explain the rise in gifted rates after the program's introduction.]"

From the SI: "In response to a budget crisis in 2007, the District elected to cut the overtime budget for
testing staff. The result, clearly evident in Fig. 1, was a sharp decline in the fraction of students placed
in the gifted program by the end of third grade. Continuing budget pressures led the District to suspend the
screening program in 2010."

**Comparison-group stability (so the reversal is not a statewide artifact).** "Between 2004 and 2007, the
gifted share of third graders in the comparison group is relatively stable, with only a slight upward trend
(3.5% in 2004, 3.6% in 2005, and 3.7% in 2006 and 2007)." Across the 22 other large Florida districts, the
mean and SD of the pre→post change in gifted rate were −0.001 and 0.007; "The observed change in the District
is 2.65 SDs from the mean for the other districts."

**AUTHORS' STATED CONCLUSION.** "A comparison of the newly identified gifted students to those who would have
been identified even without screening shows that Black and Hispanic students, FRL participants, ELLs, and
girls were all systematically 'underreferred' to the gifted program." And: "We hypothesize that parents and
teachers often fail to recognize the potential of many poor, immigrant, and minority children with less than
stellar achievement levels."

Also: "This suggests that the traditional referral system tends to miss disadvantaged students with modest
achievement levels, regardless of their cognitive abilities. Teachers and parents may simply not recognize
the abilities of many of these students."

**AUTHORS' STATED LIMITATION — quoted in full because it bounds the generalization.** "An important
limitation of our analysis is that it pertains to only a single school district. Although the student
population in the District is highly diverse, and arguably representative of the student population in many
large urban districts, the District's gifted policies have a number of distinctive features. First, both the
use of a nonverbal screening test and the lower Plan B referral threshold may have been important factors in
the success of the District's screening program. Second, the District has strict IQ thresholds for
eligibility, and, before the introduction of the screening program, the District had relatively low gifted
rates (around 3.5% for third graders) that reflected the importance of the IQ thresholds. At a minimum,
however, our findings suggest that the underrepresentation of poor and minority students in gifted education
is not due solely to the lower IQ scores of these students. A substantial share of the gap appears to be
caused by the failure of the traditional parent/teacher referral system to identify high-ability
disadvantaged students."

### 2.2 The working-paper version reports the same result in "increase in number" terms

The NBER working paper (Card & Giuliano, WP 21519, "Can Universal Screening Increase the Representation of
Low Income and Minority Students in Gifted Education?",
[nber.org PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21519/w21519.pdf)) states the same finding
in percentage-increase rather than odds-ratio form: "With no change in the minimum standards for gifted
status the screening program led to a **180% increase in the gifted rate among all disadvantaged students,
with a 130% increase for Hispanic students and an 80% increase for black students.**" And: "The added Plan
B's represent a 180% increase in the total number of disadvantaged... students in third grade relative to the
numbers in 2004/2005."

**Note the discrepancy with the published paper**, which gives 174% / 118% / 74% as odds-ratio increases. The
working-paper numbers (180 / 130 / 80) are rate increases and are rounded. **Cite the PNAS figures (174/118/74
as odds increases; 3.3%→5.5% as rates) unless you specifically want the working-paper framing.** Do not mix
them.

### 2.3 Other districts and states with before-and-after counts — what I found and what I could not

**This is the weakest part of this document and I want to be explicit about it.** I did not find a second
peer-reviewed natural experiment with published before-and-after identification counts under a
constant-standards universal screening change. What I found instead:

**(a) A statewide policy change with a modelled effect — Florida "Plan B."** Reported inside Peters & Johnson
(2024) summarizing McBee, Peters & Waterman (2012): "Starting in 2002, Florida school districts were allowed
to propose alternative identification pathways for students from low-income families or who were classified
as ELs. Up until that point, students needed to score two standard deviations above the mean on an
individually administered intelligence test to be identified for GT. McBee et al. found that **if a randomly
selected Florida school district were to adopt one of these alternative pathways, the identification rate for
students from low-income families would more than double. For Black students, the increase was by
two-thirds**, even though the Plan B policies would not apply to all Black students—only if they were also
ELs or from low-income families." **[MODELLED — "if a randomly selected district were to adopt."] I did not
retrieve McBee et al. (2012) and do not have its citation or DOI. Flagged.** Note also that this is a change
in *eligibility standard*, not a pure screening change — it is not the same experiment as Broward.

**(b) No state requires statewide universal screening (as of the source below).** Education Commission of the
States, *State and Federal Policy for Gifted and Talented Youth*
([ecs.org PDF](http://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-and-Federal-Policy-for-Gifted-and-Talented-Youth.pdf)),
states: "Universal screening has been shown to have a significant positive effect on the identification of
black and Latino gifted students. However, **Education Commission of the States is not aware of any states
that require statewide universal screening for gifted students.**" It notes Colorado's 2014 bill "required
the state to offset the costs to districts of conducting universal screening of all students by second
grade." **TIER: policy-organization report, undated in my retrieval; treat the "no state requires" claim as
possibly out of date. Flagged.**

**(c) District self-reports, which I label as vendor/press material and do not treat as evidence.** Riverside
Insights' CogAT case study on Richardson ISD (TX) claims that after universal screening plus local norms,
"nearly ten percent (9.88%) of 2nd grade students were identified as gifted and talented – an increase of
nearly 10 times as many 2nd graders identified." **TIER 5 — this is a test publisher's marketing case study
about its own product. No independent verification, no methods, no comparison group.** Similarly, *K-12 Dive*
reports Northwest ISD (TX) going from 89 identified second graders to 215 by third grade after universal
screening in second, and Charleston County (SC) seeing "three times as many kids" after universal retesting
in fourth grade. **TIER 5 — press interviews with district administrators.** I record these only so the
reader knows I looked; none of them should be cited as evidence.

**(d) State-policy correlation, not a before-and-after.** Peters & Johnson (2024) find that state mandates,
required GT plans, and compliance audits correlate with higher service availability and higher EL/SwD
representation — see §4.3 on Texas. This is cross-sectional correlation across states, not a
before-and-after within one system.

## Q3 — The referral bottleneck

### 3.1 McBee, Peters & Miller (2016) — the modelled cost of requiring a nomination

**Source.** McBee, M. T., Peters, S. J., & Miller, E. M. (2016). "The Impact of the Nomination Stage on
Gifted Program Identification: A Comprehensive Psychometric Analysis." *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 60(4),
258–278. DOI [10.1177/0016986216656256](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256).
**Crossref returned:** title "The Impact of the Nomination Stage on Gifted Program Identification"; journal
*Gifted Child Quarterly*; issued 2016-07-27; authors McBee, Matthew T. / Peters, Scott J. / Miller, Erin M.;
60(4):258–278. **MATCH.** ERIC record EJ1116527. Published PDF read in full at
[library.scottbarrykaufman.com](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2016/07/Gifted-Child-Quarterly-2016-McBee-0016986216656256.pdf).

**SAMPLE — THIS IS THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION. [MODELLED]** There are **no children in this study.** It is a
psychometric simulation: numerical integration of a 4-variable multivariate normal distribution (nomination
true score, nomination observed score, confirmatory test true score, confirmatory test observed score), done
in R with `sadmvn` from the `mnormt` library. The inputs are reliability coefficients, validity coefficients,
and cutoffs. Reliability and validity values are taken from published test manuals (SIGS, GRS-S), which were
normed on real children, but every sensitivity number reported below is a **modelled** quantity, not a count
of missed children. The authors' full R script is printed in the article's appendix.

**BASELINE — no nomination stage at all.** With confirmatory test reliability = .95 (which they call
"ambitious but also achievable") and cutoff at the 90th percentile, sensitivity = **.843**. In their words:
"The optimal performance of a single-assessment system under optimistic, but potentially realistic,
conditions (i.e., the reliability ρxx = .95) is that only about 84% of qualifying students are actually

**AUTHORS' STATED FALSE-NEGATIVE CLAIMS, quoted exactly.**

- Abstract: "Results showed that in nearly all conditions, identification systems that require a nomination
  before testing result in a large proportion of gifted students being missed. **Under commonly implemented
  conditions, the nomination stage can cause the false negative rate to easily exceed 60%.**"
- On typical teacher behavior: "We find this alarming as we believe that teachers often only nominate those
  students that they perceive to be 'truly' or extremely gifted... In this case, the system sensitivity can
  easily fall into the 30% range, **meaning that 70% of qualifying students—those who the system was
  explicitly designed to find—do not gain access to the program.**"
- On the field as a whole: "Given that the use of nomination stages is extremely common in gifted education,
  and that these issues have not been widely understood by practitioners in the field, **we find it likely
  that most identification systems that have incorporated a nomination stage have not performed well and, in
  fact, have missed larger percentages of gifted students then they have actually successfully identified.**"
- On the whole enterprise: "in this article we use quantitative techniques to ask, 'how well do these
  identification systems work?' Our answer, briefly summarized, is 'probably not very well.'"
- Summary of practice: "Currently implemented nomination practices likely result in very few false positive
  identification decisions (meaning few students are identified 'on accident') at the cost of a tremendous
  numbers of false negatives (many students are missed who should have been identified)."

**THE STRUCTURAL POINT (why a referral gate is not symmetric).** "screeners can only reduce the sensitivity
of the integrated assessment system. The inclusion of a screening or nomination phase can only result in more
gifted students being missed than if the screener was skipped and all students in the population were given
the confirmatory test(s), regardless of the quality of the screening tool or procedure used." And: "the
confirmatory assessment can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives because those students
that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory test."

Also stated as a design principle: "Above all, screening tests require very high sensitivity, because any
false negatives that occur at this stage will not have a chance to be corrected—if a student who needs gifted
services is not passed through the screening or nomination phase to the Phase-II confirmatory assessment, she
has no chance of being served."

### 6.1 Lohman's NRC/GT monograph — agreement between two good tests at a high cutoff

**THE CORE STATEMENT, quoted in full.** "respected tests give different ability scores for the same child,
one saying that the child is gifted and the other reporting a lower score, many would dismiss one
outcome—usually the lower score. **But such disagreement between tests is the rule, not the exception.**
Suppose we define gifted as scoring in the top 3% of the distribution of intelligence. We administer two of
the best individual intelligence tests—the Stanford-Binet V and the WISC-IV—to each student. Full-scale IQ
scores on these tests correlate about **r = .84** (Roid, 2003). This means, however, that **only about half
of the students who score in the top 3% on one test will also score in the top 3% on the second test**
(Lohman & Korb, in press). **If the interval between test administrations is longer than a few weeks, then
even fewer would merit the label gifted on both tests.**"

**THE FIGURE 4 RESULT — agreement as a function of correlation and cutoff. [MODELLED]** "The correlation
between two tests is shown on the abscissa (x axis) of the figure. The correlation varies from r = .5 to

of the distribution on one test who could also be in the top 1% of the distribution for the second test. The

His summary of the practical case: "Figure 4 shows clearly that there will be considerable disagreement in
classification among different tests unless the correlation is very high and the cut score is very low.
Neither of these conditions typically applies. For example, **the correlations among total scores (i.e., Full
Scale IQ scores) on individually administered ability tests range from r = .68 to r = .85. A common criterion
is scoring in the top 3% of the distribution. Figure 4 shows that this means only about one-third to one-half
of the students would be expected to score in the top 3% on both tests.** Scores for shorter tests (e.g.,
Verbal IQs) show lower correlations and would agree even less well."

**PARALLEL FORMS OF THE SAME TEST — the ceiling case. [MODELLED]** "The reliability coefficients of parallel
forms rarely exceed r = .90. Even with this degree of reliability, Figure 4 shows that **only 60% of the
students who are tested would score in the top 3% on both tests.**"

**THE MEASURED EXAMPLE — which students a test actually finds. [MEASURED]** Predicting "the best readers"
from various instruments: "Surely we would expect to identify most of the top readers using the ITBS
Composite. However, Figure 4 shows that we would probably only get about **60%** of them. This is not what
most people would expect for two variables that correlate **r = .91**. Using the CogAT Verbal Battery, we
would identify **47%** of the best readers. The CogAT Composite would get **44%**. And the Nonverbal Battery
would identify only **28%** of the best readers. Clearly, different measures do not identify the same students
in spite of the fact that the tests are highly correlated. **Indeed, even very high correlations imply far
less agreement between scores than most people think, especially for extreme scores.**"

Lohman's own footnote on that example, quoted because it is a stated limitation on his own model: "These
observed results are reported in greater detail in Lohman (in press-a). **They show less agreement than
predicted. This is because scores on achievement tests are often not normally distributed.**" That is, the
real data are *worse* than the bivariate-normal model implies.

**Nonverbal tests find different children.** "Approximately 30 to 40% of U.S. students from all ethnic
backgrounds show approximately the same level of reasoning abilities on nonverbal tests as on verbal or
quantitative reasoning tests." And: "nonverbal reasoning abilities are as different from verbal reasoning as
a test of physical fitness is from a test of basketball or ballet skills... And as with these psychomotor
domains, **the differences are most obvious at the extremes of the distribution.** Furthermore, nonverbal
reasoning tests do not identify the same students as tests of verbal or quantitative reasoning abilities...
In other words, **the assumption that all measures that load highly on G are exchangeable as selection tests
is simply false.**"

**REGRESSION TO THE MEAN AT THE TAIL — the mechanism.** "no matter where we set the cut score on the upper
tail of the score distribution, **many more students will be near that cut score than far above it.** ...
When the scores of these students regress towards the mean on retest—**and on average they always
regress**—many will fall below the cut, and the scores of an equally large group of students who previously
failed to make the cut will now rise above it."

**"AND" vs "OR" vs "AVERAGE" — Lohman's version.** "the shift between a rule that admits a student on the
basis of a high score either on test 1 or on test 2 admits many more students than a rule that admits a
student on the basis of a high score on both test 1 and test 2. The intermediate position—take the average of
test 1 and test 2—admits yet a different group." His recommendation: "**The disjunctive 'or' rule is most
defensible if the two tests measure different constructs** such as language arts and mathematics. One should
seek to identify students who excel in either domain, not just those who excel in both domains. **If both
tests measure the same construct, however, the statistically optimal rule is neither 'or' nor 'and' but
rather 'average.'** ... Further, using two scores to estimate ability (as in either the 'and' or the 'average'
rule) substantially reduces regression effects."

**HIS STATED RECOMMENDATION.** "The implications of these results, then, are that **one should never base
decisions about admission on a single score.** Rather, one should use an average score of parallel [forms]."

### 6.4 The stacking problem

The three results above compound, and each source states its piece:

1. Even with **no gate at all**, a single test at the 90th percentile with reliability .95 has sensitivity
   .843; at the 99th percentile with reliability .90 it is .665 (McBee, Peters & Miller 2016, Table 1)
   **[MODELLED]**.
2. Requiring agreement across **two measures** at a high cutoff cuts the qualifying pool to roughly one-third
   to one-half even for two excellent IQ tests (Lohman 2005) **[MODELLED, with a measured example that is
   worse than the model]**.
3. Adding a **nomination gate** at a typical cutoff drops integrated sensitivity to .28–.45 (McBee, Peters &

4. And the classification is **unstable across years** regardless: about half of top-3% students in one year

responding, "the two most common were following a teacher referral (20 states) and following a parent
referral (19 states)."

And the NRC/GT survey, quoted inside the same paper: "parent or teacher nomination or referral is still a
common entry point in the identification process at the elementary school level" (Callahan, Moon & Oh, 2013,
p. 14).

### 7.3 Who does *not* initiate, and the discretion evidence

**Findings, quoted.** From the abstract: "We document that **even among students with high standardized test

### 7.4 What happens when the initiator is removed

The Card & Giuliano result in Q2 is the direct test: with standards held fixed and the initiation requirement
supplemented by an automatic screen, the identification rate roughly doubled overall (3.3% → 5.5%) and
tripled for disadvantaged students (1.4% → 4.3%). And **when the screen was withdrawn, the rate returned to
baseline** — the authors' Figure 1 shows "By 2011, the gifted share of third graders had returned to the
level of 2004–2005." That reversal is the cleanest available evidence that the constraint is structural
rather than informational: three cohorts of screening did not durably change parent or teacher referral
behavior.

Gubbins et al. (2020), as summarized inside Peters & Johnson (2024), found the same pattern in the schools
that *do* achieve proportional EL representation: "One of the first themes to emerge was that despite using
common assessments for identification, **all schools implemented universal screening in one or more grade
levels. This means that rather than rely on teacher referrals, the school proactively screened all
students.**" (16 schools across three states, all majority low-income, enrolling 384 to 1,747 students.)
**I did not retrieve Gubbins et al. (2020) directly — flagged.**

## Q8 — Do parents know?

**BOTTOM LINE, stated first because it is the most important finding in this section: I could not find a
single study that measures, on a population of children of known high ability, what proportion of their
parents recognize them as high-ability.** Every study I found that reports a "parent accuracy" number
recruits its sample from parents who had *already* suspected giftedness and *already* sought assessment.
Those studies estimate the **positive predictive value of parent suspicion** — "given that a parent brought
the child forward, how often were they right?" — which is the reverse of the question. They cannot tell you
the **sensitivity** of parent awareness: "given that a child is high-ability, how often does the parent
know?" That absence is itself a finding and I am reporting it as one.

### 8.1 What the existing "parent accuracy" studies actually measured

**(a) Louis & Lewis (1992).** Louis, B., & Lewis, M. "Parental Beliefs about Giftedness in Young Children and
Their Relation to Actual Ability Level." *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 36(1), 27–31. DOI
[10.1177/001698629203600107](https://doi.org/10.1177/001698629203600107).
**Crossref returned:** exact title, *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 1992, authors Louis / Lewis, 36(1):27–31.
**MATCH.**

**SAMPLE — THIS IS THE CRUX.** The study's own framing, from the abstract: "**Some parents believe their
preschool children to be gifted**, but little is known concerning these implicit beliefs." The sample is
preschool-age children whose parents came forward. Affiliation: Institute for the Study of Child Development,
University of Medicine and Dentistry of New Jersey. **Exact N is NOT verified — the article is paywalled and
I read only the abstract and secondary summaries. Flagged.**

**Findings, quoted from the abstract.** "Parental beliefs were found to be associated with actual IQ status.
In particular, parents' beliefs about memory, creativity-imagination, and abstract thinking abilities were

The widely cited "61%" figure is **not** in the abstract. It appears in secondary sources, e.g. the *He kupu*

**(d) The literature-wide range, as characterized by a peer-reviewed review.** Hodge, K. A., & Kemp, C. R.

Children," *Journal for the Education of the Gifted*, 30(2), 164–204, DOI

Their summary, quoted: "**The effectiveness of parents in identifying intellectual giftedness in their young
children has been shown in a number of studies, with samples ranging from 21 to more than 500, to be between
50% and 96% for IQs above 125** (Louis & Lewis, 1992; McGuffog, Feiring, & Lewis, 1987; Parkinson, 1990;
Roedell et al., 1980; Silverman, Chitwood, & Waters, 1986)."

**Their own stated caveat, quoted, which limits every number above.** "However, **the use of IQ scores as the
sole criterion for giftedness in young children in these studies is problematic because it has been shown
that IQ scores are more reliable after the age of 6 than at a younger age** (McCall, Appelbaum, & Hogarty,
1973; Wilson, 1983)."

**SAMPLE of Hodge & Kemp's own study, which shows the selection problem in miniature. [MEASURED]** "Child
participants were a **convenience sample of 6 males and 8 females** who had been identified by their parents
or preschool teachers as potentially gifted prior to or during attendance at two preschool programs operating
at the Special Education Centre at Macquarie University, Sydney, Australia." Followed for up to 3 years of
early schooling; 26 of 33 eligible teachers participated. **N = 14 children.** The parents: "All children
came from suburbs considered middle class. Eleven were from Anglo-Australian backgrounds, and 3 were from
Asian backgrounds. Their parents were **well educated: 20 parents had completed university education, 7 had
completed other tertiary education, and 1 had completed secondary education.**"

That is: 27 of 28 parents had tertiary education, all families middle class, and every child entered the
study because a parent or preschool teacher had already flagged them. **This is a study of the most
advantaged, most aware end of the parent distribution.** It cannot speak to whether parents in general
recognize high ability.

**(e) A 2021 US parent survey — same structural problem.** Mollenkopf, D. L., Matyo-Cepero, J., Lewis, J. D.,
Irwin, B. A., & Joy, J. (2021). "Testing, Identifying, and Serving Gifted Children With and Without
Disabilities: A Multi-State Parental Perspective." *Gifted Child Today*, 44(2), 83–92. DOI
[10.1177/1076217520986589](https://doi.org/10.1177/1076217520986589).
**Crossref returned:** exact title, *Gifted Child Today*, 2021, authors Mollenkopf / Matyo-Cepero / Lewis /
Irwin / Joy, 44(2):83–92. **MATCH.** Paywalled; I read the abstract only.

**SAMPLE.** "**Parents of gifted children** completed a survey." Multi-state, US. **N, states, recruitment
method, and response rate are NOT verified. Flagged.**

**Findings, quoted from the abstract.** "Schools tested, identified, and started services for gifted children
in the early elementary grades, usually between **5 and 6 years of age**. **Most parents identified giftedness
in their children by age 2. Parents were also the most frequent person to refer their child for testing**,
although teachers also played a strong role. Twice-exceptional children did not differ significantly from
gifted children on any of these measures."

Note what this does and does not establish. Among parents whose children were eventually identified, most
suspected very early and most were the ones who initiated. It says nothing about parents whose children were
never identified. It is a survey of the survivors of the process.

The authors' own framing of the gap, quoted: "Early identification of gifted children, including those
twice-exceptional, allows schools and parents to support these children's needs, but **little is known about
this early identification process and the role parents and teachers play.**"

**(f) A recent large-N study from outside the US.** Alfaiz, F. S. (2026). "The accuracy of parent and teacher

[10.1080/2331186x.2026.2654922](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186x.2026.2654922).

**Findings, quoted from the abstract.** "**parent nominations yielded a significantly higher accuracy rate
(39.6%) than teacher nominations (31.9%)**, with a statistically significant mean difference of 7.7

accurate in nominating males, whereas teachers were significantly more accurate in nominating females.**
Furthermore, a strong grade-level effect was observed, with **nomination accuracy for both sources more than

criterion — meaning **60.4% of parent nominations were wrong in the false-positive direction.** It says
nothing about children no parent nominated. Note also this is Saudi Arabia, so transfer to the US context is
not automatic.

### 8.2 The one relevant number that is not conditioned on parent suspicion

Marsili & Pellegrini (2022) — full detail in §5.1 — pool 27 effect sizes on parent nomination and report
**ES *r* = .30** between parent nomination and traditional measures, statistically indistinguishable from
teacher nomination (.31), self (.33), and peer (.27). Because the pooled studies include designs where
nominations were collected on whole classes rather than only from volunteering parents, this is closer to a
correlational estimate of parent judgment quality than the accuracy studies above. It is still a correlation,
not a hit rate, and the prediction interval on the overall effect was [−.12, .79].

An *r* of .30 means parent judgment shares about 9% of its variance with the measure the school will
actually use.

### 8.3 Evidence on how families discover a child is gifted

Thin, and mostly indirect.

- **Most identified children were initiated by a parent, and most of those parents suspected by age 2**
  (Mollenkopf et al. 2021, §8.1(e)). Survivorship sample.
- **Very few children overall get a parent referral.** In Georgia, parent referrals reached 0.4% of
  elementary students and produced 3.0% of identified students (McBee 2006, §7.1). Among low-income students
  the parent-referral rate was 0.13%.
- **Teachers rarely initiate the formal assessment even for children later found gifted.** Hodge & Kemp
  (2006), citing Alsop (1997): an "investigation into the counseling needs of parents of **47 gifted
  children** found that **the child's teacher had recommended a formal assessment in only 17% of the
  cases.**" **Alsop (1997) not retrieved — flagged.**
- **When parents do raise it, schools often push back.** Hodge & Kemp (2006), citing the same Alsop sample:
  "only **25% of classroom teachers and 29% of school principals were supportive** when consulted about the
  child's assessment for giftedness." And they report Australian teachers' priors, citing Plunkett (2000b):
  "more of the Australian teachers interviewed by Plunkett (2000b) perceived that **parents often
  overestimated their child as gifted** than thought parents were correct in identifying giftedness (50% vs.
  36%)."
- **Parents in the Hodge & Kemp sample consistently judged that teachers underestimated their children.**
  Their measured distribution of parent ratings of teacher recognition: "1 (greatly underestimates): 3%, 2
  (underestimates a little): 51%, and 3 (accurately recognizes): 36%... **No parent rated teacher recognition
  as 4 (overestimates a little) or 5 (greatly overestimates).**" [N = 14 children, so this is 14 families'
  ratings across 3 years — treat as illustrative, not as an estimate.]
- **Even among these highly educated parents, a quarter did not tell the school.** "When enrolling their child
  in school for the first time or if changing schools, **75% of parents informed the school that their child
  might be gifted or that the child could already read, while 25% did not.**"
- **Purchase is a discovery pathway for those who can afford it.** Card & Giuliano's SI (§4.5): a "thriving
  private market for IQ testing," $500–$1,000 per evaluation in 2013, through which 20% of non-disadvantaged
  Broward students qualified — versus under 1% of disadvantaged students.

### 8.4 The explicit statement of what does not exist

To be unambiguous about the gap, here is what I searched for and did not find:

1. **No population-representative survey asking parents whether they believe their child is academically
   gifted, paired with a test score for that child.** Every accuracy estimate I found is conditioned on the
   parent having already come forward.
2. **No study reporting the sensitivity of parent recognition** — i.e., of children scoring above some
   threshold, what fraction have a parent who identifies them as high-ability. Only precision/PPV figures
   exist (39.6% in Saudi Arabia; 50–96% in the small early-childhood literature).
3. **No study of parents of *unidentified* high-ability children.** By construction these families are hard
   to sample, which is presumably why the literature does not contain one. The Card & Giuliano compliers are
   the closest available proxy: ~1,300 additional children per year in one district whose ability was
   documented only because a test was pushed at them, 79% of whom were low-income or English learners, and
   71% of whose parents did not primarily speak English.
4. **No US study measuring what proportion of families know their district has a gifted program, know it has
   an application, or know when the window is.** I found only qualitative claims (e.g. a WKU master's thesis
   summarizing that "many families do not know about gifted services or may feel intimidated about discussing
   educational issues with teachers and schools," citing Chrispeels & Rivero 2001 and Harris et al. 2009).
   **That is a graduate thesis citing other work; I treat it as a pointer, not evidence.**

**Why this matters for the mechanism claim.** The claim "aware parents get their children identified and
unaware parents do not" has two halves. The *second* half — that families who do not initiate do not get
identified — is well established (McBee 2006; Card & Giuliano 2016). The *first* half — that awareness
varies across families in a way that is not just a proxy for income or language — is **not** directly
measured by anything I found. The closest evidence is that parent-referral rates differ 5× by lunch status
and that identification rates differ ~8× by the combination of income, language and race. But those data
cannot separate "did not know" from "knew and could not act."

    any states that require statewide universal screening," but that brief is undated in my retrieval and may
    be superseded.

15. **Any second peer-reviewed district-level natural experiment comparable to Broward.** The Richardson ISD
    "nearly tenfold" increase is a test publisher's marketing case study; the Northwest ISD and Charleston
    County figures are press interviews with administrators. None has methods, a comparison group, or
    independent verification. **None should be cited as evidence.**

16. **The NSF-hosted manuscript** at par.nsf.gov/10340580, from which the "42% of American schools identified
    zero students" restatement and the state-level zero-identification figures in §4.6 are drawn. I read the
    text but could not establish its published citation, journal, or DOI.

17. **The 2011–12 OCR State/National Estimations table** cited by Card & Giuliano for the 7.6% / 3.6% / 4.6%
    / 1.8% national participation rates. I quoted their rendering rather than pulling the federal table
    myself; the ocrdata.ed.gov URL structure has changed since 2016.

18. **THE BIG ONE — any study measuring whether parents of high-ability children know their child is
    high-ability.** Specifically: no population-representative parent survey paired with child test scores; no
    sensitivity estimate for parent recognition (only precision/PPV); no study of parents of *unidentified*
    high-ability children; and no US measurement of what fraction of families know their district has a gifted
    program or how to enter it. **I searched for this from several directions and I do not believe it exists.
    Every "parent accuracy" number in the literature is conditioned on the parent having already come
    forward, which makes it structurally incapable of answering the question.** This absence is stated at
    length in §8 and is, in my judgment, the most consequential finding in this document.
