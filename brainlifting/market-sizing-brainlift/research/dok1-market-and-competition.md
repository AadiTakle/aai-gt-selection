# DOK 1 — The Market for Education Aimed at Academically Gifted Children, and Who Competes for It

**Research question.** For a tuition-charging private school for highly able children targeting 10,000 enrolled
students, using a technology-mediated accelerated model across a physical campus and an online campus: what
does the realistic addressable market look like, and who else is competing for it?

Fact extraction only. No interpretation, no editorializing. Every number and every quoted phrase below was read
out of the source named beside it. Exact quotes are in quotation marks. Anything I could not verify is flagged
inline **and** listed again in the final section.

Every DOI cited in this document was resolved against the Crossref REST API
(`https://api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>`) and the returned title, container-title, year and author list were
compared against the citation as written. DOIs that did not match were discarded, not "fixed." Every

## Source tier labels

Every source below carries one of these tags:

- **TIER 1 — Federal administrative data.** NCES (Private School Universe Survey, Common Core of Data, Digest of
  Education Statistics, NHES/PFI), US Census Bureau. Censuses or federally mandated collections.
- **TIER 2 — Peer-reviewed research.** Journal articles with DOIs, resolved against Crossref.
- **TIER 3 — Audited institutional filing / official governing-body report.** SEC filings, IRS Form 990,
  official school governing board minutes, state department of education reports.
- **TIER 4 — Research center / technical report / non-federal survey.** NRC/GT, NEPC, NBER working papers,
  National Microschooling Center. Not peer reviewed but methods are documented.
- **TIER 5 — Advocacy self-report, trade press, or the institution's own marketing.** NAGC, a school reporting
  its own enrollment on its own website or school profile, a news article. Used only where no better source
  exists, and always labeled as such.

**A note on school-reported enrollment.** School profiles and "at a glance" PDFs are published by the school
for admissions and college-counseling purposes. They are **TIER 5 (institution's own marketing)** even when
they look official. Where a federal figure exists for the same school (NCES PSS for private schools, NCES CCD
for public schools), I give both and note the gap.

## 1. Where identified gifted children currently go to school

### 1.1 The headline finding: there is no federal count of gifted students by service-delivery model

**This is the single largest evidentiary hole in this document.** I searched NCES, the Civil Rights Data
Collection, and the NAGC/CSDPG *State of the States* series for a national figure answering "what share of
gifted-identified students are served in a separate school versus a pull-out versus in-class differentiation."
**No such national student-level distribution is published.** The CRDC collects headcounts of students
*enrolled in* gifted and talented programs but does not classify the delivery model. NAGC collects
delivery-model data at the **state** level, not the student level (§1.2). Anyone citing a percentage of gifted
students in separate schools is either using a state or district figure or estimating. See item 1 in the
final "could not verify" list.

### 1.3 The best evidence on what "served in the regular classroom" actually amounts to

This is the classic pair of studies, both from the federally funded National Research Center on the Gifted and
Talented, and both still the most-cited source on the question.

**Source (peer-reviewed).** Westberg, K. L., Archambault, F. X., Jr., Dobyns, S. M., & Salvin, T. J. (1993).
"The Classroom Practices Observation Study." *Journal for the Education of the Gifted*, 16(2), 120–146. DOI
[10.1177/016235329301600204](https://doi.org/10.1177/016235329301600204). **TIER 2 — peer-reviewed.**
**Crossref-verified:** title, journal, year 1993, volume 16, issue 2, pages 120–146 and all four authors match.

From the abstract, verbatim:

> "The results indicated little differentiation in the instructional and curricular practices, grouping
> arrangements, and verbal interactions for gifted and talented students in the regular classroom. Across five
> subject areas and 92 observation days, the observed gifted and talented students experienced **no
> instructional or curricular differentiation in 84% of their instructional activities.**"

The companion NRC/GT research monograph reporting the same study adds a second figure, verbatim:

> "Across 5 subject areas and 92 observation days, gifted students received instruction in **homogeneous groups
> only 21 percent of the time,** and the target gifted and talented or high ability students experienced no
> instructional or curricular differentiation in 84 percent of the instructional activities in which they
> participated."

Monograph: Westberg, Archambault, Dobyns & Salvin (1993), *An Observational Study of Instructional and
Curricular Practices Used with Gifted and Talented Students in Regular Classrooms* (Research Monograph 93104),
NRC/GT. ERIC [ED379846](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED379846.pdf). **TIER 4 — federally funded research
center technical report.** **Sample size: 46 third- and fourth-grade classrooms.**

**Source (the national survey half of the same project).** Archambault, F. X., Jr., et al. (1993). *Regular
Classroom Practices with Gifted Students: Results of a National Survey of Classroom Teachers* (Research
Monograph 93102), NRC/GT. ERIC [ED379845](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED379845.pdf). **TIER 4.**
**No DOI is registered in Crossref for this monograph** — I queried Crossref by title and the returned records
were different papers, so I cite the ERIC document number instead of inventing a DOI.

Sample sizes and finding, verbatim from the abstract:

> "Survey samples were third and fourth grade teachers, including a general sample of teachers in public schools
> (n=3993); teachers in private schools (n=980)... The major finding was that third and fourth grade teachers in
> all settings **make only minor modifications in the regular curriculum to meet the needs of gifted students.**
> This result holds for public school teachers, for private school teachers... **There were also few
> differences in regular classroom services between schools with and without formal gifted programs.**"

**Flag on age.** Both studies are from **1993**. They are 33 years old. I could not find a comparably designed
national observational replication. See item 2 in the final list.

### 3.2 Price elasticity of demand for private schooling — the single most decision-relevant finding here

**Source.** Dynarski, S., Gruber, J., & Li, D. (2009). *Cheaper By the Dozen: Using Sibling Discounts at
Catholic Schools to Estimate the Price Elasticity of Private School Attendance.* NBER Working Paper 15461. DOI
[10.3386/w15461](https://doi.org/10.3386/w15461). **TIER 4 — NBER working paper (not peer reviewed).**
**Crossref-verified:** title, October 2009, type "report," authors Dynarski, Gruber, Li all match. A Census
Center for Economic Studies version is at
[CES-WP-11-34](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2011/CES-WP-11-34.pdf).

Method: exploits sibling discounts at Catholic elementary schools as within-neighborhood tuition variation;
data on **over 3,700 Catholic school tuition schedules** matched to restricted Census data.

Headline result, verbatim from the abstract:

> "We find that a standard deviation decrease in tuition prices increases the probability that a family will
> send its children to private school by one half percentage point, which translates into **an elasticity of
> Catholic school attendance with respect to tuition costs of -0.19.**"

**The subgroup result is the one that matters for a high-tuition school,** verbatim:

> "Our average effect masks substantial heterogeneity in the response to price. **Families with lower levels of
> parental education are about over four times as price elastic than other families. The price elasticity of
> private school attendance drops monotonically with income; it is -0.44 in the bottom tercile but near zero in
> the top tercile.**"

**Corroborating estimate.** Hungerman, D. M., & Rinz, K. (2015). *Where Does Voucher Funding Go? How
Large-Scale Subsidy Programs Affect Private-School Revenue, Enrollment, and Prices.* NBER Working Paper 21687.
DOI [10.3386/w21687](https://doi.org/10.3386/w21687). **TIER 4.** **Crossref-verified:** title, October 2015,
authors Hungerman and Rinz match. Built from a "new dataset constructed from nonprofit tax-returns." Verbatim:

> "Our results suggest **a price elasticity of -0.52;** this is slightly larger in absolute value than the
> baseline estimate in Dynarski, Gruber, and Li (2009), although one could argue that the population they study
> (families with siblings already in private school) would be less price sensitive than the population affected
> by publicly-funded subsidies that we consider here."

**Older peer-reviewed estimate, for range.** Jud, G. D. (1983). "Tuition Tax Credits and the Market for Private
Schooling in North Carolina." *Review of Regional Studies*. DOI
[10.52324/001c.9766](https://doi.org/10.52324/001c.9766). **TIER 2 — peer-reviewed. Crossref-verified:**
title, journal, 1983, author Jud. From a 1980 survey of NC private schools: "The price elasticity was estimated
to be **-0.52** and the income elasticity to be **0.85.** The market supply of private schooling was found to be
quite elastic: the elasticity of supply was estimated to be 5.88." The same paper reports two earlier
estimates it is comparing against: Erekson (1982) found tuition "had no effect at all on enrollment" for
parochial schools and **-0.21** for nonparochial private schools; Frey (1982) reported **-0.4**.

**Summary of the published range: -0.19 to -0.52 on average, near zero in the top income tercile.** Every
estimate above is in the inelastic range (|ε| < 1).

**Flag — all four of these studies price *Catholic and general* private schooling, not selective or gifted
schooling, and the two most rigorous are working papers, not peer-reviewed articles. I found no published price
elasticity estimate specific to gifted, selective, or online K-12 education.** See item 4 in the final list.

> "With a current market share at approximately **2 percent of students nationally**..."

Corroborated in trade press (TIER 5): *The 74* reports the same figures and adds the year-over-year change —
"In 2024, the median number of students in a typical microschool was **16.** That figure has jumped to **22**...
**Some now serve as many as 100 students**"
([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/exclusive-report-as-movement-grows-microschools-arent-so-micro-anymore/));

**The 750,000 figure is a RAND estimate quoted by a sector advocacy body, with a stated range up to 2.1
million — a 2.8x spread. It is not a count.** The one hard number in the sector is the median school size:

## 8. What families of gifted children say they want

### 8.1 What parents choosing a school say is "very important" — federal survey

**Source (TIER 1, federal).** NCES, PFI NHES:2023, **Table A-4**, "Among students enrolled in kindergarten
through grade 12 whose parents considered other schools for them, percentage whose parents rated various
factors used to select the school as 'very important'": 2022–23.
[Report PDF](https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2024/2024113.pdf).

Verbatim from Selected Findings:

> "For students who were not homeschooled and whose parents had considered other schools for the student, the
> factors most frequently rated as 'very important' when choosing a school were the **quality of teachers,
> principal or other staff at the school (77 percent); safety, which includes student discipline (74 percent);
> and curriculum focus or unique academic programs (57 percent)** (table A-4)."

**Universe note, quoted from the table's own note:** "Students who were homeschooled full or part-time and
students whose parents did not consider other schools were excluded from the table, resulting in a weighted
population of approximately **18,310,000** students. **The parents of 36 percent of students reported that they
considered other schools.**"

The full factor list parents were asked to rate (from Appendix C of the same report): safety including student
discipline; curriculum focus or unique academic programs; academic performance of students; number of students
in class; student body characteristics; extracurricular options; convenient location; **cost**; quality of
teachers, principal or other school staff; quality or availability of special education; special facilities;
and religious orientation of the school.

**Flag: this is the general school-choosing population, not parents of gifted children. NCES does not publish
Table A-4 broken out by gifted identification.**

### 8.2 The one poll that asks directly about a separate school versus an online school for the gifted

**This is the most directly decision-relevant survey evidence I found, and it cuts against a brick-and-mortar
campus.**

**Source.** Institute for Educational Advancement, *Americans Agree: Public Attitudes Towards Gifted Education*
(© 2019), reporting the **IEA-P** poll. Chapter 4, "Public Support for Specific Program Provisions In Gifted
Education"
([web chapter](https://ieap.educationaladvancement.org/public-support-specific-program-provisions-gifted-education))
· [Full report PDF](https://educationaladvancement.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/IEA-P-Full-Report-Web-1.pdf)
· [Poll methods](https://ieap.educationaladvancement.org/poll-methods-and-data).
**TIER 5 — advocacy organization poll,** though executed by two independent bipartisan polling firms.

**Methodology, stated exactly:** "The Benenson Strategy Group conducted **1414** 25-minute online interviews
using the iModerate™ software **between December 19, 2016 and January 6, 2017**," with **registered voters**
nationwide, including oversamples of **150 Opinion Elites, 150 Parents, 150 Hispanics, and 150 African
Americans.** "At the 95% confidence level, **the margin of error for the entire sample is ± 2.51%**... **±3.73%
among Parents.**" Conducted by the Benenson Strategy Group and The Winston Group; funded by IEA with support
from The Lynde and Harry Bradley Foundation.

**Flag on method:** respondents "were members of **online panel groups**" who "**'opt-in'** to receive
invitations." This is an opt-in online panel, not a probability sample, notwithstanding the report's use of the
phrase "random sample." **The data is now nine years old (fielded December 2016).**

**The result, verbatim:**

> "Among all IEA-P respondents, **75% responded favorably to establishing online schools for the gifted, and
> 27% offered strong support. The concept of establishing separate brick-and-mortar schools for gifted students
> received a tepid response relative to other program options, with support from just half of respondents
> (49%). The rate of support was at least 25% lower than the other four program provisions and 30% lower than
> acceleration.**"

| IEA-P program provision | Total support | Strong support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Allowing gifted students to **accelerate** | **87%** | 36% |
| Requiring teacher training for gifted education | 86% | — |
| Funding programs in underserved areas | 86% | 35% |
| **Creating online schools for the gifted** | **75%** | **27%** |
| **Creating separate brick-and-mortar schools for the gifted** | **49%** | — |

**Broken out by group, verbatim:** "Support for the concept of an online school for the gifted was equally high
among Opinion Elites and Parents (**77% and 80%**, respectively). Fewer supported a brick-and-mortar school...
**Only 53% of Opinion Elites and 56% of Parents supported the creation of a separate school for the gifted.
Parents were far more likely to offer strong support for an online school (32%) than they were for a separate
school for the gifted (21%).**"

**On acceleration versus enrichment,** the report's finding on who resists acceleration, verbatim:

> "**76% of IEA-P respondents indicated concern that gifted students do not have opportunities to
> accelerate.**"

> "Paradoxically, trends in the IEA-P suggest **the group most hesitant about acceleration are educators.**
> While the number of educators in the IEA-P was too small to generalize, the teachers and administrators who
> participated in the poll were least likely of any group to lend strong support to acceleration: **25% of this
> group of educators reported strong support for acceleration compared to 45% of Opinion Elites, and 41% of
> Black respondents.**"

On the acceleration-favoring subgroup: "Support from **Parents** matched other subgroups, with **39%** offering
strong support and **48%** responding they 'Somewhat Support' acceleration."

**Flag: the IEA-P asked about "Parents," meaning parents of school-aged children generally. The report
separately notes that its "Parents With Gifted Child" cut "includes any member of the population identifying as
having a gifted kid, regardless of that child's age" — i.e. self-identified, not identification-verified.**

### 8.3 The gap: parent attitudes toward technology-mediated or self-paced instruction

**I could not find a survey, peer-reviewed or otherwise, measuring attitudes of parents of gifted children
toward technology-mediated, AI-mediated, mastery-based, or self-paced instruction.** The closest verified
proxies are:

- The IEA-P's 75% support for **online** schools for the gifted (§8.2) — which measures modality, not

- The NAGC *State of the States* delivery-model lists (§1.2), where "**continuous progress / self-paced

- NCES PFI Table A-4 (§8.1), in which "**curriculum focus or unique academic programs**" was rated very

See items 18 and 19 in the final list.

## 9. The economics of selective programs shutting down

### 9.1 Duke Talent Identification Program (Duke TIP) — closed 2020

**The fact and date.** Duke University announced the closure of Duke TIP in a message to the public dated
**Thursday, October 8, 2020**; layoff notices for **75 employees** were filed publicly, with layoffs "expected
to begin on **Jan. 6**" [2021].

**Source (TIER 5, trade/local press quoting the university's chief communications officer).** ABC11
Raleigh-Durham, published **Friday, October 9, 2020**
([article](https://abc11.com/post/duke-university-layoffs-tip-college-covid/6874746/)). Verbatim, attributed to
Michael Schoenfeld, chief communications officer:

> "**This is, unfortunately, a direct result of COVID-19, which forced the cancellation of the TIP residential
> summer session on the Duke campus and other colleges in 2020 and likely again in 2021 and thus created an
> unsustainable financial position for the program.**"

The article records: "The layoff specifically targets the Duke University Talent Identification Program (Duke
TIP) -- a **40-year-old** nonprofit organization... **Public documents show Duke University will be laying off
75 employees** by the start of the new year... The notice, issued on **Oct. 8**, says layoffs are expected to
begin on **Jan. 6.**"

**Duke's own stated reasons (TIER 5, archived Duke TIP announcement).** From the announcement text, preserved
at [archive.tipwiki.net](https://archive.tipwiki.net/A/End_of_Tip.html) and reproduced identically at
[Transformative Tutoring](https://transformativetutoring.com/duke-tip-talent-search-discontinues-use-of-sat-act/):

> "The pandemic-induced closure of Duke TIP's Summer Studies residential programs on our main campus in Durham
> and at colleges across the country in 2020 and likely again in 2021 **has fundamentally disrupted the business
> and financial models that have long supported the TIP organization.**"

And, in the announcement's own Q&A:

> "**First, COVID forced the closure of all Summer Sessions in 2020 and likely in 2021, which has had a
> disruptive impact on the business and financial models that have long sustained TIP. Without the revenue from
> Summer Session, TIP cannot continue operating in its current form.** Second, Duke is adapting to significant
> changes in the educational, technological and regulatory landscapes for precollege programs that have taken
> place in recent years."

**The talent search specifically was ended permanently, not paused.** Verbatim:

> "As we redesign our precollege and talented offerings, **we will not resume the talent search with above-level
> testing,** but will instead be looking at new ways to identify students and facilitate their access to these
> enrichment programs."

**Scale of what closed.** From the same announcement: TIP had made "such a transformative resource for **more
than three million students over the past 40 years.**"

**What replaced it (TIER 5, archived Duke TIP homepage,
[Wayback Machine capture 2022-01-20](https://web.archive.org/web/20220120131040/https:/tip.duke.edu/)).**
Verbatim: "The pandemic also significantly disrupted the Duke TIP and Duke Youth Programs business models,
**which were based on providing a high-quality, campus-based, intensive residential experience to large numbers
of students,** and led us to make the difficult decision in 2020 to suspend these residential programs...
**all Duke pre-college programming has been consolidated within Duke Continuing Studies.**"

**Flag: Duke did not publish revenue, enrollment, or deficit figures for TIP in any of these announcements. The
phrase "unsustainable financial position" is the university's characterization; no audited number supporting it
was released.** See item 13 in the final list.

### 9.2 Johns Hopkins CTY — did not close, but its talent search shrank by roughly 80%

**This is arguably stronger evidence about market size than the Duke closure, because CTY survived and the
numbers are still down.**

**The 2005 baseline (TIER 2, peer-reviewed).** Barnett, L. B., Albert, M. E., & Brody, L. E. (2005). "The
Center for Talented Youth talent search and academic programs." *High Ability Studies*, 16(1), 27–40. DOI
[10.1080/13598130500115197](https://doi.org/10.1080/13598130500115197). **Crossref-verified:** title, journal,
June 2005, volume 16, issue 1, pages 27–40, authors Barnett / Albert / Brody all match. Verbatim from the
abstract:

> "**CTY now serves approximately 80,000 students each year through its talent search and various academic
> offerings.**"

And from the article body: "**CTY has assessed approximately 1.5 million students since its first talent search
was held in 1980**"; "Approximately **15,000 schools** have worked with CTY to inform strong candidates about
the talent search"; "In 2004 **9766 students** participated in the summer programs"; "**A total 117,043
students have participated in summer programs since their inception in 1980**"; "**CTY's talent search reaches
approximately 80,000 students in Grades 2–8 each year and over 10,000 students are served through a variety of
academic programs.**"

**The 2019-20 figure (TIER 5, CTY's own press release, January 2021).**
[Press release PDF](https://bbk12e1-cdn.myschoolcdn.com/ftpimages/27/misc/misc_238607.pdf), verbatim:

> "**More than 15,000 students in grades two through eight tested through CTY's Talent Search between July 1,
> 2019 and June 30, 2020,** representing all 50 states plus Washington D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin
> Islands, and Armed Forces installations in Europe and the Pacific, as well as more than 70 countries."

The same release gives program enrollment: "**Typically, there are more than 9,700 enrollments** by bright
pre-college students in CTY Summer Programs, held at two-dozen sites... Last year, there were **more than
20,600 enrollments in CTY Online Programs** courses and **more than 1,400 enrollments in CTY Family Academic
Programs.**"

**The comparison, stated as arithmetic on the two published figures: CTY talent search participation went from
"approximately 80,000 students each year" (2005, peer-reviewed) to "more than 15,000 students" tested in
2019-20 (CTY's own release). That is a decline of roughly 81%.**

**Flag on comparability: the 2005 figure is described as students "served... through its talent search and
various academic offerings" and separately as the talent search "reaching" ~80,000 in grades 2–8; the 2019-20
figure counts students who actually *tested*. "Reached" and "tested" are not necessarily the same measure, and
I could not find a CTY series that uses one consistent definition across both years.** Note also that CTY
summer program enrollment barely moved: 9,766 in 2004 versus "more than 9,700" typically as of 2021. See item
20 in the final list.

### 9.3 New York City gifted and talented — announced closure, then reversed and expanded

**Reported here because it is frequently cited as a gifted-program shutdown, and it is not one.**

**The announcement (TIER 5, wire and trade press), October 8, 2021.** Mayor Bill de Blasio announced a plan to
phase out NYC's Gifted and Talented program; the Associated Press
([AP](https://apnews.com/article/new-york-new-york-city-race-and-ethnicity-education-d027be86b7d202f1d4ef62e5c3255802))
reported: "Starting in the next school year, the city will stop giving 4-year-olds a screening test used to
identify gifted and talented students." Chalkbeat
([Chalkbeat, Oct 8 2021](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2021/10/8/22716211/gifted-talented-test-segregation-nyc-overhaul/))
reported the replacement program, Brilliant NYC, would "train roughly **4,000 teachers**." The *New York Post*
reported the program "admits roughly **2,500 kids per year**"
([NY Post](https://nypost.com/2021/10/08/nyc-to-phase-out-gifted-and-talented-program/)).

**The reversal (TIER 3, the city's own official announcement), April 14, 2022.** Mayor Eric Adams and
Chancellor David Banks announced an **expansion**, not a phase-out:
[NYC Mayor's Office release](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2022/04/mayor-adams-chancellor-banks-expansion-gifted-talented-programs-citywide).
Chancellor Banks, quoted in the city's own release: "**Today we move to end the era of scarcity — the era of
making families fight amongst themselves for limited Gifted and Talented seats in far off schools.**"

Per Chalkbeat's reporting of the same announcement
([Chalkbeat, Apr 14 2022](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2022/4/14/23024384/nyc-gifted-and-talented-programs-kindergarten-third-grade/)):
"the education department plans to add **1,000 new seats** across the city for gifted programs launching in
third grade... officials aim to add **100 seats** in kindergarten 'gifted and talented' classrooms so that
**every district will now have a gifted program** starting at that grade." Chalkbeat later reported "The city
expects to have [2],500 gifted seats for next year's rising kindergarten class"
([Chalkbeat, Nov 30 2022](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2022/11/30/23486915/nyc-gifted-talented-admission-timeline-change-kindergarten-applications/)).

**Net: the entrance exam for 4-year-olds was permanently removed; the separate gifted track was retained and
expanded by 1,100 seats. This is a change in identification method, not a closure.**

## What I searched for and could NOT verify

1. **A national, student-level distribution of gifted service-delivery models** — what share of the ~3.3 million
   gifted-identified US students sit in a separate school, a separate class, a pull-out, or in-class
   differentiation. **This number does not exist publicly.** CRDC counts gifted enrollment but not delivery
