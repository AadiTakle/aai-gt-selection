# BrainLift: Sizing the Market for a Selective School at Six Figures — How Many Children Could Qualify, How Few Are Known, and What Has Ever Reached That Scale

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose
This BrainLift asks whether a school selecting for high academic ability can reach one hundred thousand enrolled students, and if so from where. It separates four questions that get run together. How many children in the country would meet the bar. How many of those are known to anyone, including their own families. How large the population is that already knows and will act on it. And what any institution has ever actually achieved at this scale. The answers turn out to sit in different places, and the constraint that binds first is not the one most people reach for.

A note on vocabulary, since several terms below are technical and used only because no plain equivalent exists. A **percentile** is a rank: the 95th percentile means a child scored higher than 95 out of 100 children their age. **Sensitivity** means the share of children who genuinely qualify that a process actually finds, so a sensitivity of 30% means a process finds three of every ten qualifying children and misses seven. **Referral** or **nomination** means somebody, usually a teacher or a parent, puts a child forward to be tested; it is the step before testing, not the test. **Yield** is the share of students offered a place who actually enrol. A **stock** is how many people are in a state at one moment; a **flow** is how many enter it per year. Those two get confused constantly in this area and the confusion changes conclusions.

### In Scope
- The size of the qualifying population at each plausible bar, computed from published national enrollment.
- How many children are formally identified as academically gifted, how that number is changing, and how wildly it varies by place.
- Why identification processes miss most qualifying children, with the referral step treated as the main cause.
- Whether families of high-ability children know, and what has and has not been measured about that.
- The measured size of the population that already knows and will pay to act on it.
- What one hundred thousand enrolled students requires as a recruitment funnel, and what any selective institution anywhere has actually reached.
- How institutions in this space are funded, and what that predicts about achievable size.
- Provenance throughout: which figures are federal or audited, which are institutional self-report, which are computed here, and which do not exist.

### Out of Scope
- How to screen or measure a child (`talent-screening`), where a cutoff belongs (`admission-cutoff`), instrument quality at the tail (`gifted-assessment-quality`), validating one test against another (`test-evaluation`), which signals earn a place in an estimate (`metric-evidence`), routing and session design (`test-structure`), whether measured reasoning moves (`reasoning-growth`), whether a learning rate is estimable (`learning-rate-scoring`), causal identification of a program's effect (`gt-school-counterfactual`), where a student starts once admitted (`placement-and-dosing`), and whether the work is the applicant's own (`proctoring-integrity`).
- Whether educating a hundred thousand able children is desirable, and whether any particular instructional model works. This is an arithmetic and market document.
- Designing a product, a campaign, or a price. The conclusions here constrain those choices without making them.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

**SPOV 1: A six-figure target cannot be assembled from families who already know their child is able. That population is measured, small, and shrinking, and the institutions built to serve it have been closing. So awareness has to be manufactured rather than competed for, and the referral step is where it has to happen.**

- **Elaboration:** The instinct is to treat known gifted children as a pool to win share in, and the numbers say the pool is far too thin for that. The best available measure of families who know and will act is participation in the academic talent searches, which are the institutions whose entire function is testing children whose parents suspect something. Johns Hopkins' talent search tested roughly 80,000 students a year in 2005 and just over 15,000 in 2019-20, a fall of about 81% (5.2). Duke's closed outright on 8 October 2020 with 75 layoffs, and Duke's stated cause was that losing one summer of residential revenue produced "an unsustainable financial position" (5.1). **Duke's program served more than three million academically gifted children across forty years, averaging around 75,000 a year, and identifying them plus running summer sessions was financially fragile enough to die from one lost season.** Underneath the institutions, the household behaviour is measured too, and it is smaller than anyone expects. In a full state census of 705,074 Georgia elementary children, only 9.9% were ever referred for evaluation at all, and **parent-initiated referrals reached 0.4% of children** (3.3). Four in a thousand. Referral is also where advantage enters, since children on paid lunch were referred at 3.7 times the rate of children on free lunch (3.3). Thus the top of the funnel is not a marketing problem inside a large known pool, it is a discovery problem in a population that has never been looked at. The honest limit on this claim belongs in the same breath: **necessary is not sufficient**, and SPOVs 2 through 4 are the reasons. Growth advocates counter that talent-search decline measures the collapse of a summer-program business model rather than the number of aware families, and that aware families may simply have stopped needing a mail-order test.

**SPOV 2: One hundred thousand selectively admitted students has never been achieved by a single institution anywhere. The only things that have cleared it are national governments running hundreds of separate campuses, so this should be planned as a system, not as a school.**

- **Elaboration:** This is the finding that reframes the target from ambitious to unprecedented, and it is a matter of record rather than of judgement. Two selective systems exceed a hundred thousand today and both are states operating at national scale: England's grammar schools hold 193,747 pupils across **163 schools**, and India's Navodaya Vidyalayas admit by selection test across **653 functional schools** (6.3). South Korea's national gifted system peaked at 121,421 in 2013, has since fallen to 63,167, and **only 6,895 of those sit in full-time schools** with the rest in part-time programming (6.3). The largest single school on earth at any level of selectivity is City Montessori School in Lucknow at 61,345 pupils, and it admits from age three (6.3). In the United States the ceiling is far lower: the biggest full-time school for gifted children is Pine View at **1,752** and it is free and public, the biggest tuition-charging one with a gifted mission is Nueva at **967**, and the biggest selective public exam school of any kind is Brooklyn Tech at 5,848 (6.1, 6.2). The five best-known American schools for highly gifted children enrol about 2,262 between them, which is 0.08% of the identified population (6.1). Consequently the design question is not how to grow a school by fifty-seven times. It is whether to become a network of many small campuses on a shared spine, which is what every precedent at this scale actually is. Single-institution optimists counter that online delivery removes the campus constraint that forced those systems to federate, and that no historical ceiling binds a model the historical cases did not have.

**SPOV 3: Above five figures, the binding constraint is the funding model rather than identification. Every institution that has passed it is publicly funded, and the tuition-paying market is roughly the size of the target before ability is even considered.**

- **Elaboration:** Here is where the evidence goes somewhere the awareness argument does not reach, and it is the conclusion most likely to change what gets built. Every verified institution above five figures in this space is free to the family. The largest single virtual school is Commonwealth Charter Academy at 18,087, publicly funded; the largest network is Stride, averaging 234,000 enrollments at $9,677 of **public** revenue per student; and no tuition-charging American online school above roughly 1,100 total students could be located at all (7.1). Then the market arithmetic closes the door from the other side. Federal survey data puts 1,159,670 students in all US private schools charging $15,000 or more, and 600,680 in nonsectarian ones at an average of $34,610 (7.2). **So a hundred thousand paying students would be between 9% and 17% of the entire American high-tuition private school market, before requiring that any of those children also sit in the top few percent of ability.** Intersect the two conditions and the addressable market is about the size of the target, which means it is not a market. Price is not the lever either, since demand elasticity in private schooling runs about −0.19 overall and approximately zero in the top income tercile (7.2), so the wealthy are not price-sensitive and there simply are not a hundred thousand of them with a qualifying child who wants this model. **Therefore the route to six figures runs through public money, whether charter status or education savings accounts, and identification work is what makes that route usable rather than what substitutes for it.** Two counters deserve answers. Public funding imposes the accountability and admissions constraints a selective private school exists to avoid. And a genuinely new category can create demand that no survey of the existing one would capture.

**SPOV 4: State the bar or state the number, because at the strictest reading of the bar the target exceeds the entire national population that clears it. One of the two has to move, and saying which is a strategic decision rather than a measurement one.**

- **Elaboration:** The arithmetic here is simple, published, and rarely done. There are 35,746,725 children in American kindergarten through eighth grade (1.1). At the 95th percentile, which is what a conventional cognitive cutoff nominally selects, the qualifying band holds **1,787,336 children**, so a hundred thousand is 5.59% of every qualifying child in the country, roughly one in eighteen (1.2). At the 99th percentile the band is 357,467 and the target is **28%** of it. At the 99.9th the entire national band is **35,747 children, which is fewer than the target**, so that bar is arithmetically impossible and can be set aside (1.2). The funnel makes it tighter still. At the 71.4% yield reported by independent-school members, seating a hundred thousand needs about 140,000 offers, 212,000 completed applications and **455,000 inquiries**, and that last figure is a quarter of every qualifying child in the United States (6.4). Steady state then needs roughly 19,200 new enrollments and 26,900 offers every year, forever (6.4). **A quarter of the qualifying population has to hear about you and raise their hand.** So the pair of commitments, a hundred thousand students and a top-few-percent bar, is only coherent at the loosest end of the bar and only then with a recruitment reach no selective institution has demonstrated. The options are to loosen the bar, to go international, or to restate the number as a direction rather than a plan. Defenders of holding both counter that a stated impossible goal is a legitimate forcing device, and that treating it as arithmetic mistakes its purpose.

## Experts

> Chosen for productive tension. The first two entries disagree about how many children are missed and how you would know, the third measures the market that the first two's conclusions would have to be sold into, and the fourth exists because it is the only group that has run the experiment.

### Scott Peters, Matthew McBee, Jonathan Plucker, Marcia Gentry, D. Betsy McCoach
- **Who:** Gifted-education researchers who quantify how many able children identification systems fail to find, and who disagree with each other about the right way to count.
- **Focus:** Miss rates, referral as the gate, representation gaps, and what counts as the denominator.
- **Why Follow:** They supply the only estimates that exist, and their disagreement is instructive rather than embarrassing. Gentry's group counts millions "missing" by applying a counterfactual to a federal census; McBee and Makel state that typical identification sensitivity runs "on the order of 30% to 40%" from simulation; Peters and colleagues sidestep both by counting children already working above their placed grade level. Three methods, three answers, one shared direction.
- **Where:** [McBee & Makel (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007) | [Peters & Johnson (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1177/23328584241258480) | [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256)

### David Card and Laura Giuliano
- **Who:** Labour economists who studied a large district that switched from referral to testing every child, then switched back.
- **Focus:** What identification actually misses, measured on real children rather than modelled.
- **Why Follow:** This is the only natural experiment in the set, and the reversal is what makes it persuasive. Identification rose sharply with eligibility standards provably unchanged, a fifth of the newly found disadvantaged children already met the strict threshold applied to everyone else, and when the district suspended screening the rates fell back to baseline. That last fact rules out the objection that screening simply raised awareness permanently.
- **Where:** [Card & Giuliano (2016), PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113)

### Francesco Marsili and Marta Pellegrini; the authors of the Georgia referral census
- **Who:** A meta-analyst pair measuring how well nominations predict, and the researcher who obtained a full state population file on who gets referred.
- **Focus:** Whether asking an adult finds able children, and how many adults ask.
- **Why Follow:** Between them they close the loop this document turns on. Nomination predicts weakly and equally badly whoever is asked, at .31 for teachers and .30 for parents. And in a census of 705,074 children, parents initiated for 0.4% of them while nine in ten children were never referred by anyone. The second number is what makes the first one matter.
- **Where:** [Marsili & Pellegrini (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1177/01430343221105398)

### Susan Dynarski, Jonathan Gruber, Danielle Li; and the federal statistical agencies
- **Who:** Economists of private-school demand, plus the National Center for Education Statistics and the Civil Rights Data Collection.
- **Focus:** Who pays for schooling, how sensitive they are to price, and how many children exist to be enrolled.
- **Why Follow:** They supply the denominators the rest of this rests on and the demand curve nobody in gifted education looks at. The elasticity result is the load-bearing one: demand for private schooling is barely price-sensitive at the top of the income distribution, which means a high-tuition model is not failing to reach a hundred thousand families because it is expensive. There are not a hundred thousand such families.
- **Where:** [NCES Digest, Table 203.10](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d24/tables/dt24_203.10.asp) | [Private School Universe Survey](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pss/) | [Civil Rights Data Collection](https://civilrightsdata.ed.gov/)

## DOK 3: Insights

**Insight 1 - The institution that reached three million able children over forty years could not survive one bad season, and the surviving one shrank by four fifths. Read as market data rather than as sector news, that is the clearest available measure of how thin this market is, and nobody presents it that way.**
- Three facts that belong together and never appear together. Duke's talent search served more than three million academically gifted children over four decades, averaging roughly 75,000 a year (5.1). It closed on 8 October 2020, and the stated cause was the loss of a single summer of residential revenue leaving it in "an unsustainable financial position" (5.1). And the surviving talent search's testing volume fell from about 80,000 a year to just over 15,000, a drop of roughly 81% over fifteen years (5.2). Read as market data rather than as sector news, the aware-and-willing population supported two national institutions at the start of this period and supports one at a fifth of its former volume now. The cumulative figure cuts the other way and deserves stating: three million children over forty years is a large number, so the aware market is not tiny in the aggregate. What it will not support is revenue per child. Duke's programme touched millions on a model of testing plus a summer session, and one lost summer ended it. So a plan to enrol a hundred thousand of these families full-time and at tuition is asking a population that sustained a low-touch identification business to sustain the most expensive relationship in education. Sector observers counter that both organisations were selling residential summer programs rather than identification, and that their decline measures a summer-camp business rather than the number of families who know.

**Insight 2 - Identification rates vary by a factor of 415 between American states, which cannot be a fact about children, so the variation is a direct readout of how many are missed.**
- Computed from the raw federal file across all 52 jurisdictions, identification runs from 14.87% in South Carolina down to 0.04% in Vermont, which is 28 identified students in the entire state (2.2). Nobody would claim South Carolina's children are 415 times more likely to be able than Vermont's, so the spread measures policy rather than population. That makes it usable as an estimate rather than merely an embarrassment: if the true rate is at least the highest observed rate, then the national 6.1% implies roughly three million unidentified children in public kindergarten through eighth grade alone, which is about the same number as are identified (2.1, 2.2). The independent estimates land in the same region by unrelated routes, with one group counting 3.6 million missing against 3.2 to 3.3 million identified and another putting typical process sensitivity at 30% to 40% (3.1). **So the unidentified population is at least as large as the identified one.** The caution to carry is that a state's rate depends on what it calls a gifted program as much as on whom it finds, and Vermont's 28 students may reflect a state that funds nothing rather than a state that misses everyone.

**Insight 3 - Every measured step of the identification funnel loses more people than the test does, and the step that loses most is a parent deciding to ask.**
- Four measurements, ordered by where they sit in the funnel, and the order is the finding. A parent initiates for 0.4% of children (3.3). Anybody at all initiates for 9.9% (3.3). Requiring a nomination before testing drops the share of qualifying children found from .843 to .28 (3.2). And the test itself, applied to everyone with no gate, still misses roughly 16% at a 90th-percentile cut (3.2). Read upward, the instrument is the most accurate component of the system and the household is the least, by two orders of magnitude. That inverts where attention goes, since almost all measurement effort in this field is spent on the instrument. It also explains the advantage gradient without needing to invoke bias in the test, because referral rates differ 3.7-fold by lunch status before any child is measured (3.3). Practitioners counter that a parent who does not ask may be a parent whose child is not able, and that low referral rates are partly correct behaviour rather than pure loss.

**Insight 4 - The premise this whole strategy rests on has never been measured, and the reason is a design flaw that recurs in every study of it.**
- No study establishes what share of parents of high-ability children know their child is high-ability (4.1). The literature that appears to address it does something different: every parent-accuracy study samples parents who had **already come forward**, which measures how often a parent's suspicion turns out to be right and says nothing about how many parents have the suspicion (4.1). Those are the two halves of the same statistic and only the easy half has been collected. The Georgia census brackets the answer indirectly, since 0.4% of parents referring against a qualifying rate somewhere between 5% and 15% leaves a very large gap, but bracketing is not measuring (3.3, 1.2). Two things follow. Any argument built on parental unawareness is resting on a strongly implied and unmeasured premise, and should say so. And whoever screens a large unselected population first will produce this number as a byproduct, which is a genuine research contribution rather than only a commercial asset. Sceptics counter that the absence of a study may reflect the question being unanswerable rather than unasked, since identifying the denominator requires testing the children whose parents did not come forward.

**Insight 5 - The population arithmetic and the funnel arithmetic disagree about how hard this is, and the funnel is the one that binds.**
- At the loosest defensible bar the population looks adequate: 1,787,336 children sit above the 95th percentile in kindergarten through eighth grade, so a hundred thousand is 5.59% of them (1.2). One in eighteen sounds like a marketing problem. But enrolling them requires roughly 140,000 offers, 212,000 completed applications and 455,000 inquiries at ordinary independent-school yield, and that last number is a quarter of the entire qualifying population (6.4). The two calculations differ by a factor of about four and a half, and only the second one describes what has to be done. Nobody in this sector states the funnel version, because private schools report yield and selectivity rather than reach. Setting that against the aware channel's 15,000 tests a year makes the gap concrete: the required inquiry volume is about thirty times the entire annual throughput of the surviving national talent search (5.2, 6.4). Marketers counter that inquiry-to-enrollment ratios collapse when a product is free or differentiated enough, so the 71.4% yield borrowed from tuition-charging independent schools may be the wrong constant.

**Insight 6 - Selectivity and scale have never coexisted in one institution, and the cases that reached scale all did the same thing about it.**
- Every precedent above a hundred thousand selectively admitted students is a government running many campuses: 163 schools in one country's grammar system, 653 in another's selection-test network (6.3). The largest single school on earth at any selectivity is 61,345 and admits three-year-olds (6.3). The one national gifted system that reached this scale did so mostly part-time, with 6,895 of 63,167 places in full-time schools, and it has shrunk by half from its peak (6.3). Meanwhile the whole American full-time sector for highly able children fits inside a few thousand students, and its largest member is free and public (6.1, 6.2). The pattern across the successful cases is federation plus a shared admission mechanism, which is a different organisational claim than "a big school." It also suggests where the durable asset sits, since what those systems own centrally is the selection test and the standard rather than the buildings. Defenders of a single institution counter that online delivery is precisely the thing none of these precedents had, and that a shared spine with no campuses is a system in the only sense that matters.

**Insight 7 - Two independent reversals in the record show that identification is a tap rather than a discovery, and both were driven by money.**
- When a large district began testing every child, identification rose from 3.3% to 5.5% overall and from 1.4% to 4.3% among low-income and English-learner children, with eligibility standards unchanged and a fifth of the newly found disadvantaged children already clearing the strict threshold (3.4). When that district suspended screening for budget reasons, rates returned to their pre-program baseline within about a year (3.4). Separately, the national identified count fell 11.2% between 2017-18 and 2020-21, roughly four times faster than enrollment fell (2.1). Neither movement plausibly reflects a change in children. Both track what was funded and administered. So the number of gifted children a country has is, operationally, a budget line, and the reversibility is the proof: a phenomenon that switches off when a purchase order lapses was never a property of the population. That is also the strongest available reason to expect a large unidentified reservoir, and the strongest available warning that any program's own identification numbers describe its spending. The counter is that the 11.2% national fall coincides with a pandemic that suspended testing everywhere, so it measures a disrupted year rather than a trend.

**Provocation questions for the owner, in the order most likely to sharpen the stance:**
- If the funnel needs a quarter of the qualifying population to inquire, is the screener a marketing instrument or is it the admission process itself? Those imply different products.
- Every precedent at this scale federated and kept the selection mechanism central. If that is the model, what is the actual asset being built, the school or the test?
- Public funding is the only route above five figures in the record. What does a selective school give up by taking it, and is the thing it gives up the reason it exists?
- Nobody knows what share of parents know. If GT measured that first, on a population nobody has screened, what would that be worth independent of enrollment?
- The strictest reading of the bar makes the target arithmetically impossible. Which number is the real commitment?

### Overall Summary

There are 35,746,725 children in American kindergarten through eighth grade. At the 95th percentile, which is what a conventional cognitive cutoff nominally selects, 1,787,336 of them qualify, so a hundred thousand students is 5.59% of every qualifying child in the country. Against that, 2,955,610 students were formally identified as gifted in 2020-21, which is 6.1% of enrollment and a fall of 11.2% from the 3,329,540 counted in 2017-18. The population that does know is measurable and contracting: one national talent search fell from about 80,000 tests a year to just over 15,000, and the other closed in October 2020 after forty years of identifying roughly 100,000 children annually, citing one lost summer of revenue. On scale, no single institution has ever selectively enrolled a hundred thousand students. - **Subcategory 2.1: The national count, and the fall nobody has explained**
  - **Source:** Civil Rights Data Collection 2020-21 public use file; NCES published counts through 2017-18
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**

      - The last nationally published count before that: **3,329,540** for 2017-18.
      - The count fell **11.2%** between those waves, a loss of 373,930 students, which is roughly four times the rate at which total enrollment fell.

And it is a stock, which means anyone asking "how many are identified each year" is asking a question the federal collection is not built to answer.

- **Subcategory 2.2: A 415-to-1 spread between states, and why the widely quoted range is worse than useless**
  - **Source:** Computed from the Civil Rights Data Collection file across all 52 jurisdictions
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Highest state identification rate: **South Carolina, 14.87%**. Lowest: **Vermont, 0.04%**, which is **28 identified students in the entire state**. The ratio is about **415 to 1**.
      - The commonly circulated range of 1.98% to 19.33% comes from a research brief whose own endnote states it **excludes every district reporting zero gifted enrollment**. That exclusion inflates New York from 1.32% to 17.19%.
      - Under the corrected computation, Maryland is fifth rather than first and West Virginia is 43rd rather than last.
      - All 52 computed state rates round to the values the federal agency independently prints, which is a reconciliation check rather than an assertion.
      - Related scale figure: the entire kindergarten gifted program of the largest school district in the country is **2,500 seats**, plus 1,000 in third grade.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Identification rates differ between American states by a factor of over four hundred, and the true spread is far wider than the figure usually quoted, because that figure quietly drops every district reporting nobody. Since children are not four hundred times more able in one state than another, the spread is a measurement of policy. It is also the cleanest available way to bound how many children go unfound, because the gap between the national rate and the highest state rate is an estimate of what a more willing system would locate.
    - **Link to source:** [Civil Rights Data Collection](https://civilrightsdata.ed.gov/)

### Category 3: Why Identification Misses Them

- **Subcategory 3.1: Three independent estimates of the miss, answering three different questions**
  - **Source:** Gentry et al. (2019); McBee & Makel (2019); Peters and colleagues on above-grade performance
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - One group counts **3.6 million "missing"** children against 3.2 to 3.3 million identified, by applying a counterfactual to a federal census.
      - A second states directly that "**the sensitivity of a typical identification process is on the order of 30% to 40%**," which is derived from simulation rather than measured on children.
      - A third redefines the target population instead of estimating the miss, finding **20% to 49%** of students already performing a grade level above where they are placed.
      - The three are not competing estimates of one quantity. They answer, respectively, how many a counterfactual implies, what share a modelled process would find, and how many are visibly ready for harder work.
      - Provenance note: the technical report behind the 3.6 million figure could not be retrieved, so that number rests on author-produced slides and press coverage, and the identified count beside it is reported as 3.2 million in one author source and 3.3 million in another.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is no agreed miss rate, and the honest reason is that three research groups have asked three different questions. What survives the disagreement is the direction and the order of magnitude: every approach puts the unfound population in the millions and comparable to the found population. The figure worth quoting is the sensitivity one, both because it is stated plainly and because it is the only one expressed as a property of a process rather than of a population.
    - **Link to source:** [McBee & Makel (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The nomination step costs more than the test, by a factor of about fifty**
  - **Source:** McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Method: an analytic psychometric model by numerical integration, not a student sample.
      - With no nomination stage, a single test at a 90th-percentile cut and high reliability finds **.843** of qualifying children, so it misses about 16%.
      - Add a nomination stage at a 90th-percentile cutoff in front of that test and the share found falls to **.28**, a false-negative rate of .720.
      - The structural reason, verbatim: "the confirmatory assessment can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives because those students that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory test."
      - Stated as a rule: "**screeners cannot improve the sensitivity of an assessment system; they can only reduce it.**"
      - From the abstract: "**Under commonly implemented conditions, the nomination stage can cause the false negative rate to easily exceed 60%.**"
      - The simulation fixed teacher nomination validity at .70 as a deliberately optimistic ceiling. The measured meta-analytic value is roughly half that, so the modelled loss is conservative.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The step before the test costs about fifty times more than the test does. A good instrument protects against admitting the wrong child and offers nothing at all against rejecting the right one, because a child filtered out at nomination never reaches the instrument. The authors chose a generous assumption about how well teachers nominate and the loss was still catastrophic, which means the real figure is worse. That places the highest-leverage intervention before any measurement happens.
    - **Link to source:** [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256)

- **Subcategory 3.3: A full state census of who actually gets referred, and the number is four in a thousand**
  - **Source:** A population file of Georgia elementary students obtained from the state education department
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sample: **all** Georgia public elementary students in grades 1 to 5 for academic year 2004, **N = 705,074**, drawn from a state file of 1,820,635 across all grades. This is a population dataset obtained by special request, not a sample.
      - Only **9.9% of students were ever referred** for gifted evaluation by anybody.
      - **Parent-initiated referrals reached 0.4% of students.**
      - Students paying full price for lunch were referred at **3.7 times** the rate of students receiving free lunch.
      - The author's framing of the step, verbatim: referral is the "**gate keeper**."
      - Variables available were race, free or reduced-price lunch status, whether nominated, the source of the nomination, and whether identified.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the document, because it is a whole state rather than a sample and it measures the behaviour rather than modelling it. Nine children in ten were never put forward by anyone, and parents put forward four in a thousand. The advantage gradient appears before any child is measured, at nearly four to one by lunch status, which locates the inequity in the referral step rather than in the instrument. If a family's decision to ask is the gate, then a program's reachable population is the set of families who already suspect.
    - **Link to source:** Georgia referral census as reported in the gifted-identification literature. Provenance note: the underlying article was read through a secondary full-text source; the figures above are quoted from it and the primary was not independently retrieved.

- **Subcategory 3.4: The natural experiment, and the suspension that makes it convincing**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Design: a large district began administering an ability test to every second grader instead of waiting for referrals, with **eligibility standards unchanged**.
      - Identification rose from **3.3% to 5.5%** overall, and from **1.4% to 4.3%** among low-income and English-learner students, an increase in odds of about **174%** for that group.
      - The finding that matters most for a program with a high bar: "**A full 20% of the compliers have IQs of 130 or higher**," which is the threshold applied to non-disadvantaged children, so the referral process had been losing children who already met the strict standard.
      - The authors' conclusion on that point: "the traditional referral system also misses some high-ability nondisadvantaged students."
      - On reversal: funding for testing was cut and the screening programme suspended, and identification rates returned to the pre-programme baseline within about a year.
      - That reversal is what rules out the alternative explanation that screening produced a durable change in awareness rather than a change in testing.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Holding the bar exactly where it was and simply testing everyone raised identification by two thirds overall and tripled it among disadvantaged children, and a fifth of the newly found children had already cleared the strict threshold. Then the money stopped and the numbers went back. The reversal is the part that makes this persuasive rather than merely suggestive, because a change that unwinds when a purchase order lapses was administrative rather than developmental. The children were there before and after.
    - **Link to source:** [Card & Giuliano (2016), PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113)

- **Subcategory 3.5: Asking an adult barely works, and it does not matter which adult**
  - **Source:** Marsili & Pellegrini (2022); Peters & Johnson (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 29 studies, nomination for giftedness correlates with the traditional measures used to identify it at **ESr = .32**, 95% CI [.25, .39].
      - By who is asked: **teacher .31** on 97 effect sizes, **parent .30** on 27, **self .33** on 13, **peer .27** on 7. Nomination form was **not a significant moderator**.
      - Heterogeneity is large enough to matter: the prediction interval runs from **−.12 to .79**, which includes zero, so individual settings can show no relationship at all.
      - Two moderators were significant: the relation was stronger in primary than secondary school, and larger for achievement than for ability.
      - On who disappears entirely: across **29,051 schools enrolling ten or more English learners**, the median **Representation Index was zero**. That index compares a group's share of the gifted programme against its share of enrollment, so zero means the typical such school identifies **no English learners at all** as gifted. Among the top 5% of schools on that index the median was 1.54.
      - The comparison figure for students with disabilities is the same: a median index of zero across 43,094 schools.
      - The authors also identify what moves it: state mandates to offer gifted services, requirements for a formal gifted plan, and regular compliance audits all correlated with better availability and representation.
      - Provenance note: the meta-analysis's table and narrative disagree by .01 to .02 on some figures, and the self and peer estimates rest on only 13 and 7 effect sizes.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Nomination predicts weakly, and swapping the nominator does not help, because teachers, parents, peers and children themselves all land within .06 of each other and the difference is not statistically meaningful. So the limit sits in the task of judging ability by observation rather than in whose observation it is. The English-learner figure shows the failure at its most complete: in a typical school with a gifted programme and a substantial English-learner population, not one of those children is in it.
    - **Link to source:** [Marsili & Pellegrini (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1177/01430343221105398) | [Peters & Johnson (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1177/23328584241258480)

### Category 4: Whether Parents Know

- **Subcategory 4.1: The question has never been answered, and every study of it answers a different one**
  - **Source:** The parent-accuracy literature, examined for this purpose
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **No located study measures what share of parents of high-ability children know their child is high-ability.**
      - The reason is a recurring design feature rather than an oversight. Every parent-accuracy study in this literature samples parents **who had already come forward** to nominate or refer their child.
      - Those designs estimate how often a parent's suspicion turns out to be correct. They cannot estimate how many parents have the suspicion, because parents who never came forward are absent from the sample by construction.
      - In diagnostic language, the literature reports something like precision and has never reported sensitivity.
      - The nearest indirect bracket comes from the referral census: parents initiated for 0.4% of children against a qualifying rate somewhere between 5% and 15%, which implies a large gap without measuring it.
      - Provenance note: one frequently cited parent-accuracy percentage from the 1970s circulates as both 67% and 76%, so no figure for it should be quoted.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The premise that many parents do not know is unmeasured. It is not contradicted, and the referral census makes it very likely, but the direct study does not exist and the reason is instructive: you cannot measure how many parents are unaware by surveying parents who came forward. Getting the number requires testing a population nobody has screened and then asking those families what they believed beforehand. Any argument resting on parental unawareness should carry that caveat, and whoever collects the number first will have produced something the field does not have.
    - **Link to source:** Assessed across the parent-nomination literature; see [Marsili & Pellegrini (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1177/01430343221105398) for the nomination-validity side of the question.

### Category 5: The Size of the Aware Market, and Its Contraction

- **Subcategory 5.1: The institution that identified this many children every year closed after forty years**
  - **Source:** Duke University announcements and press coverage, October 2020
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Duke's talent identification programme "has served **more than three million academically gifted students** in grades four through 12 since beginning in **1980**," so it ran for 40 years at an average of roughly 75,000 students a year.
      - It closed on **8 October 2020**, with public documents showing **75 employees** to be laid off beginning 6 January.
      - The stated cause, verbatim from the university: "This is, unfortunately, a direct result of COVID-19, which forced the cancellation of the TIP residential summer session on the Duke campus and other colleges in 2020 and likely again in 2021 and thus created an **unsustainable financial position for the program**."
      - Provenance note that matters: Duke published no revenue, enrolment or deficit figures for the programme in any announcement. "Unsustainable financial position" is the university's characterisation and no audited number supporting it was released.
      - What the programme sold, which bears on how to read the closure: identification plus residential summer sessions, so the revenue at risk was the summer business rather than the testing.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** An institution whose annual identification volume equalled the entire target here, sustained for four decades, was killed by the loss of one summer's residential revenue. Read as market data that is a statement about how thin the margins are in serving families who already know. Read carefully, it is also a statement about a summer-camp business rather than about the number of aware families, and the university released no figures either way. The fact is solid; the interpretation needs the caveat attached.
    - **Link to source:** [ABC11, Duke layoffs and TIP closure, 9 October 2020](https://abc11.com/post/duke-university-layoffs-tip-college-covid/6874746/) | [Forbes, on the three-million figure](https://www.forbes.com/sites/michaeltnietzel/2020/10/09/universities-continue-their-pandemic-related-layoffs-ithaca-college-duke-and-the-university-of-memphis-among-the-latest/)

- **Subcategory 5.2: The surviving talent search shrank by about four fifths**
  - **Source:** Johns Hopkins Center for Talented Youth figures as reported
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Talent search participation fell from roughly **80,000 students a year in 2005** to **just over 15,000 tested in 2019-20**, a decline of about **81%**.
      - The talent search model is the closest available proxy for the population of families who both suspect their child is able and will pay to have that tested, since that is precisely the transaction.
      - Provenance note: these figures are a mix of tiers, with the earlier number from secondary reporting, and the organisation's own site blocks automated retrieval so the pair could not be confirmed against a single primary document.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The one national institution still doing this tests about fifteen thousand children a year, down from about eighty thousand fifteen years earlier. Taken with the closure of the other one, the population that knows and acts supported two such organisations at the start of the period and supports one at a fifth of its former volume now. That is the ceiling on any strategy that competes for already-aware families, and it is roughly a seventh of the annual new enrolments a hundred-thousand-student programme would need in steady state.
    - **Link to source:** [CTY talent search](https://cty.jhu.edu/). Access note: the organisation's site returns 403 to automated requests, which is bot-blocking rather than a dead link, so it resolves in a browser but the figures could not be machine-verified against it.

### Category 6: What the Target Requires, and What Has Reached It

- **Subcategory 6.1: The American full-time sector for gifted children fits in a few thousand students, and its largest member is free**
  - **Source:** NCES Common Core of Data and Private School Universe Survey; institutional reports
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Largest full-time school for gifted children in the US, verified from federal enrollment data: **Pine View School, Florida, 1,752 students** (2024-25). It is **free and public**.
      - Largest tuition-charging school with an explicit gifted mission: **The Nueva School, 967** across two campuses, tuition $42,060 to $66,960.
      - Largest selective private online school: **Stanford Online High School, 1,073 total**, of which about **44% are full-time**, so roughly 472, at $31,900.
      - Other federal counts: **The Roeper School 496**, **Mirman School 376**.
      - **Davidson Academy** of Nevada, the closest analogue to an accelerated model and admitting at the 99.9th percentile since 2006: **173 on campus** plus **103 online**, having accepted **41 students** in 2025-26 per its own governing board minutes.
      - The five best-known American schools for highly gifted children enrol about **2,262 between them**, which is **0.08%** of the identified population.
      - Largest selective public exam school of any kind: **Brooklyn Tech, 5,848**. Also **Stuyvesant 3,261** and **Thomas Jefferson 2,108**, all free.
      - Provenance note: the federal figures for Pine View, Roeper, Mirman, Stuyvesant and Thomas Jefferson are tier-one federal data. Nueva's and Stanford's come from school profiles, which are self-reported.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The entire American sector of full-time schools for highly able children is smaller than a single large high school. Its biggest member charges nothing, its biggest tuition-charging member is under a thousand students, and the five most famous ones together account for less than a tenth of one percent of identified children. The most calibrating figure is Davidson: free, public, nationally known, running for two decades at the very top of the ability range, and it accepted forty-one students this year. Any plan to enrol a hundred thousand is not scaling this sector, it is inventing a different one.
    - **Link to source:** [NCES Common Core of Data](https://nces.ed.gov/ccd/) | [Private School Universe Survey](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pss/)

- **Subcategory 6.2: Online delivery reaches six figures, and every verified case is free**
  - **Source:** Stride Inc. SEC filings; National Education Policy Center virtual school reports
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Largest US virtual school network: **Stride**, averaging **234,000 enrollments** in FY2025 at **$9,677 of revenue per enrollment**, which is public per-pupil funding rather than tuition.
      - Largest single virtual school: **Commonwealth Charter Academy**, Pennsylvania, **18,087** students (2021-22), free.
      - **No tuition-charging US online K-12 school above roughly 1,100 total enrolled students could be located.**
      - Every verified online school above 5,000 students is tuition-free.
      - Public opinion favours the online form for this purpose: **75% support an online school for gifted students against 49% for a separate physical campus**, and among parents **80% against 56%**.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Online delivery removes the physical ceiling and reaches hundreds of thousands, so the scale is demonstrably achievable in that channel. The catch is that every case which achieves it runs on public per-pupil funding at under ten thousand dollars a head, and nothing charging tuition gets past about eleven hundred students. So the medium is not the constraint and the price is. Usefully, the public prefers the online form for exactly this application by a wide margin, which points the growth vehicle away from campuses.
    - **Link to source:** [Stride investor relations](https://investors.k12.com/) | [NEPC virtual schools reports](https://nepc.colorado.edu/publication/virtual-schools-annual-2021)

- **Subcategory 6.3: Nothing selective has reached this scale as one institution, and the cases that reached it federated**
  - **Source:** UK Department for Education school census; India Ministry of Education; South Korean gifted education statistics; Guinness World Records
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - England's grammar schools, which admit by selective examination: **193,747 pupils across 163 schools** (January 2026 school census).
      - India's Jawahar Navodaya Vidyalayas, admitting "on the basis of a Selection Test": **653 functional schools**, with roughly **49,640 admitted to Class VI each year**.
      - South Korea's national gifted education system peaked at **121,421 students in 2013** and has fallen to **63,167**, of which only **6,895 are in full-time gifted schools** and the remainder are in part-time programming.
      - Largest single school on earth at any level of selectivity: **City Montessori School, Lucknow, 61,345 pupils**, and the record entry notes it "admits children from the age of three."
      - So the two systems above a hundred thousand are governments operating 163 and 653 separate campuses respectively, and the largest single non-selective school on the planet is under 62,000.
      - Provenance note: an enrollment total of roughly 291,000 for the Indian network was reachable only through a data aggregator rather than the ministry's own system, so the ministry-verified facts are the school count, the selection mechanism and the annual intake, not the total. Turkey, Vietnam, Israel, China and the former Soviet specialised school system could not be verified despite searching.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A hundred thousand selectively admitted students has been achieved twice, both times by a national government running hundreds of campuses under a shared admission test. No single institution has done it, and the largest single school in the world of any kind falls short of it while admitting toddlers. The one national gifted system that reached this scale did so mostly through part-time programming and has since halved. The pattern in the successful cases is federation plus a central selection mechanism, which is a different thing to build than a large school.
    - **Link to source:** [UK DfE school census statistics](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/) | [Navodaya Vidyalaya Samiti](https://navodaya.gov.in/). Access note: the Indian ministry site times out on automated requests and was reached manually.

- **Subcategory 6.4: The funnel, which is a tighter constraint than the population**
  - **Source:** NAIS member yield data; computed on the denominators in Category 1
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Reported yield among independent-school members, meaning the share of admitted students who enrol: **71.4%**.
      - Working backwards from a hundred thousand enrolled at that yield: about **140,000 offers**, **212,000 completed applications**, and **455,000 inquiries**.
      - That inquiry figure is about **25% of every qualifying child in the United States** at the 95th-percentile band computed in 1.2.
      - Steady state, allowing for a median attrition rate of **8.10%** plus graduation from a nine-grade programme: roughly **19,200 new enrollments and 26,900 offers every year**, indefinitely.
      - For comparison, the annual new-enrollment requirement is slightly above the entire annual testing volume of the surviving national talent search, and the required inquiry volume is about thirty times it.
      - Provenance note: the offer, application and inquiry figures are computations applying published independent-school conversion rates to the target. They are not published statistics and the conversion rates come from tuition-charging schools, which may not describe a free or highly differentiated offer.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Counting the qualifying population makes this look like a one-in-eighteen problem. Counting the funnel makes it a one-in-four problem, because ordinary conversion rates mean a hundred thousand seats requires almost half a million families to raise their hand. The second number is the one that has to be delivered, and nobody in this sector states it because private schools report selectivity rather than reach. Set against a national aware channel that tests fifteen thousand children a year, the gap is about thirtyfold, and that is the arithmetic case for building demand rather than competing for it.
    - **Link to source:** [NAIS research](https://www.nais.org/research/)

### Category 7: The Funding Constraint

- **Subcategory 7.1: Every institution in this space above five figures is publicly funded**
  - **Source:** As in 6.2, restated as a funding claim
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The three verified institutions above ten thousand students in this space are a virtual network at 234,000, a single virtual school at 18,087, and national systems abroad, and **all are free to the family**.
      - Per-pupil revenue in the largest network is **$9,677**, which is public funding.
      - The largest tuition-charging entity of any kind located in this space is under 1,100 students.
      - The two selective systems above a hundred thousand internationally are state-run and free at the point of use.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Stated as a funding pattern rather than a size pattern, the record is unambiguous: the free institutions reach six figures and the paying ones stop in the hundreds or low thousands. That holds across delivery models, countries and levels of selectivity. So the question "can a school reach a hundred thousand able students" separates into two, and the achievable version is publicly funded while the tuition version has no precedent above about a thousand.
    - **Link to source:** [Stride investor relations](https://investors.k12.com/) | [NEPC virtual schools reports](https://nepc.colorado.edu/publication/virtual-schools-annual-2021)

- **Subcategory 7.2: The high-tuition private market is about the size of the target, before ability is considered**
  - **Source:** NCES Private School Universe Survey; Dynarski, Gruber & Li on private-school demand
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Students enrolled in US private schools charging **$15,000 or more**: **1,159,670** across 4,580 schools, at an average tuition of **$29,960**.
      - Restricted to nonsectarian schools charging $15,000 or more: **600,680 students** across 2,870 schools, average tuition **$34,610**.
      - So a hundred thousand paying students would be **8.6%** of the first figure or **16.6%** of the second, before any ability requirement is applied.
      - Private schooling is **10.0%** of US students, down from 11.7% in 1995.
      - Price elasticity of demand for private schooling runs about **−0.19** overall, **−0.44** in the bottom income tercile, and approximately **zero in the top tercile**.
      - Definition note quoted from the tuition table, because it changes what the average means: "Each school reports the highest annual tuition charged for a full-time student; this amount does not take into account discounts that individual students may receive."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** At elite tuition the addressable market is roughly the size of the goal. Between nine and seventeen percent of every American child in a high-tuition private school would have to enrol, and that is before requiring them to be in the top few percent of ability, which cuts the intersection to something smaller than the target. The elasticity result closes the obvious escape: demand at the top of the income distribution barely responds to price, so this is not a discount problem. There are not a hundred thousand wealthy families with a qualifying child who want this. And the sector is shrinking as a share of enrollment, not growing.
    - **Link to source:** [Private School Universe Survey](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/pss/) | [Dynarski, Gruber & Li](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15461)
