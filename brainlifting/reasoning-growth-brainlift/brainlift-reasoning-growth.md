# BrainLift: Training a Trait That Is Supposed to Be Fixed — What Moves Reasoning Ability, and What a Learning System Can See

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose

Reasoning ability is the part of a child's profile instruction is least expected to touch — a cognitive-abilities test measures it *because* it is supposed to resist coaching. This BrainLift asks two questions that turn out to be one. **What has been shown to move measured reasoning rather than just the score?** And **what can a software learning environment observe that separates real improvement from compliance, familiarity, or gaming?** Both reduce to telling durable change apart from visible activity, which is the discipline the document is built around.

Four claims are treated as separate throughout, because they rest on separate evidence: *the training raised the score*, *the training raised the ability*, *the gain survived the training being taken away*, and *the gain showed up on material the training never covered*. The same split applies to the child: *working*, *learning*, *improving faster than peers*, and *that being a property of the child* are four different findings.

### In Scope

- Whether measured reasoning moves at all — the cognitive-training controversy, schooling's causal effect on intelligence, fadeout, and the population-level Flynn evidence.
- Programs that train reasoning directly, and the transfer problem they must clear.
- How much of an apparent gain is bought by familiarity — coaching effects and retest gains with no instruction at all.
- The instructional mechanisms with the best evidence, and the boundary condition where each one reverses.
- Why improvement visible inside a session is a poor index of durable learning.
- Validated behavioral detectors built from real interaction logs: gaming, futile persistence, help misuse, carelessness, off-task behavior, and affect.
- The dispositions "hustle" is meant to name, at both their original claim and their meta-analytic revision.
- What adaptive and machine-delivered instruction has measurably delivered.

### Out of Scope

- **Scoring how fast an individual learns** — owned by the companion `learning-rate-scoring` BrainLift.
- **Diagnosing which reasoning areas a child is weak in.** This document starts after a weakness profile exists.
- **Instrument validity and tail quality** (`gifted-assessment-quality`), **validating a new test against an old one** (`test-evaluation`), **test structure and routing** (`test-structure`), **which signals earn a place in an ability estimate** (`metric-evidence`), **causal identification of program effect** (`gt-school-counterfactual`), and **whether the work is the child's own** (`proctoring-integrity`).
- **The ethics of profiling children's effort and affect from logs.** The documented fairness failures are recorded as evidence; what may be collected and retained is a separate question.
- Any mapping onto a particular system, curriculum, or plan.

>
> Questions to push against once DOK 3 exists:
>
> - Schooling raises measured intelligence by 1–5 points a year; computerized training raises it by

>   software have it — or is the dose the mechanism, in which case no short program qualifies?
> - The strongest positive reasoning-training result measured transfer on tests built from the same item
>   types it trained. What would a fair transfer measure look like, and does a product that also sells

>   What claim survives being told a third of your measured gain would have happened anyway?
> - A guardrailed AI tutor removed the harm, produced no benefit, and left students feeling they had
>   learned more. What is an AI layer *for*, then?

## Experts

> Chosen for productive tension. These ten form five pairs, and the two members of each pair are on
> record against each other on a question this document has to answer: whether reasoning training
> transfers, whether any broad intervention moves measured ability, whether a machine tutor teaches or
> substitutes, whether an effort disposition is a real construct, and whether a behavioral record tells
> you what you want to know.

- **Expert 1 — Karl Josef Klauer (1929–2023)**
  - **Who:** German educational psychologist; Professor of Education at RWTH Aachen 1976–1994. Built three age-graded reasoning-training programs and the meta-analysis evaluating them.
  - **Focus:** A theory that decomposes inductive reasoning into six problem classes, and training built to teach the comparison strategies it specifies.
  - **Why Follow:** The strongest positive pole in the debate — about half a standard deviation on fluid-intelligence tests across 74 experiments with ~3,600 children, effects that grew rather than faded, and a near-zero placebo arm. Any claim that reasoning cannot be taught must answer this first. **Conflict of interest:** he evaluated his own program, and the outcome tests were built from the item types he trained.
  - **Where:** [Meta-analysis (2008)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313402) | [Extension (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123)

- **Expert 2 — Randall W. Engle**
  - **Who:** Professor of Psychology, Georgia Institute of Technology; elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2020.
  - **Focus:** Working memory, attention control, and the methodological bar a training study must clear before its transfer claim counts.
  - **Why Follow:** The pole against Klauer, and the objection is about evidence rather than theory. His group's placebo-controlled replication found no transfer on any of 17 measures despite large gains on the trained task. The general form matters more than the study: the biggest transfer effects come from small samples with weak control groups, and **the better the design, the smaller the effect**.
  - **Where:** [Georgia Tech profile](https://psychology.gatech.edu/people/randall-w-engle) | [No evidence of intelligence improvement (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029082)

- **Expert 3 — Stuart J. Ritchie**
  - **Who:** Psychologist; lecturer at the Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology and Neuroscience, King's College London since 2018; author of *Science Fictions* (2020) on bias and hype in research.
  - **Focus:** Human intelligence, and the credibility of the evidence behind published psychological claims.
  - **Why Follow:** He supplies the one intervention shown to raise measured intelligence and keep it raised — schooling, at 1 to 5 IQ points per year across three designs and 600,000+ people. That is the existence proof the skeptics do not dispute, and it moves the question from *whether* ability can be moved to *what dose* moves it. He is also unusually well placed to be read against himself, since his other work is about why published effects shrink.
  - **Where:** [How much does education improve intelligence? (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618774253) | [Science Fictions](https://www.sciencefictions.org/about)

- **Expert 4 — Douglas K. Detterman**
  - **Who:** Professor Emeritus, Case Western Reserve University; founded the journal *Intelligence* in 1977 and edited it until 2016; founded the International Society for Intelligence Research.
  - **Focus:** The empirical status of transfer as an explanation for intelligence.
  - **Why Follow:** The restraint pole against Ritchie, and the most uncompromising skeptic of the premise every general-reasoning program rests on. His position: most studies find no transfer, the ones claiming it manufacture it with hints, and transfer is rare and governed by similarity. Worth reading because he states the strong version — that education built on transfer is "totally lacking in empirical support." If he is right, teaching reasoning strategies teaches strategies, not reasoning.
  - **Where:** [Transfer as an epiphenomenon (1993)](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/1993-detterman.pdf) | [CV](https://isironline.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Detterman-Vita-2019-1-1-2019.pdf)

- **Expert 5 — Hamsa Bastani**
  - **Who:** Associate Professor of Operations, Information and Decisions, Wharton School; co-directs the Wharton Healthcare Analytics Lab; has deployed algorithms at national scale for COVID-19 border screening and medicine access.
  - **Focus:** Designing and evaluating AI systems in consequential settings, including one of the first large field trials of generative-AI tutors.
  - **Why Follow:** She ran the experiment that separates the two things this document is about. With nearly a thousand students, an unguarded GPT-4 tutor raised practice performance 48% and then left students **17% below those who never had access** once it was withdrawn. The cleanest demonstration that a rising curve inside a tool can be uninformative about what the child keeps — from someone whose work is deploying algorithms, not debunking them.
  - **Where:** [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) | [Personal site](https://hamsabastani.github.io/)

- **Expert 6 — Greg Kestin**
  - **Who:** Physicist and Preceptor in the Department of Physics, Harvard University.
  - **Focus:** Whether an AI tutor built deliberately around established instructional principles can beat the best available classroom practice.
  - **Why Follow:** The pole against Bastani, and the disagreement is empirical. His randomized trial found an AI tutor beating an active-learning class by 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations, in less time. **But** his outcome was measured immediately and his tutor was heavily scaffolded, so the two studies may be measuring different things rather than contradicting each other — and working out which is the interpretive problem any AI learning product inherits.
  - **Where:** [AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6)

- **Expert 7 — Angela Duckworth**
  - **Who:** Professor of Psychology, University of Pennsylvania; MacArthur Fellow; founder of Character Lab; introduced grit as a construct in 2007.
  - **Focus:** Non-cognitive predictors of achievement, and the measurement problems attached to all of them.
  - **Why Follow:** She is the source of the idea that effort predicts success beyond talent, and the most useful critic of the use this document cares about. **Within a decade she argued publicly against high-stakes character assessment**, stated the Grit Scale is unfit for selection, and noted it can be faked easily. Her methodological work is sharper still: self-report and behavioral measures of the same construct converge at r = .10 to .15, and students in more demanding schools rate themselves *lower* on grit while their attendance improves.
  - **Where:** [Grit (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.6.1087) | [Measurement matters (2015)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X15584327)

- **Expert 8 — Marcus Credé**
  - **Who:** Associate Professor of Psychology, Iowa State University.
  - **Focus:** Meta-analytic evaluation of popular educational constructs — specifically whether they add anything over the established traits they resemble.
  - **Why Follow:** The direct rebuttal to Duckworth, and not a hedge. Pooling 584 effect sizes from 66,807 people, he found grit correlating .84 with conscientiousness, the proposed structure unconfirmed, and **grit adding literally nothing over conscientiousness** in predicting academic performance. His bar is the one any behavioral "hustle" measure has to clear: beat conscientiousness, which is cheap to measure and already understood.
  - **Where:** [Much ado about grit (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000102) | [What shall we do about grit?](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f4a39379-1728-482a-8e3f-9bd7cecba9d8/content)

- **Expert 9 — Ryan S. Baker**
  - **Who:** Professor of AI and Education, Adelaide University; Adjunct Professor and Director of the Penn Center for Learning Analytics, University of Pennsylvania. Built the first automated detector of student disengagement.
  - **Focus:** Mining interaction logs to detect gaming, off-task behavior, carelessness, help misuse, and affect in real time — with no camera, microphone, or sensor.
  - **Why Follow:** The constructive pole on what a learning system can know about a child, and the answer is more than intuition suggests. He is also his own best critic: detectors trained on one population fall to near-chance on another, acting aggressively on a detected behavior can produce a worse one, and his review of algorithmic bias documents uneven failure across race, language, and disability.
  - **Where:** [Penn Center for Learning Analytics](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/index.html) | [Gaming detector (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11257-007-9045-6) | [Algorithmic bias in education (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-021-00285-9)

- **Expert 10 — Robert A. Bjork**
  - **Who:** Distinguished Research Professor of Psychology, UCLA; co-directs the Learning and Forgetting Lab; Fellow of the National Academy of Sciences; past editor of *Psychological Review*.
  - **Focus:** Human learning, memory, and the gap between how people learn and how they think they learn.
  - **Why Follow:** The pole against Baker on what a behavioral record means. Baker's detectors read the learner's current state; Bjork's central finding is that **the current state is the wrong thing to read** — the conditions that most improve visible performance differ systematically from those that build durable learning, and both learner and instructor are fooled. He named the manipulations that slow apparent progress and improve retention "desirable difficulties." Any system optimizing a live accuracy or engagement signal is optimizing the quantity he identifies as misleading.
  - **Where:** [UCLA profile](https://www.psych.ucla.edu/faculty-page/bjork/) | [Learning versus performance (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

## DOK 3: Insights

> **Owner-authored. Not yet written.** A DOK 3 insight is a connection across sources that no single
> source states. If the claim appears in one paper, it belongs in the Knowledge Tree below instead.
>
> `candidate-tensions.md` holds the raw material: roughly eighty recorded contradictions, each naming
> two sources and what each found, none resolved. Turning a contradiction into an insight — deciding
> which side is right, or that both are right under different conditions, or that the disagreement
> exposes a third thing neither side measured — is the work this level exists to do.
>
> Starting questions:
>
> - **The two literatures that never meet.** Education raises measured intelligence; cognitive training
>   does not. Both are well evidenced. Which of the differences between them can software actually have?
> - **Measurement collapsing into intervention.** Repetition alone moves reasoning scores. Performance
>   under support is not learning. Tutoring effects shrink sixfold when the test is standardized rather
>   than written to match the lesson. What can an improvement number produced inside a product mean?
> - **Effort as a signal.** Futile persistence is common. Careless errors cluster among the *most*
>   engaged. Help avoidance predicts nothing; help abuse predicts a lot. What single account explains why
>   nearly every intuitive reading of an effort signal is backwards?
> - **What the deflated constructs share.** Grit dissolves into conscientiousness; mindset survives only
>   in narrow subgroups; deliberate practice explains 4–5% in education. The one that held is defined as
>   a process rather than a possession. Coincidence, or a fact about what is measurable at all?
> - **The pair that may not disagree.** One AI trial found a 17% loss, another a 1.3 SD gain. One measured
>   after withdrawal, one immediately. If they are compatible, what does reconciling them cost in claims?
> - **Who this identifies.** Tutoring systems helped general-population students more than low achievers;
>   AI tutoring helped higher performers most; mindset helped only low achievers. What does a tool built
>   to find rapid improvers do if improvement is easiest to produce in the children who needed it least?

### Category 1: Whether reasoning ability moves at all, and what has been shown to move it

*General idea: Reasoning ability is environmentally movable — schooling and population trends prove it — but no short computerized training program has been shown to move it, and the better the study design, the smaller the effect.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: The founding n-back claim — *did not replicate***
  - **Source:** Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Perrig (2008); Redick, Shipstead, Harrison, Hicks, Fried, Hambrick, Kane & Engle (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Fluid intelligence (Gf)** is reasoning on unfamiliar problems — what a matrix puzzle measures. The **n-back** task shows a stream of items and asks whether the current one matches the item *n* steps back, getting harder as the trainee improves. **Near transfer** is improvement on tasks resembling the trained one; **far transfer** is improvement on tasks unlike it, such as Gf. Effect sizes (**Cohen's d**, **Hedges' g**) are stated in standard deviations: roughly 0.2 small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large.
      - Jaeggi and colleagues trained 70 healthy young adults (mean age 25.6) on adaptive dual n-back across four experiments, leaving 34 trained and 35 untrained controls; the experiments differed only in session count — 8 days (n = 16), 12 (n = 22), 17 (n = 16), 19 (n = 15). `[healthy young adults, not children]`
      - Trained groups gained more on Gf (t(33) = 5.53, P < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.65) than controls, whose smaller significant gain (t(34) = 2.08, P < 0.05, Cohen's d = 0.25) the authors put down to retest practice; the group × test-session interaction was significant (F(1,67) = 5.27, P < 0.05).
      - The gain scaled with training time (F(3,30) = 9.25, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.48), reaching significance at 17 and 19 days but not 8. Lower initial Gf predicted larger gains, and gain did not depend on initial working-memory capacity measured by digit span or reading span.
      - The authors flagged that their study "does not permit us to know how long the training gain persists." **But** the individual experiments were tiny — Redick and colleagues note "n = 7, 8, or 11 in each group of the four studies."
      - Redick and colleagues ran 20 sessions of the same task against both an **active control** (a placebo matched for contact and effort — adaptive visual search) and a **passive** no-contact control, leaving 73 participants aged 18–30. Both trained groups improved on their own task; n-back mean n rose from 2.3 in Session 1 to 4.1 in Session 20. `[young adults 18–30, not children]`
      - **No transfer appeared.** "Out of 17 ANOVAs, there were no significant Group × Session interactions," and reanalyses dropping the visual-search group or the midtest session found none either. They also found "no evidence of a dose-dependent relationship between the amount of dual n-back training practice and fluid intelligence gains" — the 2008 paper's central claim.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Improvement on the trained activity is easy to produce and easy to measure, and is not by itself evidence that reasoning moved. The 2008 result rested on 7 to 11 people per experiment; when Redick and colleagues repeated it for 20 sessions with a placebo control, both the transfer and the dose-response disappeared.
    - **Link to source:** [Improving fluid intelligence with training on working memory](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0801268105), [No evidence of intelligence improvement after working memory training](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029082)

- **Subcategory 1.2: Two meta-analyses of the same literature — *the gap is the control group***
  - **Source:** Au, Sheehan, Tsai, Duncan, Buschkuehl & Jaeggi (2015); Melby-Lervåg & Hulme (2013); Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Au and colleagues pooled 20 n-back studies with Gf outcomes and a control group, healthy participants aged 18–50, yielding 24 comparisons: "a small but statistically significant net effect of n-back training on Gf outcome measures (g = 0.24, SE = .07), about the equivalent of 3–4 points on a standardized IQ test." `[healthy adults 18–50, majority college undergraduates; no children]`
      - Their own moderator analysis found "studies that used passive controls demonstrated more net transfer (g = 0.44, SE = .10) than those with active controls (g = 0.06, SE = .09), a difference that is also statistically significant at p < .01." **But** they conclude "the type of control group used in studies (active or passive) did not moderate the training effect," attributing the gap to geographic region and remuneration instead.
      - Melby-Lervåg and Hulme covered 23 studies, 30 comparisons, spanning clinical samples, typically developing children, and adults. Their immediate effect on nonverbal ability across 22 effect sizes was d = 0.19, 95% CI [0.03, 0.37], p = .02, adjusted up to d = 0.34 by **trim-and-fill** (a publication-bias correction that imputes the studies a lopsided funnel plot implies are missing). Studies with treated control groups "had a mean effect size close to zero." `[mixed ages, includes clinical samples]`
      - Their 2016 review with Redick expanded to 87 publications and 145 experimental comparisons. Against treated controls, nonverbal-ability effects were g = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.13], k = 67; against untreated controls, g = 0.20, 95% CI [0.11, 0.28], k = 53.
      - Near transfer held up against treated controls: criterion measure g = 0.80 [0.62, 0.97], verbal working memory g = 0.31, visuospatial working memory g = 0.28. N-back alone showed "a significant effect on nonverbal ability (g = 0.15, p = .02)" there, though its five largest effect sizes all had fewer than 20 observations per group.
      - Their mediation analysis found "improvements in nonverbal ability were not significantly related to improvements on the near-transfer working memory tasks (β = −.01, p = .86, k = 26)," while control-group type did predict nonverbal gains (β = .27, p = .04).
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two meta-analyses of overlapping literature reported far-transfer effects differing roughly fivefold, and both found the same reason inside their own data: weaker control groups produce bigger effects. Near transfer is real. The mediation result is the load-bearing one — how much a participant's working memory improved did not predict how much their reasoning moved.
    - **Link to source:** [Improving fluid intelligence with training on working memory: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0699-x), [Is working memory training effective? A meta-analytic review](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028228), [Working memory training does not improve performance on measures of intelligence or other measures of far transfer](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616635612)

- **Subcategory 1.3: Comprehensive reviews — *the better the design, the smaller the effect***
  - **Source:** Simons, Boot, Charness, Gathercole, Chabris, Hambrick & Stine-Morrow (2016); Sala & Gobet (2019); Sala, Aksaylı, Tatlidil, Tatsumi, Gondo & Gobet (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Simons and colleagues reviewed every published peer-reviewed intervention study cited on leading brain-training companies' own websites, since "these citations presumably represent the evidence that best supports the claims of effectiveness." The review was prompted by duelling open letters: a consensus statement from more than 70 scientists saying brain games do not provide a scientifically grounded way to improve cognitive functioning, and a counter-letter from 133 scientists and practitioners saying the opposite.
      - Their verdict on that best-case list: "extensive evidence that brain-training interventions improve performance on the trained tasks, less evidence that such interventions improve performance on closely related tasks, and little evidence that training enhances performance on distantly related tasks or that training improves everyday cognitive performance." **And** "None of the cited studies conformed to all of the best practices we identify as essential to drawing clear conclusions about the benefits of brain training for everyday activities."
      - Sala and Gobet report results "highly consistent across all the reviewed domains: minimal effect on domain-general cognitive skills," with "the observed between-study variability… accounted for by design quality and statistical artefacts" rather than by real differences between programs. `[review of the authors' own prior meta-analyses]`
      - They state the gradient plainly: "the better the design, the smaller the size of the effect – militates in favour of the null hypothesis according to which brain-training programs do not exert any appreciable effects on cognition."
      - Sala and colleagues' second-order meta-analysis (a meta-analysis of existing meta-analyses) modeled working-memory near transfer (k = 99), working-memory far transfer (k = 119), and far transfer across working-memory, video-game, music, chess, and exergame training (k = 233). "Crucially, when placebo effects and publication bias were controlled for, the overall effect size and true variance equaled zero. That is, no impact on far-transfer measures was observed regardless of the type of population and cognitive-training program."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Handed the industry's own best-case citation list, the reviewers still found gains only on the trained task. Sala and colleagues show the spread between studies is explained by design quality rather than by which program was used, and that once placebo effects and publication bias are removed, both the far-transfer effect and its variance are zero.
    - **Link to source:** [Do "brain-training" programs work?](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100616661983), [Cognitive training does not enhance general cognition](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.10.004), [Near and far transfer in cognitive training: a second-order meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.203)

- **Subcategory 1.4: Children, and transfer conditional on training gain — *no usable threshold***
  - **Source:** Sala & Gobet (2017); Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Shah (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sala and Gobet found that in typically developing children aged 3 to 16, working-memory training produced near transfer of g = 0.46, 95% CI [0.35, 0.57], k = 30, but far transfer of only g = 0.12, 95% CI [0.06, 0.18], k = 74. `[typically developing children only]`
      - Design quality drove that far-transfer number down further. Random allocation was a significant moderator (randomized g = 0.07, k = 50, p = .046; non-randomized g = 0.27, k = 24, p < .001), and "the overall effect size in randomized samples with active control groups was g = 0.03, CI [−0.07; 0.14], k = 34, p = .521." Far-transfer follow-up effects were g = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.20], k = 24, p = .122.
      - Of six outcome domains only mathematics reached significance (g = 0.20, k = 17, p = .018), and there the active-control estimate was g = −0.11, k = 6, p = .426 against passive-control g = 0.31, k = 11, p = .001; trim-and-fill on the fluid-intelligence model gave g = 0.03, 95% CI [−0.09, 0.15].
      - Jaeggi and colleagues trained 62 children (mean age about 9) for one month: 32 on adaptive spatial n-back, 30 on an active-control knowledge-and-vocabulary task. The trained group improved on its task (t(31) = 6.38, P < 0.001) and the control did not, yet **there was no Gf transfer for the group as a whole** (F(1,59) < 1, ns). `[children aged ~9; n = 16 per training-gain subgroup after post hoc median split]`
      - Splitting the trained group at the median of training gain after the fact, "only those participants above the median in WM training improvement showed transfer to measures of Gf" (group × session F(2,58) = 3.23, P < 0.05); the small-gain half was indistinguishable from the active control. Training-task improvement correlated with Gf improvement (r = 0.42, P < 0.05), and the subgroup difference held at a 3-month follow-up (P < 0.05).
      - Both subgroups enjoyed the training equally, but the children who improved least rated the game as more difficult and effortful, while big improvers found it "challenging but not overwhelming" (t(28) = 2.05; P = 0.05).
      - Pretest and initial training performance did not differ between the two subgroups, and the large-gain group "do not end up with significantly higher Gf scores at posttest." **The authors' own caveat:** the median split gives no "clearly defined training criterion a participant has to reach in order to show transfer effects."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** In children, far transfer falls to g = 0.03 once designs are randomized with an active control. The one positive signal — that only children who improved a lot on the training task gained on reasoning — comes from a post hoc median split with 16 per cell, and its authors say it yields no threshold for predicting who will transfer.
    - **Link to source:** [Working memory training in typically developing children: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000265), [Short- and long-term benefits of cognitive training](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1103228108)

- **Subcategory 1.5: Education's causal effect on measured intelligence — *real, but the dose is a year of school***
  - **Source:** Ritchie & Tucker-Drob (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A **quasi-experimental design** uses a natural accident — a change in compulsory-schooling law, a birthday cutoff for school entry — to approximate random assignment where a real trial is impossible. Ritchie and Tucker-Drob pooled "142 effect sizes from 42 data sets involving over 600,000 participants" across three such designs: controlling for earlier intelligence (26 effect sizes, N = 51,645), compulsory-schooling policy changes used as instrumental variables (30 effect sizes, N = 456,963), and regression discontinuity at school-entry age cutoffs (86 effect sizes, N = 107,204). `[quasi-experimental, not randomized; the treatment is a year of institutional schooling, not a software program]`
      - One additional year of education raised IQ by 1.197 points (SE = 0.203, p = 3.84×10⁻⁰⁹) in the prior-intelligence design, 2.056 points (SE = 0.583, p = 4.23×10⁻⁰⁴) in the policy-change design, and 5.229 points (SE = 0.530, p = 6.33×10⁻²³) in the school-age-cutoff design; an overall model across all three gave 3.394 points (SE = 0.503, p = 1.55×10⁻¹¹).
      - The estimates survive tightening. A maximal-covariate analysis, possible for 27 of the 142 effect sizes, still gave 0.903 and 1.852 IQ points; four publication-bias tests "broadly indicated minimal systematic bias," with p-curve indicating evidential value for all three designs; PET-PEESE correction gave 1.091, 1.371, and 4.244 points.
      - "Moderator analyses indicated that the effects persisted across the life span and were present on all broad categories of cognitive ability studied." **But** by test type the prior-intelligence design was stronger on composite tests (1.876 points, p = 5.94×10⁻⁰⁵) than on fluid tests (0.836 points), a significant difference (1.039 points, p = .036), while the policy-change design found the two comparable (composite 2.314, p = .008; fluid 2.272, p = .003).
      - Their conclusion: "Education appears to be the most consistent, robust, and durable method yet to be identified for raising intelligence."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest causal evidence that anything raises measured intelligence: three independent designs, more than 600,000 participants, 1.197 to 5.229 IQ points per additional year of school, durable across the life span. The caveat is dose. The treatment is a year of full-time institutional schooling, not a computerized exercise.
    - **Link to source:** [How much does education improve intelligence? A meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618774253)

- **Subcategory 1.6: Fadeout of intervention-induced IQ gains — *gains decay to nothing***
  - **Source:** Protzko (2015); Bailey, Duncan, Odgers & Yu (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Protzko's abstract covers "7584 participants across 39 randomized controlled trials, using a mixed-effects analysis with growth curve modeling," while the Results section describes 23 fully randomized trials yielding 39 different interventions. Inclusion required nonclinical participants, individual-level randomization, a widely accepted IQ measure, at least two IQ measurements after the intervention ended, and a start before kindergarten. `[abstract and Results give different study counts; both recorded verbatim]` `[early interventions beginning before kindergarten; IQ outcomes only]`
      - Interventions raised intelligence by the time they ended (ES = .368, 95% CI = .212 to .523), and "over time, the salutary effects an early intervention declined to nothing (b = −.109/year, 95% CI = −.199 to −.019)." Restricting to the 17 studies (1,758 participants) whose interventions worked gave ES = .523 at intervention end and a steeper decline of b = −.132/year.
      - The mechanism matters more than the size: "We further show this is because children in the experimental group lose their IQ advantage and not because those in the control groups catch up," with control-group scores staying stable over time.
      - Neither starting earlier nor training longer helped. "Interventions that started earlier in a child's life were no more effective than those which started later in a child's life, nor did they affect how long the effects lasted (both ps > .15)," and "duration played no appreciable role in explaining the fadeout effect."
      - Bailey and colleagues found the same shape across 67 high-quality early childhood education interventions: "At the end of the programs, effect sizes averaged .23 standard deviations… Post-treatment impacts measured no more than 12 months after the end of treatment had dropped by more than half, to .10 sd, and again by half one to two years later." Perry Preschool's "large end-of-treatment impact on IQ (.75 sd) at age 5 had dropped to a statistically insignificant .08 sd by age 8." `[the authors' own calculations from an existing meta-analytic database]`
      - **But** the Abecedarian Project "generated IQ impacts that persisted well beyond age 8," which the authors say "suggests that fadeout patterns based on cross-study average impacts are likely to conceal study-to-study variation."
      - Bailey and colleagues argue lasting effects require "trifecta" skills — "ones that are malleable, fundamental, and would not have developed eventually in the absence of the intervention" — and warn that because IQ impacts are inconsistent, "training designed to enhance performance on a specific cognitive skill or domain may be unlikely to enhance children's general learning."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Early interventions do raise IQ, and then the treated children lose the advantage at roughly 0.11 to 0.13 standard deviations a year while control scores stay flat — so this is decay, not control-group catch-up and not test familiarity. Starting earlier or training longer changes nothing. Abecedarian is the exception showing averages hide real variation.
    - **Link to source:** [The environment in raising early intelligence: a meta-analysis of the fadeout effect](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2015.10.006), [Persistence and fadeout in the impacts of child and adolescent interventions](https://doi.org/10.1080/19345747.2016.1232459)

- **Subcategory 1.7: The Flynn effect and its reversal — *scores move at population scale***
  - **Source:** Trahan, Stuebing, Fletcher & Hiscock (2014); Pietschnig & Voracek (2015); Bratsberg & Rogeberg (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Trahan and colleagues, across 285 studies (N = 14,031) since 1951 that gave two intelligence tests with different normative bases, found a mean gain of 2.31 standard score points per decade, 95% CI [1.99, 2.64]; across the 53 comparisons using modern Stanford-Binet and Wechsler tests it was 2.93 IQ points per decade, "comparable to previous estimates of about 3 points per decade, but not consistent with the hypothesis that the Flynn effect is diminishing." `[comparisons of test norms, not an intervention]`
      - Pietschnig and Voracek's century-scale meta-analysis — 219 studies, 271 independent samples, 3,987,892 participants, 31 countries, 105 years — found "0.41, 0.30, 0.28, and 0.21 IQ points annually for fluid, spatial, full-scale, and crystallized IQ test performance, respectively," the largest gain in fluid ability. **But** they report gains "are stronger for adults than children, and have decreased in more recent decades," contradicting Trahan and colleagues on whether the effect is fading. `[secular norm changes across countries; includes patient samples]`
      - Bratsberg and Rogeberg's Norwegian conscription data — male birth cohorts 1962–1991, tested at age 18–19 on an aggregate of three speeded tests — show the full arc: "The average IQ score from these tests rose from 99.5 for the 1962 birth cohort to 102.3 for the 1975 cohort, after which it declined to 99.4 for the 1989 cohort." `[Norwegian males only; ability scores not available for women]`
      - Both the rise and the fall appear between brothers raised in the same family. For 1962–1975, "the average within-family Flynn effect is 0.26 IQ point per year… similar to the 0.28 estimated annual gain for full-scale IQ from a metaanalysis based on 271 independent samples from 31 countries." For 1975–1991, the within-family decline was −0.08 IQ point per year versus −0.23 across families in the standard fixed-effects model, and −0.33 versus −0.34 in the selection-corrected Bayesian model.
      - That rules out composition effects: the trends "are not due to a changing composition of families, and that there is at most a minor role for explanations involving genes… and environmental factors largely fixed within families." **But** the design cannot say which environmental factors, and the authors list "changes in educational exposure or quality, changing media exposure, worsening nutrition or health, and social spillovers from increased immigration" as all still consistent.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Measured fluid reasoning moves at population scale, and the Norwegian brother comparisons show the movement is environmental rather than genetic or compositional. That settles that the trait is not fixed. It says nothing about whether any particular program can move it in an individual child.
    - **Link to source:** [The Flynn effect: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037173), [One century of global IQ gains](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615577701), [Flynn effect and its reversal are both environmentally caused](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1718793115)

- **Subcategory 1.8: High heritability does not imply low malleability — *the inference is invalid***
  - **Source:** Turkheimer, Haley, Waldron, D'Onofrio & Gottesman (2003); Sauce & Matzel (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Heritability (h²)** is the share of variation in a trait, within one particular population, that tracks genetic variation; **c²** is the share tracking the shared family environment. Neither is a ceiling on how much the trait can change.
      - Turkheimer and colleagues analyzed Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children scores in 319 pairs of 7-year-old twins from the National Collaborative Perinatal Project, a substantial proportion raised near or below the poverty level. "The models suggest that in impoverished families, 60% of the variance in IQ is accounted for by the shared environment, and the contribution of genes is close to zero; in affluent families, the result is almost exactly the reverse." `[US sample of 7-year-old twins with substantial representation of poverty; a single cohort]`
      - On a median split the low-SES half was consistent with h² of .10 and c² of .58, the high-SES half with h² of .72 and c² of .15, demonstrating that "the proportions of IQ variance attributable to genes and environment vary nonlinearly with SES." The heritability estimate therefore depends on which environments the sample occupies.
      - Sauce and Matzel state the point directly — "Intelligence can have an extremely high heritability, but also be malleable; a paradox that has been the source of continuous controversy" — and argue that gene-environment interplay "is difficult to specify with current methods, and is underestimated in standard metrics of heritability (thus inflating estimates of 'genetic' effects)." `[theoretical review synthesizing existing evidence, not new data]`
      - Their evidence for malleability existing "on top of heritability" covers "cognitive gains consequent to adoption/immigration, changes in IQ's heritability across life span and socioeconomic status, gains in IQ over time consequent to societal development (the Flynn effect), the slowdown of age-related cognitive decline, and the gains in intelligence from early education." They treat "the loss of initial benefits from early intervention programs (such as 'Head Start')" as a phenomenon their account must explain, not as evidence against malleability.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** "Reasoning is highly heritable, so a program cannot move it" is not a valid argument: heritability is a population-specific variance ratio that ran from .10 to .72 across the poor and affluent halves of a single twin sample. **But** neither source demonstrates that any particular program does move it.
    - **Link to source:** [Socioeconomic status modifies heritability of IQ in young children](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.0956-7976.2003.psci_1475.x), [The paradox of intelligence: heritability and malleability coexist in hidden gene-environment interplay](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000131)

### Category 2: Training reasoning directly — the inductive-reasoning programs, and the transfer problem

*This category settles what instruction can actually move on a reasoning test: real gains on trained item classes, near-zero far transfer once placebo and publication bias are removed, and a retest floor of roughly a third of a standard deviation.*

- **Subcategory 2.1: What Klauer's training actually trains — *six item classes, not a general ability***
  - **Source:** Klauer & Phye (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Inductive reasoning** — working out the rule behind a set of items — is decomposed into six problem classes: generalization, discrimination, cross-classification, recognizing relationships, differentiating relationships, and system construction. The six are generated by crossing two facets: whether the learner examines attributes of objects or relations among them, and whether the learner looks for similarities, differences, or both.
      - Each class maps onto an item format found on intelligence tests: generalization to class formation, class expansion, and finding common attributes; discrimination to identifying disturbing attributes; cross-classification to 4-fold, 6-fold, and 9-fold schemes; recognizing relationships to series completion, ordered series, and analogy; differentiating relationships to disturbed series; and system construction to matrices.
      - Three age-graded manuals exist — Program I for ages 5 through 8, Program II for 11 through 13, Program III for 14 through 16 — and the standard dose is 10 lessons at two per week over 5 weeks, with testing one week before and one week after, for a 7-week study.
      - The trained content is a comparison procedure taught two ways: an analytic strategy that checks objects predicate by predicate for commonalities and diversities, and a heuristic one in which the learner inspects globally, builds a hypothesis, and tests it by directed comparisons before falling back on the analytic scan.
      - The theory is prescriptive rather than descriptive: it specifies how to proceed to solve inductive problems efficiently, not what learners commonly do.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Klauer trains a comparison procedure, not a general ability. The six classes he teaches are the item formats intelligence tests already use — analogies, series, matrices — so any score gain has to be read against that overlap. The authors say as much themselves: the theory prescribes how to solve such problems rather than describing what learners do.
    - **Link to source:** [Inductive reasoning: a training approach](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313402)

- **Subcategory 2.2: How far Klauer's training transferred, and how long it lasted — *half a standard deviation, on tests built from the trained material***
  - **Source:** Klauer & Phye (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The **meta-analysis** — a statistical pooling of separate studies — covered 74 training experiments with nearly 3,600 children. On **fluid intelligence** tests (reasoning through unfamiliar problems rather than recalling taught knowledge), 79 contrasts from 73 experiments (N = 3,595) gave a weighted mean **effect size** of d+ = 0.52, 95% CI [0.46, 0.59]. d+ is an average standardized gap between trained and untrained groups, so 0.52 is about half a standard deviation, or roughly 20 percentile ranks for the average participant in the authors' own translation. The unweighted mean was Mg = 0.59 ± 0.31, with individual effects from −0.05 to 1.30.
      - Transfer to academic learning of newly taught classroom subject matter was larger, not smaller: 38 contrasts (N = 1,723) gave d+ = 0.69, 95% CI [0.59, 0.79], significantly above the 0.52 intelligence-test effect (p < .05) and, in the authors' words, "in contrast to expectations." It peaked in special education at d+ = 0.94, 95% CI [0.74, 1.14] (n = 13, N = 434), and for Program III at d+ = 0.84 (n = 11).
      - Durability: 22 experiments with 1,094 students ran a follow-up posttest 3 to 15 months after the first, and the partial correlation between follow-up score and elapsed months, pretest partialed out, was r₁₂.₃ = .44 (df = 19), p = .045 — read by the authors as effects not diminishing and even increasing slightly. In a placebo test, nine three-group experiments comparing an alternatively trained group with a no-training control yielded d+ = 0.004 (n = 9, N = 230), not significantly different from zero, so the gains are not simply attention and social contact.
      - Subgroup effects on intelligence tests were Program I d+ = 0.57 (n = 42), Program II 0.43 (n = 24), Program III 0.50 (n = 13); by setting, kindergarten 0.47, school kindergarten 0.43, primary school 0.61, secondary school 0.42, special education 0.54; by grouping, one-to-one 0.34 (n = 11), pairs 0.59 (n = 15), small groups 0.57 (n = 36), whole classes 0.51 (n = 17). **Conflict of interest:** studies authored by Klauer's own staff yielded d+ = 0.57 (n = 35) against 0.49 (n = 44) for other authors, and the meta-analysis of the program was conducted by the man who designed it.
      - The intelligence tests were themselves made of the trained material: entirely inductive reasoning items (Raven Coloured, Standard, and Advanced Progressive Matrices), or tests carrying inductive subtests (Cattell Culture Fair, German Cognitive Ability Test, German Columbia Mental Maturity Test). The authors state the data refer to tests that "in part, or in their entirety, measure inductive reasoning." Where pretest, posttest, and follow-up were identical they note this "leads to considerable retest effects" — gains from having simply seen the test before — unconfounded with training only where a no-training or alternatively trained control existed.
      - **But** they reject a pure **coaching** account, a score gain bought by rehearsing the item format, testing it indirectly against two criteria borrowed from Hasselhorn (1995) — durability, and transfer to other variables — both judged fulfilled. Only a few of the 71 published articles appeared in English-language journals, the search terms included "Denktraining," the German label of the programs, and three of the 74 experiments were unpublished theses. `[predominantly German-language and European school samples; the meta-analysis was conducted by the program's own author]`
      - Klauer's own later extension, covering 109 studies and N = 4,668, reports d = 0.60 on fluid intelligence, d = 0.68 on learning in classroom lessons, and d = 0.65 on further cognitive variables. `[German-language journal; extension authored by the program's originator]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest pro-training result available, and it is compromised in one specific way: the intelligence tests were built from the very item classes the program teaches. The placebo result and the flat 3-to-15-month follow-ups are genuinely hard to dismiss. **Conflict of interest:** the program's designer conducted the meta-analysis of his own program.
    - **Link to source:** [Inductive reasoning: a training approach](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313402), [Extension of the 2008 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123)

- **Subcategory 2.3: An independent test of the same program — *a fraction of the claimed effect***
  - **Source:** Baumeister & Rindermann (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Two field studies tested Klauer's Program I with special-needs students, one using the paper-and-pencil version and one the PC version. `[refereed conference proceedings; small special-needs samples in Austria and Germany]`
      - The paper-and-pencil version (N₁ = 34 students; mean age 8 years 9 months) produced no improvement in cognitive abilities (CFT 1: d_mean = −0.17; β_mean Train→Int = −.08) or verbal abilities (HSET: d_mean = −0.02, β_mean Train→Verb = .04).
      - The PC version (N₂ = 27; mean age 7 years 8 months) produced a small positive effect on intelligence (CFT 1: d_mean = 0.28; β_mean Train→Int = .13) and on verbal performance (HSET: d_mean = 0.25, β_mean Train→Verb = .12).
      - The authors attribute the slight, relatively stable increases in the paper-and-pencil study to naturally occurring developmental effects and to a retest or practice effect from getting used to working on the performance tests, not to the training. They also argue the small PC effects are practically meaningful, because they correspond to more than half a year of schooling and maturation.
      - They cite the Klauer and Phye figures as d = 0.52 for intelligence gains across 78 evaluation studies and d = 0.69 across 38 studies for academic learning, and position their own work as testing the program with a heterogeneous special-needs group rather than the homogeneous samples used previously.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The nearest thing to an independent field test of Klauer's program found at most half the meta-analytic figure its own authors cite, and slightly negative on paper. Their reading of the movement they did see — maturation plus test familiarity rather than training — is the one that matters for measurement. Small special-needs samples, so this bounds the claim rather than overturning it.
    - **Link to source:** [Klauer's inductive reasoning training as a cognitive apprenticeship approach for special-needs students](https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2014.38)

- **Subcategory 2.4: Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment — *modest effects, mostly on tests resembling the training materials***
  - **Source:** Romney & Samuels (2001); Savell, Twohig & Rachford (1986)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Romney and Samuels pooled 40 controlled studies of Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment comprising 47 different samples and found significant but "modest" average effect sizes in all three claimed areas — achievement, ability, and behaviour — with the most extensive improvement in ability. Their stated motivation was that "some outcome studies have produced negative results."
      - In the same analysis, gains in spatial/perceptual ability were related to the length of the intervention in hours, and self-esteem was related to age, older children showing increases and younger children decreases.
      - Savell, Twohig and Rachford, reviewing some 35 reports of empirical research on the program, state the effects observed have "for the most part" been on standard nonverbal measures of intelligence — PMA, Lorge-Thorndike, Cattell, Raven's — which they describe as largely measures of skill in processing figural and spatial information.
      - Effects on self-concept, classroom behavior, impulsivity, academic achievement, and course content were "either absent, inconsistent, or difficult to interpret," with few demonstrations of effects in real-life, including academic, or simulated real-life situations.
      - In Feuerstein's original Israeli two-year field experiment — roughly 45 minutes of the program 3–5 days a week for two years, an estimated 200–300 hours, as an adjunct to the usual curriculum — experimental/control differences were larger and more clear-cut on the measures most similar in content to the training materials, prompting the reviewers to ask "whether the study produced anything more than near-transfer or 'practice effects'." **Near transfer** means the gain appears on tasks resembling what was trained; **far transfer** means it appears on something genuinely different.
      - Studies tending to show statistically reliable treatment effects usually provided a greater dosage than those tending not to — generally 80 hours or more of student exposure over a 1- or 2-year period, plus at least a week of instructor training with follow-up support — yet Savell and colleagues state there had been no research on dosage, and that it is not clear what dosage is required to produce what kinds of effects, in what magnitude, in what kinds of subjects.
      - Effects were observed almost entirely in individuals in primary or secondary school and in the 12- to 18-year age range at the time of exposure. `[ages 12–18, not younger children]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The second training tradition is weaker and more equivocal than Klauer's. Romney and Samuels find modest but real effects; Savell and colleagues find them concentrated on figural nonverbal tests and absent or uninterpretable everywhere else. Their question about Feuerstein's own Israeli trial is the one that carries: were the biggest gains simply near transfer on look-alike measures?
    - **Link to source:** [A meta-analytic evaluation of Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment program](https://doi.org/10.53841/bpsecp.2001.18.4.19), [Empirical status of Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment technique](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543056004381)

- **Subcategory 2.5: Teaching for broader intelligence raised achievement — *but no intelligence test was given***
  - **Source:** Sternberg, Torff & Grigorenko (1998)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Two studies tested triarchic instruction with 213 third-grade students taught a social-studies unit and 141 eighth-grade students taught a psychology unit.
      - Students were taught in one of three ways: traditional instruction (primarily memory-based), critical-thinking instruction (primarily analytically based), and triarchically based instruction infusing analytical, creative, and practical work.
      - Performance was assessed with multiple-choice items measuring primarily memory and performance-based items measuring analytical, creative, and practical aspects of achievement; in general triarchic instruction was superior to the other modes, even on the multiple-choice memory-based items.
      - The reported outcome variables are school achievement measures within the taught units, not scores on a standardized fluid-intelligence or cognitive-abilities test. `[achievement outcomes only; no intelligence-test outcome reported]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Instruction mixing analytical, creative, and practical work beat both conventional memory-based teaching and critical-thinking teaching, and it won even on the memory-based multiple-choice items, the comparison least favourable to the authors' own hypothesis. **But** the outcomes are achievement inside the taught units. This shows teaching changes what is learned, not that measured reasoning ability moved.
    - **Link to source:** [Teaching triarchically improves school achievement](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.374)

- **Subcategory 2.6: "Transfer" names nine dimensions, not one — *so a single pooled far-transfer number is the wrong question***
  - **Source:** Barnett & Ceci (2002); Salomon & Perkins (1989)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Barnett and Ceci argue that a century of research on far transfer has made little progress toward resolution because studies fail to specify the various dimensions along which transfer can occur, producing comparisons of "apples and oranges."
      - Their framework describes 9 relevant dimensions in two global factors: content, meaning what is transferred, and context, meaning when and where it is transferred from and to. The three content dimensions are specificity of the learned skill, nature of the performance change (speed, accuracy, or approach), and memory demands of the transfer task.
      - The six context dimensions are each a near-to-far continuum: knowledge domain, physical context, temporal context, functional context, social context, and modality. Published anchors run from mouse vs. rat (near) through science vs. cooking (far) for knowledge domain; same session through years later for temporal context; and both clearly academic through academic vs. at play for functional context.
      - Barnett and Ceci state that estimation of a single effect size for far transfer is misguided in view of this complexity, and conclude that the past 100 years shows substantial evidence for transfer under some conditions while critical conditions for many key questions are untested.
      - Salomon and Perkins supply the mechanism. Low-road transfer depends on extensive, varied practice and occurs by automatic triggering of well-learned behavior in a new context. High-road transfer occurs by intentional mindful abstraction of something from one context and its application in a new one, split into forward-reaching transfer, abstracting in anticipation of later application, and backward-reaching transfer, facing a new situation and deliberately searching for relevant prior knowledge. Findings of transfer or nontransfer, they state, reflect whether either route's conditions were met.
      - In Perkins and Salomon's instructional formulation, "hugging" means teaching so the learning experience approximates the target performance, and "bridging" means mediating abstraction, connection-searching, mindfulness, and metacognition. That text states "a superficial look at how research on transfer casts its vote is discouraging" and that "the preponderance of studies suggest that transfer comes hard," but concludes "education can achieve abundant transfer if it is designed to do so." `[reference-work entry, not a peer-reviewed journal article]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two studies can both be about "transfer" and share almost nothing, which is why the literature deadlocks. Barnett and Ceci supply nine dimensions so a study can be located rather than labelled, and say outright that any single pooled far-transfer effect size is misguided. Salomon and Perkins add why: a finding of no transfer usually means a precondition was missing.
    - **Link to source:** [When and where do we apply what we learn? A taxonomy for far transfer](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612), [Rocky roads to transfer: rethinking mechanisms of a neglected phenomenon](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep2402_1)

- **Subcategory 2.7: The case against transfer — *far transfer goes to zero once placebo and publication bias are removed***
  - **Source:** Detterman (1993); Sala, Aksaylı, Tatlidil, Tatsumi, Gondo & Gobet (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Detterman defines transfer as "the degree to which a behavior will be repeated in a new situation," and concludes: "First, most studies fail to find transfer. Second, those studies claiming transfer can only be said to have found transfer by the most generous of criteria and would not meet the classical definition of transfer…. Transfer is rare, and its likelihood of occurrence is directly related to the similarity between two situations." `[edited academic book chapter, not a peer-reviewed journal article]`
      - In every study he knows of that claims transfer, he says, it is produced by "tricks" — most often just telling the subject to transfer via hints or outright suggestions, or manipulations that call attention in obvious ways to what the experimenter expects. He writes that "Cognitive psychologists, and other people who should know better, continue to advocate a philosophy of education that is totally lacking in empirical support." `[edited academic book chapter]`
      - His historical anchors: after 1,000 to 2,000 practice trials estimating rectangle areas, Thorndike and Woodworth's (1901) subjects made errors on a transfer series of non-rectangular shapes about 90% as large as before training; Thorndike (1924) found Latin and geometry no more useful for improving reasoning than utilitarian courses like bookkeeping; and with formally identical isomorphic problems there is no significant transfer in one order and only some in the reverse order when subjects are given hints about problem similarity. `[edited academic book chapter; college-student samples in the isomorph study]`
      - Sala and colleagues pooled statistically independent first-order meta-analyses rather than individual studies — a second-order meta-analysis — covering cognitive-training programs across children, adults, and older adults: Model 1 k = 99 first-order effects, Model 2 k = 119, Model 3 k = 233.
      - Model 1, near transfer from working-memory training to memory tasks, gave a grand mean of g̿ = 0.19, with typically developing children highest at ḡᵢ = 0.49 and adults at 0.14. (g is a standardized mean difference like d, corrected for small-sample bias.)
      - Model 2, working-memory training to far-transfer measures including fluid reasoning, speed, and language, gave first-order means of 0.13 for typically developing children, 0.12 for learning-disabled children, 0.12 for adults, and 0.13 for older adults; after ruling out placebo effects and publication bias the overall effect was g̿ = 0.01 with τ² = 0. Model 3 added working-memory, action and non-action video-game, music, chess, and exergame training (k = 233) and put the unbiased overall effect at g̿ = 0.00, with second-order sampling error accounting for the observed between-meta-analysis variance (σ² = 0) in all models.
      - Chess alone gave ḡ = 0.13, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.27], k = 9, p = .089, and among active-control studies only ḡ = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.15, 0.25], k = 3, p = .623. The authors state: "The lack of generalization of skills acquired by training is thus an invariant of human cognition." Only immediate post-training effects were included; due to their dearth, no follow-up effects were. `[no delayed follow-up effects modeled; covers working-memory, video-game, music, chess, and exergame training — Klauer-style inductive-reasoning programs are not among the regimens]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest case against everything above. Detterman sets a strict bar and argues the literature clears it only with hints that tell subjects to transfer; Sala and colleagues show far transfer falling from an uncorrected 0.12–0.13 to 0.01 and then 0.00 once placebo effects and publication bias are removed, across five training regimens. **But** Klauer-style training is not among those regimens.
    - **Link to source:** [The case for the prosecution: transfer as an epiphenomenon](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/1993-detterman.pdf), [Near and far transfer in cognitive training: a second-order meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.203)

- **Subcategory 2.8: What coaching actually buys on an aptitude test — *10 to 30 points, at steeply rising cost***
  - **Source:** Messick & Jungeblut (1981); DerSimonian & Laird (1983); Powers & Rock (1999)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Messick and Jungeblut found rank-order correlations between student contact time in **coaching** programs — instruction aimed specifically at raising a test score — and size of score effect upwards of .7 for both SAT-Verbal and SAT-Mathematics.
      - The relationship was nonlinear: arithmetically increasing amounts of score effect required geometrically increasing amounts of contact time. On their logarithmic models, the contact time needed for average score increases of more than 20–30 points on a 200–800 point scale rapidly approaches that of full-time schooling.
      - They describe the reviewed studies as methodologically flawed in a variety of ways, with attendant noise obscuring the results, and the time-dimension data as fragmentary. Contact time, they note, may also be serving as a proxy for increasing curriculum emphasis on content knowledge and skill development, as opposed to item review and practice.
      - DerSimonian and Laird found that studies comparing coached students' gains to national norms yielded estimated coaching effects four to five times greater than corresponding effects from matched or randomized evaluations, and that the matched or randomized evaluations were far more consistent with one another than the less well-controlled ones.
      - They conclude the data support a positive effect of coaching on SAT scores, but that the effect estimated from the matched or randomized studies — 10 points — "seems too small to be practically important."
      - Powers and Rock analysed a College Board-sponsored survey of a nationally representative sample of 1995–96 SAT takers, more than 4,000 examinees, about 500 of whom had attended formal coaching programs outside their schools. Using seven statistical models and discounting two outlier estimates, they state coaching affects SAT I verbal scores by about 6–8 points and math scores by about 13–18 points, about twice as much as for verbal, and that by commonly used standards these effects can be regarded as small.
      - All of their estimates suggested coaching effects are far less than major commercial test preparation companies claim, and that the revised SAT is no more coachable than its predecessor. **But** the What Works Clearinghouse records that this study does not meet its group design standards, because equivalence of the analytic intervention and comparison groups prior to the intervention was necessary and not demonstrated. `[federal review document; the underlying study is an observational survey with no random assignment]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Instruction aimed straight at an aptitude score moves it very little. The best-controlled estimates are 10 points, and 6–8 verbal against 13–18 math; weaker designs inflate that four- to five-fold. Gains scale logarithmically with hours, so pushing past 20–30 points costs contact time approaching full-time schooling. Commercial claims sit far above all of it.
    - **Link to source:** [Time and method in coaching for the SAT](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.89.2.191), [Evaluating the effect of coaching on SAT scores: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.53.1.n06j5h5356217648), [Effects of coaching on SAT I: Reasoning Test scores](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1999.tb00549.x)

- **Subcategory 2.9: Score gains with no instruction at all — *the floor any "reasoning improved" claim must clear***
  - **Source:** Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007); Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018); Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Hausknecht and colleagues summarized 50 studies of practice effects for tests of cognitive ability — 107 samples, 134,436 participants. The samples gave a sample-weighted mean d of .24, or an overall mean δ of .26 corrected for sampling error; from the first to the third administration, d = .51 with δ = .56. In percentile terms, a candidate at the 50th percentile on the first test would be expected at the 60th on the second and the 71st on the third.
      - Studies including some form of test coaching had an overall effect of δ = .70 against δ = .24 for studies with none, with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals. Regression predicted the identical-form effect would equal the alternate-form effect after an interval of 685 days, just under two years. Formal instruction contact time was not a statistically significant predictor of practice effects; test form and coaching time were.
      - As many as 25% to 50% of applicants in organizational and educational settings are retested with measures of cognitive ability. `[organizational and educational selection settings, predominantly adults]`
      - Scharfen, Peters and Holling detected 174 samples from 122 studies, producing 786 test outcomes and an overall N = 153,185. The standardized mean change from the first to the second administration was SMCR₁.₂ = 0.327, 95% CI [0.278, 0.375], p < .001 — SMCR expresses the gain in pretest standard deviations — rising to SMCR₁.₃ = 0.495 and SMCR₁.₄ = 0.516.
      - Between consecutive administrations, SMCR₂.₃ = 0.169, p < .001, while SMCR₃.₄ = 0.021, 95% CI [−0.020, 0.062], p = 0.318, not significantly different from zero; the authors state there are no further score gains after the third administration. For reasoning tests specifically, SMCR₁.₂ = 0.326, 95% CI [0.248, 0.404], p < .001, with reasoning SMCR₁.₃ = 0.549 and SMCR₁.₄ = 0.710.
      - Identical test forms produced SMCR₁.₂ = 0.372 versus 0.226 for alternate forms, a difference of 0.15 (p < .001), and test form mattered less with each further administration. Participant age was significantly negatively associated with the retest effect — first-to-second intercept 0.398 with β = −0.003 per year, and comparable negative slopes for later comparisons — while the sample itself was of comparably low age (mean age M₁.₂ = 28.92 years) and of general intelligence above average (M₁.₂ = 0.69 standard deviations). `[mean age ~29 years — predominantly adults, not children]`
      - Lievens, Reeve and Heggestad analysed 941 candidates who took a cognitive ability test in a high-stakes context, using a latent variable approach to detect measurement and predictive bias. Both the within-group between-occasions and between-groups within-occasion comparisons showed no measurement bias at initial testing, but retesting induced both measurement and predictive bias: the factor underlying retest scores was less saturated with **g** — the general-intelligence factor shared across cognitive tests — and more associated with memory than the factor underlying initial scores, and these changes eliminated the test's **criterion-related validity**, its ability to predict the outcome it is used to predict. `[adult applicants in high-stakes personnel selection, not children]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Taking the same kind of test twice buys 0.33 of a standard deviation on reasoning, and about 0.55 by the third sitting, with no instruction claimed. That is the floor. Lievens and colleagues show the retest score is also a different thing — less g, more memory — and that shift erased the test's criterion-related validity. Younger testers gain more.
    - **Link to source:** [Retesting in selection: a meta-analysis of coaching and practice effects for tests of cognitive ability](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373), [Retest effects in cognitive ability tests: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003), [Psychometric bias due to retesting on cognitive ability tests in selection settings](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672)

### Category 3: The instructional mechanisms with the strongest evidence, and the boundary conditions where each stops working

*Every headline effect here is an average that includes conditions where the mechanism is zero or negative; this category fixes each one's size and the exact point where it stops working or reverses.*

- **Subcategory 3.1: Retrieval practice — *a retention effect, not an acquisition effect***
  - **Source:** Roediger & Karpicke (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Students read prose passages and then either took one or three immediate free-recall tests with no feedback (retrieval practice — being made to pull the material back out of memory) or reread it the same number of times (the restudy control). A final test followed after 5 minutes, 2 days, or 1 week — the retention interval. `[undergraduate laboratory samples; prose passages; free recall, no feedback]`
      - In Experiment 1, **restudying beat testing on the 5-minute test** (81% vs. 75% of idea units), t(39) = 3.22, d = 0.52 — d being effect size in standard deviations, where 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. The pattern reversed at 2 days and 1 week.
      - In Experiment 2, the repeated-study group led at 5 minutes (83% vs. 78% vs. 71%) and the order inverted at 1 week (61% vs. 56% vs. 40%).
      - Repeated studying raised students' confidence that they would remember the material even though it produced worse delayed retention.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Measured immediately, testing loses to rereading by 6 percentage points; measured a week later it wins by 21. Learners' confidence tracks the immediate result, not the durable one. Any product optimizing for within-session gains or for learner-reported confidence will systematically select against the method with better long-term retention.
    - **Link to source:** [Test-enhanced learning: taking memory tests improves long-term retention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The meta-analytic estimate — *about 0.50, and one identifiable cell where it goes to zero***
  - **Source:** Rowland (2014); Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017); Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Rowland's meta-analysis pooled 159 effect sizes from 61 studies comparing testing against a restudy control; the overall effect was Hedges' g = 0.50 (g is the same effect-size scale as d, corrected for small samples). `[laboratory studies with restudy controls only]`
      - In Rowland's high-exposure data set, initial recognition testing gave the smallest effect (g = 0.36) against g = 0.72 for cued recall and g = 0.82 for free recall; all multiple-choice tests were coded as recognition.
      - **The effect goes to zero in one cell.** With no feedback, initial test performance of 50% or less produced no reliable testing effect: g = 0.03, 95% CI [−0.21, 0.27], p = .79. Still without feedback, 51%–75% initial success gave g = 0.29 and above 75% gave g = 0.56; **with feedback, regardless of success, g = 0.73**, 95% CI [0.61, 0.86].
      - Matching the initial and final test formats added nothing to the effect, which Rowland reports as inconsistent with transfer-appropriate processing — the theory that practice pays most when it resembles the final test.
      - Adesope and colleagues' larger meta-analysis (272 independent effects from 188 experiments) found a weighted mean of +0.51 for practice testing over restudying and +0.93 over filler or no activity; mixed-format and multiple-choice practice tests produced the largest effects (g = 0.80 and g = 0.70), with multiple-choice significantly above short-answer (g = 0.70 vs. g = 0.48).
      - Yang and colleagues' classroom-only review covered 48,478 students across 222 independent studies: g = 0.499, 95% CI [0.442, 0.557], p < .001, of which 82.9% of effects were positive, 15.5% negative, and 1.6% null. `[classroom/applied settings, not laboratory]`
      - **The youngest band is the weakest:** elementary school g = 0.328, middle school g = 0.597, high school g = 0.655, university g = 0.486. Test format did not significantly modulate classroom benefits across seven formats — and Yang's team notes that Rowland found feedback almost doubled the benefit while Adesope's team found no feedback moderation at all.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Three meta-analyses converge near 0.50. **The effect goes to zero when there is no feedback and initial retrieval succeeds half the time or less** — precisely the condition an adaptive difficulty algorithm creates by design. Corrective feedback restores it. Elementary school returns the weakest estimate of any age band, and format guidance is contradictory across the three.
    - **Link to source:** [The effect of testing versus restudy on retention](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559), [Rethinking the use of tests: a meta-analysis of practice testing](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306), [Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Transfer — *the strictest boundary, and the one that limits any reasoning claim***
  - **Source:** Pan & Rickard (2018); Agarwal (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Pan and Rickard's review covered 192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments and 67 articles (N = 10,382); a random-effects model gave d = 0.40, 95% CI [0.31, 0.50], against a control that simply re-exposed the material without testing.
      - Transfer was greatest across test formats, to application and inference questions, to medical-diagnosis problems, and to mediator and related word cues; it was weakest to rearranged stimulus–response items, to untested material seen during study, and to worked-example problems (a worked example is a fully solved problem given to study rather than to solve).
      - With no response congruency between initial and final tests — that is, when the final test does not call for the answer that was practiced — the weighted effect was d = 0.28; congruency added d = 0.30, yielding d = 0.58. Elaborated retrieval practice added d = 0.23, and higher initial test performance predicted greater transfer.
      - **After correcting for publication bias** — the tendency for positive results to reach print — the moderator effects were minimally affected but the intercept predictions dropped substantially, "often indicating no positive transfer when none of the aforementioned moderators are present."
      - Agarwal quizzed middle-school and college students with fact questions, higher-order questions, or a mix, in laboratory and K-12 settings. **Higher-order and mixed quizzes improved higher-order performance; fact quizzes did not** — and higher-order practice did not improve delayed fact recall. `[middle school and college samples; expository passages]`
      - When quiz type matched final-test type, retrieval practice beat the mixed condition for both fact and higher-order learning. Agarwal concludes that building a fact base may be less potent than practicing at the higher-order level, contrary to intuitions from Bloom's taxonomy, and reads the results as most consistent with transfer-appropriate processing.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Transfer averages 0.40, below the 0.50 for same-material retention, and the average is carried by three moderators: response congruency, elaborated retrieval, and high initial performance. **Strip those and the bias-corrected estimate often shows no transfer at all.** Agarwal adds that fact practice does not ladder up to higher-order performance. Build practice items at the level you intend to move.
    - **Link to source:** [Transfer of test-enhanced learning: meta-analytic review and synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151), [Retrieval practice and Bloom's taxonomy](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000282)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Spacing — *robust, but the optimal gap shrinks as a fraction of the horizon***
  - **Source:** Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006); Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The 2006 review found 839 assessments of distributed practice — study sessions spread apart rather than massed back-to-back — in 317 experiments across 184 articles. `[verbal memory tasks scored by recall only]`
      - Across all retention intervals, massed presentation yielded 36.7% accuracy against 47.3% for spaced, over 271 comparisons with 14,811 participants, t(540) = 6.6, p < .001; only 12 of the 271 comparisons showed no effect or a negative one.
      - **But** within individual retention-interval bins the difference was not significant: 10 minutes to under 1 day (p = .535), 1 day (p = .249), 2–7 days (p = .190). The gap between study sessions that maximized retention grew as the retention interval grew, and the authors state the century-old literature is sorely lacking the information most needed to derive serious practical benefit from it.
      - The 2008 follow-up taught facts to more than 1,350 people, reviewed after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and tested up to 1 year later; at any given test delay, a longer gap first increased and then gradually reduced final performance. `[fact learning; internet-recruited adults]`
      - As a proportion of test delay, the optimal gap fell from about 20%–40% at a 1-week delay to about 5%–10% at a 1-year delay. Interpolated optimal gaps for recall were roughly 3, 8, 12, and 27 days — 43%, 23%, 17%, and 8% of the 7-, 35-, 70-, and 350-day retention intervals.
      - **The payoff depends on the horizon:** those 7-, 35-, 70-, and 350-day intervals gave 10%, 59%, 111%, and 77% improvement at the optimal gap over a zero-day gap.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Spacing is the most consistent mechanism here — only 12 of 271 comparisons failed to favor it. **But** every retention-interval bin under a week, which is the range most software schedulers operate in, was individually non-significant. The optimal gap is a shrinking ratio rather than a constant, so a scheduler cannot be tuned until the retention horizon is fixed.
    - **Link to source:** [Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: a review and quantitative synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354), [Spacing effects in learning: a temporal ridgeline of optimal retention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)

- **Subcategory 3.5: Interleaving — *reverses sign on verbal material***
  - **Source:** Rohrer & Taylor (2007); Brunmair & Richter (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Rohrer and Taylor had college students practice several problem types either blocked by type (all of one kind together) or randomly mixed — interleaved — with a test one week later. `[college students; small samples; mathematics volume-formula problems]`
      - Mixing hurt practice: the blocked group averaged 89% against the mixed group's 60%, t(16) = 3.14, p < .01, d = 1.06. That advantage came mostly from the first session (87% vs. 43%); by the second session the difference was not significant (91% vs. 78%).
      - **One week later the ranking inverted:** the mixed group averaged 63% against the blocked group's 20%, t(14) = 2.64, p < .05, d = 1.34, with a significant strategy × phase interaction, F(1,16) = 35.08, p < .001.
      - Brunmair and Richter's meta-analysis of 59 studies with 238 effect sizes nested in 158 samples found a moderate overall interleaving effect of Hedges' g = 0.42.
      - **The sign flips with the material.** Interleaving was best for paintings (g = 0.67) and other visual material; mathematics was small at g = 0.34, 95% CI [0.11, 0.57]; expository text was ambiguous at g = 0.21, p = .119 `[verbal materials]`; and **for words, blocking beat interleaving, g = −0.39, 95% CI [−0.64, −0.14]**.
      - Meta-regression found stronger interleaving effects where material was more similar between categories, less similar within categories, and more complex. The authors conclude interleaving "should be used with caution in certain conditions, especially for expository texts and words," while noting the negative word effect "might not be caused by the use of words but rather by the task types or the specific categories or other (unknown) variables that characterized the few studies using words."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Blocking beat mixing by 29 percentage points during practice and lost by 43 a week later — and the practice penalty had largely closed by the second session, so early session data would condemn interleaving at exactly its worst moment. The 0.42 average hides paintings at 0.67 and words at −0.39. The material, not the schedule, decides the sign.
    - **Link to source:** [The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8), [Similarity matters: a meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)

- **Subcategory 3.6: Desirable difficulties and cognitive load — *the same manipulation helps or harms depending on the learner***
  - **Source:** Bjork & Bjork (2011); Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Bjork and Bjork separate storage strength — how entrenched and interassociated a memory is — from retrieval strength, how accessible it is right now given cues and recency. Current performance is entirely a function of retrieval strength, while storage strength retards forgetting and enhances relearning. `T1` `[book chapter; theoretical framing plus illustrative studies, not peer-reviewed]`
      - Their key idea is that "conditions that most rapidly increase retrieval strength differ from the conditions that maximize the gain of storage strength," so learners who mistake the first for the second become susceptible to preferring poorer conditions of learning to better ones. The desirable difficulties they enumerate are varying the conditions of learning, interleaving separate topics, spacing rather than massing, and using tests rather than presentations as study events. `T1`
      - **The boundary is stated outright:** "if, however, the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties." `T1`
      - Sweller and colleagues hold that novel information passes through a capacity- and duration-limited working memory before reaching unlimited long-term memory, after which "the capacity and duration limits of working memory disappear." Intrinsic load is the material's own complexity — its element interactivity, meaning how many pieces must be held in mind at once; extraneous load comes from how the material is presented and can be changed by changing the procedure.
      - Element interactivity depends on both the nature of the information and the knowledge of the person processing it, which makes load learner-relative rather than a property of content.
      - **The 2019 restatement differs from the version usually cited.** Departing from 1998, the authors now assume "germane cognitive load redistributes working memory resources from extraneous activities to activities directly relevant to learning," leaving only intrinsic and extraneous load as basic categories. The fixed-capacity assumption "must be modified" given evidence that working memory depletes after cognitive effort and recovers after rest.
      - The authors' own caveat: many experiments behind the original cognitive load effects never directly measured comparative load; the theory generated techniques, and techniques producing the expected learning outcomes were assumed to strengthen the theory.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Difficulty is not the point: conditions that speed up momentary accessibility differ from those that build durable memory, and only the first is visible in the moment. **The same manipulation turns harmful when the learner lacks the background knowledge to respond successfully** — the theoretical twin of 3.2's zero cell. The 2019 restatement also drops germane load as a separate category.
    - **Link to source:** [Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf), [Cognitive architecture and instructional design: 20 years later](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5)

- **Subcategory 3.7: Worked examples — *guidance that helps a novice reverses for an expert***
  - **Source:** Sweller & Cooper (1985); Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003); Renkl & Atkinson (2003); Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across five experiments on algebra manipulation with Year 9, Year 11, and university mathematics students, Sweller and Cooper found worked examples took considerably less time to process than conventional problems, and later similar problems were solved more rapidly with fewer mathematical errors — **but** "both of these findings were specific to problems identical in structure to the initial ones." `[algebra problems; secondary and university samples]`
      - Kalyuga and colleagues name the reversal: "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners. We call this phenomenon the expertise reversal effect." A large number of cognitive load theory effects apply only to learners with very limited experience; with more experience, "specific experimental effects can first disappear and then reverse."
      - **Two of their reviewed cases are full crossovers, not just fade-outs.** In spreadsheet experiments, more knowledgeable students gained more from imagining procedures than from studying worked examples, while for less knowledgeable students imagining had a negative effect. In reading studies, coherence additions to a biology text helped only low-knowledge readers, while high-knowledge readers benefitted most from the original, minimally coherent text.
      - Their stated implication: "to be efficient, instructional design should be tailored to the level of experience of intended learners. Without such tailoring, the effectiveness of instructional designs is likely to be random."
      - Renkl and Atkinson report that worked examples are very effective early in skill acquisition but "in later stages, however, solving problems is superior," and propose backward fading — the first task fully worked, the second missing its last step, the third missing the last two, then a full problem — while calling for research on making fading adaptive to prior knowledge. `[two intact classrooms totalling 35 students, one physics topic, 2-day posttest]`
      - Atkinson and colleagues add the decisive qualification: fading reliably improves near-transfer performance over example–problem pairs, but "this effect is not reliable on far-transfer tasks." Pairing fading with self-explanation prompts — asking the learner to name the principle behind each worked step — produced medium to large effects on both near and far transfer across two experiments, with no additional time on task.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The founding study reported the benefit and its limit together: faster, more accurate solving, but only on problems structurally identical to those studied. Guidance that reduces load for a novice becomes redundant for a more knowledgeable one, so **the format that gains in week one can lose in week six**. Fading fixes near transfer only; far transfer required self-explanation prompts.
    - **Link to source:** [The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3), [The expertise reversal effect](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4), [Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3), [Transitioning from studying examples to solving problems](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774)

- **Subcategory 3.8: Feedback — *moderate on average, harmful in up to 38% of cases***
  - **Source:** Kluger & DeNisi (1996); Hattie & Timperley (2007); Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kluger and DeNisi extracted 607 effect sizes from 131 usable papers covering 12,652 participants and 23,663 observations: "The weighted mean (weighted by sample size) of this distribution is 0.41, suggesting that, on average, FI has a moderate positive effect on performance. However, **over 38% of the effects were negative**."
      - That harm "cannot be explained by sampling error, feedback sign, or existing theories" — the weighted variance of the effect distribution was 0.97 against a sampling-error variance estimate of only 0.09, so the spread is real heterogeneity rather than noise.
      - Their Feedback Intervention Theory holds that feedback shifts attention among three hierarchical levels — task learning, task motivation, and meta-tasks including self-related processes — and that effectiveness decreases as attention moves toward the self and away from the task.
      - About 37% of the papers manipulated feedback without a control group and 16% reported confounded treatments, so roughly two thirds of the empirical feedback literature cannot speak to feedback's effect on performance.
      - Hattie and Timperley's synthesis of 12 previous meta-analyses covering 196 studies reported an average feedback effect of 0.79. Effective feedback answers one of "Where am I going?", "How am I going?", and "Where to next?", and of the four levels it can target — task, process, self-regulation, and self — feedback aimed at the self is least effective. `[synthesis of prior meta-analyses, not a primary meta-analysis]`
      - Wisniewski and colleagues' primary meta-analysis of 435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000, found d = 0.48 cleared of extreme values: "The integration of all study effects with the random-effects model leads to a weighted average effect size of d = 0.55. 17% of the effects were negative."
      - **Type matters more than anything else:** reinforcement or punishment, k = 39, d = 0.24; corrective feedback, k = 238, d = 0.46; high-information feedback, k = 42, d = 0.99, 95% CI [0.82, 1.15]. Feedback moved cognitive and motor outcomes more than motivational and behavioral ones, and heterogeneity was high (I² = 86.47%), so feedback is not one consistent treatment. The same authors record estimates ranging across 0.48 (their own), 0.70 (Hattie, 2009), and 0.79 (Hattie and Timperley, 2007), and describe Kluger and DeNisi as reporting "an average effect of 0.38... about a third of the effects were negative."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The mean is moderate, roughly 0.41 to 0.55, but **between 17% and 38% of individual effects are negative**, and the variance is real rather than sampling noise. Feedback aimed at the person rather than the task is where it turns. Type spreads fourfold: high-information 0.99 against reinforcement or punishment 0.24. The much-cited 0.79 was later revised to 0.48 on primary data by its own co-author.
    - **Link to source:** [The effects of feedback interventions on performance](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254), [The power of feedback](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487), [The power of feedback revisited](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087)

- **Subcategory 3.9: Self-explanation — *a large, stable effect that depends on who actually explains***
  - **Source:** Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994); Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Chi and colleagues asked 14 eighth-graders, without extensive training, to self-explain after each line of a passage on the human circulatory system; 10 control students read the same text twice with no prompts. `[24 eighth-graders total; single expository text; study time equated]`
      - Pretest-to-posttest gain was greater for the prompted group (32%) than the unprompted group (22%), and the group-by-test interaction was significant.
      - **The larger split was inside the prompted group.** High explainers generated 87 inferences against 29 for low explainers, learned with greater understanding, and all reached the correct mental model of the circulatory system, whereas many unprompted students and low explainers did not.
      - Bisra and colleagues found 69 effect sizes from 64 research reports; a random-effects analysis of 5,917 participants gave an overall g = .55, 95% CI [.45, .65], p < .001, favouring prompted self-explanation, with detectable heterogeneity, Q = 196.63, p < .001, df = 68.
      - Of 20 coded moderator variables, only three showed statistically detectable differences with implications for theory or practice. Citing the limits of instructor-scripted prompts, the authors recommend that future research explore computer generation of self-explanation prompts.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** At g = .55, prompted self-explanation is the largest mechanism estimate here apart from high-information feedback, and the most stable — only three of 20 moderators mattered. **But** the prompt itself bought 32% against 22%; the wide separation was between prompted learners who generated many inferences and those who generated few. The measurable signal is the explanation produced, not the prompt delivered.
    - **Link to source:** [Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding](https://doi.org/10.1016/0364-0213(94)90016-7), [Inducing self-explanation: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)

- **Subcategory 3.10: Mastery learning — *0.52 on locally written tests, nothing on standardized ones***
  - **Source:** Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990); Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kulik and colleagues analysed 108 studies of mastery learning — requiring a set standard on each unit before moving on — of which 72 used Keller's Personalized System of Instruction in college teaching and 36 used Bloom's Learning for Mastery, only 2 of them reporting primary-grade results. `[predominantly college-level; only 2 studies from primary grades]`
      - "The average effect size in the 103 studies was 0.52... t(102) = 15.78, p < .001." The average student in a mastery class performed at the 70th percentile against the 50th in a class with no mastery requirement.
      - All five examined variables significantly predicted effect sizes: group-based programs beat self-paced ones, high mastery standards beat lower ones, locally developed tests produced larger effects than standardized commercial tests, and effects were larger where control groups got less quiz feedback.
      - **The effect disappears on standardized tests.** "None of the five LFM studies that employed standardized criterion tests found a significant or even small effect on the standardized test. And yet two of the same studies found moderate treatment effects on locally developed tests." The authors record Slavin's position that with standardized tests and no extra time for feedback and remediation "mastery teaching turns out to be no more effective than conventional instruction," and concede that "Slavin is correct in pointing out that size of effect differs in LFM evaluations that use standardized and local tests."
      - Across 13 studies reporting by ability level, the average effect was higher for less able students (M = 0.61) than more able ones (M = 0.40), but not significantly, t = 1.23, p > .10. Self-paced mastery programs often reduce completion rates in college classes.
      - Dunlosky and colleagues rated ten learning techniques on whether benefits generalize across learning conditions, student characteristics, materials, and criterion tasks. **Only two cleared the bar:** "Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility assessments because they benefit learners of different ages and abilities and have been shown to boost students' performance across many criterion tasks and even in educational contexts."
      - Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice were rated moderate, their efficacy in educational contexts largely unknown. Five were rated low — summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text learning, and rereading — and rereading and highlighting, the two techniques students report using most, failed to "consistently boost students' performance."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Mastery learning is 0.52, about half the one-sigma figure Bloom claimed. **The effect vanishes on standardized tests** — none of the five studies using them found even a small effect, while the same programs moved locally developed tests. The evidence is almost entirely postsecondary. Rated on generalizability rather than average size, only practice testing and distributed practice cleared the bar.
    - **Link to source:** [Effectiveness of mastery learning programs: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265), [Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)

### Category 4: Performance during learning is not learning — why an app watching a child improve in real time may be watching nothing durable

*This category settles that in-app performance gains are not evidence of learning: the only trial here that withdrew its AI tutor found practice scores up 48% and later exam scores 17% below students who never had access.*

- **Subcategory 4.1: Performance during practice is not learning — *the two can move in opposite directions***
  - **Source:** Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The learning–performance distinction: what can be observed and measured during instruction is performance, "which is often an unreliable index of whether the relatively long-term changes that constitute learning have taken place." Learning itself must be inferred.
      - They dissociate both ways. Early animal and motor-skills research established that learning can occur with no discernible change in performance; the converse has more recently been shown, with performance improvements failing to yield significant learning and certain manipulations having opposite effects on the two.
      - In Landauer and Bjork's (1978) massed-testing condition — several tests in succession immediately after presentation of a name — acquisition performance was nearly errorless, yet long-term learning was worse than under expanded-interval and equal-interval schedules.
      - Shea and Morgan (1979) found blocked practice better during acquisition while random (interleaved) practice produced better retention at 10 minutes and 10 days. A badminton-serve study split the same way, whether serves were tested from the same or the opposite side of the court from which they were practiced.
      - In Goode, Geraci and Roediger's (2008) study, participants who practiced multiple variants of an anagram took longer during practice — a short-term performance decrement — but solved relatively more anagrams on a later test than participants who repeatedly solved the exact anagram that was later tested.
      - Under Bjork and Bjork's new theory of disuse, conditions that raise current retrieval strength — how easily something comes to mind right now, as opposed to how durably it is stored — "might benefit performance in the short term but will fail to produce the type of permanent changes that characterize long-term learning."
      - Learners share the error: they show a "stability bias," believing whatever they can retrieve now will stay accessible, and in a survey of 472 college students most reported rereading while 64% reported not revisiting material once they felt they had mastered it.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The easiest signal to collect is the least trustworthy one. Learning is a durable change that has to be inferred; performance is what you can watch during practice, and the two can dissociate in either direction. Massed testing, blocked practice, and repeated identical drills all look better during acquisition and worse later. Learners make the same mistake about themselves.
    - **Link to source:** [Learning versus performance: an integrative review](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

- **Subcategory 4.2: "It worked" is undefined until you name the test — *no study method wins in the abstract***
  - **Source:** Morris, Bransford & Franks (1977); connection drawn in Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Morris, Bransford and Franks crossed how material was studied against how it was tested. In Experiment 1, semantic (meaning-based) acquisition was superior to rhyme acquisition on a standard recognition test, whereas rhyme acquisition was superior to semantic acquisition on a rhyming recognition test.
      - The first finding supports, and the second contradicts, the levels-of-processing claim that depth of processing leads to stronger memory traces.
      - Experiment 2 replicated the reversal on both immediate and delayed recognition tests, and Experiment 3 showed the effects did not depend on how many times a rhyme sound was presented during acquisition.
      - The authors read this as transfer-appropriate processing: the value of a study activity is not a property of the activity but of how well the thinking it demands matches the thinking the test demands.
      - Soderstrom and Bjork later connect the principle to desirable difficulties and the related encoding specificity principle, both of which hold that memory improves to the extent that the engaged study and test processes overlap.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** No study method is better in the abstract. Which one wins flips when you change the test, and the flip survives a delay. So an app reporting that a child "improved" has said nothing until it names the test that improvement transfers to — and in-app items are not that test.
    - **Link to source:** [Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371%2877%2980016-9)

- **Subcategory 4.3: How much help to give is a named, unsolved problem — *open, by the field's own account***
  - **Source:** Koedinger & Aleven (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Koedinger and Aleven name the trade-off the "assistance dilemma" — "How should learning environments balance information or assistance giving and withholding to achieve optimal student learning?" — and state that "How best to achieve this balance remains a fundamental open problem in instructional science."
      - The field lacks the specific conditions and parameters that would indicate when and to what extent to give versus withhold information. Concrete instances include whether feedback should be immediate or delayed, and whether hints should be more specific or more general.
      - They tie the dilemma to Bjork's "desirable difficulties": assisting performance during instruction can sometimes improve learning, and in some cases making performance more difficult during instruction improves learning.
      - Their own Cognitive Tutor took the assistance-giving side — "an overriding concern was to minimize floundering on the part of students," because under ACT-R theory time spent pursuing incorrect paths does not contribute to learning.
      - Log data from the Geometry tutor show students frequently using the bottom-out hint — the last hint in a sequence, which simply states the answer — to obtain answers without reading the prior hints that explain the material.
      - **But** withholding is not the answer either: a study by Corbett and Anderson (1995) provided no support for the effectiveness of information withholding via simple forms of delayed feedback.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The field has a name for the choice an adaptive tutor makes on every click — the assistance dilemma — and no settled answer to it. Koedinger and Aleven call the balance point a fundamental open problem. Their own tutor erred toward helping, and students promptly mined its hints for answers. Simply withholding help did not work either.
    - **Link to source:** [Exploring the assistance dilemma in experiments with Cognitive Tutors](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0)

- **Subcategory 4.4: An AI tutor that raised practice scores and lowered exam scores — *the harm is measured***
  - **Source:** Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman (2025); earlier working-paper version (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A randomized controlled trial at a large high school in Turkey, Fall semester of the 2023–2024 academic year: four 90-minute sessions for about fifty 9th, 10th and 11th grade classes and nearly 1,000 students, collectively about 15% of each grade's semester math curriculum.
      - Three arms: control, with course notes and textbook only; "GPT Base," a simple GPT-4 chat interface similar to ChatGPT; and "GPT Tutor," the same interface prompted to give hints without directly giving the answer, supplied with teacher-provided correct solutions and common student mistakes per problem.
      - On the GPT-assisted practice sessions, intention-to-treat estimates — counting every student assigned to an arm, whether or not they used the tool — show GPT Base and GPT Tutor increased scores by 0.137 and 0.361 (out of 1) against a control-arm mean of 0.28, implying performance increases of 48% and 127%.
      - **But** on the subsequent unassisted exam, GPT Base performance degraded by 0.054 (out of 1) relative to the control arm — **17% worse than students who never had access** — which the authors describe as statistically significantly worse than control.
      - GPT Tutor's exam performance was statistically indistinguishable from control, with a point estimate of −0.004, smaller by an order of magnitude than the GPT Base estimate — **and produced no learning benefit either**: the authors state they "still do not observe a positive effect."
      - Queried ten times on each of the 57 practice problems with students' most common message, "What is the answer?", GPT Base was correct only 51% of the time on average — wrong 49% — with logical errors 42% of the time and arithmetic errors 8%. Those logical errors hurt practice performance but showed no evidence of spilling over to the corresponding exam problems, so the authors favour the "crutch" explanation over "misled by errors."
      - On students' very first interaction in the first session, 56% of GPT Base and 42% of GPT Tutor messages either repeated the question text or asked for the answer; across the first interaction on all problems in that session the GPT Base figure rose to 67% while GPT Tutor's decreased. Students also sent substantially fewer messages per problem to GPT Base — consistent, the authors say, with it simply providing solutions.
      - The work previously circulated as an SSRN working paper under the shorter title "Generative AI Can Harm Learning," SSRN abstract ID 4895486, dated 2024. `T1` `[superseded by the peer-reviewed PNAS version; the retitling added "without guardrails," which materially narrows the claim]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the trial that switched the tool off and measured what was left. Practice scores rose 48% and 127%; on the unassisted exam the plain-chatbot group landed 17% below students who never had access, and the guardrailed group merely broke even. The size of the visible gain told you nothing about what remained.
    - **Link to source:** [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), [Earlier working-paper version](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4895486)

- **Subcategory 4.5: The contrary result — a scaffolded AI tutor beat an active-learning classroom, *but only measured immediately***
  - **Source:** Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti (2025); earlier preprint version (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A randomized controlled crossover experiment in a large undergraduate physics course at Harvard University with N = 194 students; of 233 enrolled, 194 were eligible based on consent, participation in both conditions, and completion of all pre- and post-tests. `[undergraduates at a highly selective university, not children]`
      - In week one, group 1 used the AI tutor at home while group 2 took an in-class active-learning lesson; the conditions reversed the following week, covering surface tension then fluid flow.
      - The AI group reached a higher median post-score (M = 4.5, N = 142) than the in-class active-learning group (M = 3.5, N = 174) against a combined pre-test baseline of M = 2.75 (N = 316), with median learning gains over double the in-class group's; a two-sample rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) test on the post-score distributions gave z = −5.6, p < 10⁻⁸.
      - The authors report a linear-regression effect size of 0.63, call it an underestimate because of a ceiling effect in post-test scores, and report a quantile-regression estimate in the range of 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations.
      - In-class students spent 15 of a 75-minute period on the pre- and post-tests with an assumed 60 minutes of learning; the AI group's median time on task was 49 minutes and 70% spent less than 60 minutes. Time on task showed no correlation with post-test scores, despite the wide range of measured times.
      - The tutor was not an unmodified chatbot: the authors avoided relying solely on GPT-4 to generate solutions, enriched their prompts with comprehensive step-by-step answers, and designed the platform to guide students sequentially through each part of each problem.
      - **But** learning was measured by post-tests administered immediately following each lesson. No delayed retention test is reported, and the authors note in discussion that studying a full course would allow "systematic integration of well-established retention-enhancing strategies (e.g., spacing)."
      - An earlier version circulated as a Research Square preprint posted 14 May 2024 under the title "AI Tutoring Outperforms Active Learning," whose abstract claimed students "learn more than twice as much in less time when using an AI tutor." `T1` `[superseded by the peer-reviewed version]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The strongest published counter-case, measuring a different thing. The AI group reached a median 4.5 against 3.5 in class, more than double the gain, in less time. **But** the tutor was heavily scaffolded — closer to the guardrailed arm in 4.4 than to a plain chatbot — and every score was collected immediately after the lesson. Nobody took the tutor away.
    - **Link to source:** [AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6), [Earlier preprint version](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-4243877/v1)

- **Subcategory 4.6: Handing thinking to a tool changes what stays in the head — *and the drift feeds itself***
  - **Source:** Risko & Gilbert (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Cognitive offloading is "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand" — and the propensity to do it is influenced by metacognitive evaluations of our own mental abilities, not only by actual internal demand.
      - Offloading memory demands into a transactive system — an arrangement in which part of what you know is held outside you, in a device or another person — is "not a 'free pass'" in terms of cognitive consequences.
      - Their framework predicts that offloading to a more accurate external artefact will likely reduce both internal memory for that content and metacognitive confidence in it, which in turn increases the likelihood of offloading again — "a kind of self-reinforcing pattern that will produce a drift away from reliance on internal capabilities when situated in an environment with effective cognitive technologies."
      - Any benefit of offloading is contingent on the offloaded demand being unnecessary with respect to the learning goal.
      - Long-term reliance on automated processes could lead to cognitive "skill decay," where a developed ability deteriorates over time.
      - When participants knew they would subsequently have Internet access, they were more likely to answer "don't know" and reported lower feeling-of-knowing on trivia questions, reducing their willingness to offer an answer based on their own knowledge.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the mechanism behind the crutch. Leaning on an accurate tool lowers both what you retain and your confidence that you could have managed alone — which makes you lean on it again. Offloading only pays when the work offloaded was not the point, and an app cannot tell the difference from performance data.
    - **Link to source:** [Cognitive offloading](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002)

- **Subcategory 4.7: The offloading effect measured directly — *it replicates, but only when the tool has proved reliable***
  - **Source:** Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011); Schooler & Storm (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sparrow, Liu and Wegner report across four studies that when people expect to have future access to information, they have lower rates of recall of the information itself and enhanced recall instead for where to access it. `[adult participants, trivia statements]`
      - In their Experiment 2, participants who believed the computer erased what they typed recalled the trivia statements best: Erase M = 0.31 (SD = 0.04) and Erase-Remember M = 0.29 (SD = 0.07), against Save M = 0.22 (SD = 0.07) and Save-Remember M = 0.19 (SD = 0.09); the omnibus test was F(3, 56) = 2.80, P < 0.05.
      - Explicitly instructing participants to try to remember produced no significant effect; only the saved/erased manipulation did. They conclude the Internet has become a primary form of external, or transactive, memory, where information is stored collectively outside ourselves.
      - Given recent concerns about the robustness and replicability of this "Google Effect," Schooler and Storm ran two experiments to replicate and extend it by identifying a potential boundary condition.
      - In Experiment 1, Schooler and Storm found the effect replicates only when a practice phase has demonstrated the reliability of the saving process; no evidence of it appeared when the practice phase demonstrated the saving process to be unreliable. Experiment 2 replicated the reliable-condition results and showed the effect robust across 10 different topics of trivia statements.
      - They conclude the Google Effect is a replicable phenomenon, but that the perceived reliability of the saving process is critical for determining whether it is observed.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Expecting to have information available suppresses memory for it, and trying harder does not undo that — but the effect only shows up once the store has proved reliable. Sparrow and colleagues found the suppression; Schooler and Storm found its condition. An app whose hints are always accurate and always one tap away is exactly that condition.
    - **Link to source:** [Google effects on memory](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745), [Saved information is remembered less well than deleted information](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2021.1962356)

- **Subcategory 4.8: The child's confidence comes from the same misleading signal — *self-report is not an independent check***
  - **Source:** Koriat & Bjork (2005); Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Koriat and Bjork state that monitoring one's own knowledge during study "suffers from an inherent discrepancy between study and test situations: Judgments of learning (JOLs) are made in the presence of information that is absent but solicited during testing." A judgment of learning is the learner's own prediction of whether they will remember something later.
      - Failure to discount the effects of that information when making judgments of learning "can instill a sense of competence during learning that proves unwarranted during testing."
      - Using a paired-associates task they distinguish a priori association strength, which they argue has the greater influence on later recall, from a posteriori association strength, which has the greater influence on judgments of learning.
      - They define foresight bias as predicting your own success at recalling the correct answer while that answer is in front of you, and distinguish it from hindsight bias: hindsight bias constitutes a memory distortion, foresight bias a metacognitive bias.
      - Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell report evidence that "people often have a faulty mental model of how they learn and remember, making them prone to both misassessing and mismanaging their own learning," with ongoing self-assessments influenced by current performance and by "the subjective sense of fluency" — the fluency illusion, in which material that feels easy to process is taken to be learned.
      - They frame the problem as increasingly consequential because both the need and the opportunities for individuals to learn on their own outside formal classroom settings have grown, with technological advances providing new opportunities for such learning.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Asking the child does not fix the problem. Confidence is formed while the answer is visible, so it tracks the wrong quantity and runs high — and it is generated by the same easy-feeling signal the dashboard is already reporting. A smooth, frictionless interface amplifies the illusion rather than correcting it.
    - **Link to source:** [Illusions of competence in monitoring one's knowledge during study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.2.187), [Self-regulated learning: beliefs, techniques, and illusions](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)

### Category 5: What a learning system can actually observe, and what those observables have been shown to mean

*This category settles what interaction logs can honestly say: a handful of behaviors are measurable and outcome-linked, several point the opposite way from intuition, and none transfers reliably to a new population.*

- **Subcategory 5.1: Gaming the system — *the one off-task behavior that predicts learning loss***
  - **Source:** Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner (2004); Baker, Corbett, Roll & Koedinger (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Gaming the system** means exploiting a tutor's help and feedback to advance without thinking about the material — clicking hints until the software supplies the answer, or entering answers fast and systematically. Baker and colleagues coded it from 563 human observations of 20 seconds each, 8.0 per student, in six categories. `[5 classrooms, 70 students aged ~12–14, one Cognitive Tutor unit; observation, not an automated detector]`
      - Students were on-task 82% of observed time; gaming took 3%, against off-task conversation 11%, inactivity 3%, off-task solitary behavior 1%. 24% of students gamed at least once, 11% gamed more than 10% of the time, and 51% were off-task some other way at least once.
      - **But** gaming was the only off-task behavior significantly correlated with post-test score, F(1,68) = 11.82, p < 0.01, r = −0.38, and partial r = −0.34 controlling for pre-test and general achievement. Off-task conversation was marginal at best (r = −0.19); inactivity and off-task solitary behavior (both r = −0.08) were not significant.
      - The 8 high-gaming students averaged 44% at post-test against 78% for the 53 who never gamed; matched on low prior knowledge, that gap narrowed to 68% versus 44% and was only marginal, t(30) = 1.69, p = 0.10. Gaming did not correlate with other off-task behavior, so disinterest is an unlikely cause; performance orientation versus learned helplessness stayed undecided. `[authors' interpretation]`
      - Their 2008 follow-up split gaming by outcome — GAMED-NOT-HURT (a sizeable pre-post gain, or a pre-test too high to allow one) versus GAMED-HURT (low on both). Across four lessons and 436 students, HURT/NOT-HURT ran 8%/25% in Scatterplot, 10%/6% in Probability, 27%/3% in Geometry, 8%/5% in Percents. `[outcome-defined labels, not observed behavior]`
      - A **detector** — a model reading behavior off logs alone — using Latent Response Models over 26 per-action features (correctness, widget type, timing relative to other students on the same step, the tutor's estimate the student knew the skill, history) scored A' = 0.80 for GAMED-HURT versus non-gaming and A' = 0.71 between gaming types in training, A' = 0.73 and A' = 0.68 under leave-one-out **cross-validation** (scored on held-out students), and A' = 0.80 on a held-out fourth lesson. On poor learners who did not game it managed only A' = 0.53 — chance, so it caught behavior, not low achievement. `[A': 0.5 is chance, 1.0 is perfect]`
      - The authors report "still not conclusive evidence as to whether the relationship between harmful gaming and learning is causal or correlational," and that the split's generality across tutoring systems is "not yet clear." `[authors' caveat]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Gaming is how correct answers come apart from learning. It filled only 3% of observed time, less than ordinary off-task behavior, yet it alone predicted lower post-test scores even after controls for prior knowledge. Some gaming did no harm; causality and cross-system generality stay unclaimed.
    - **Link to source:** [Off-task behavior in the Cognitive Tutor classroom: when students game the system](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985741), [Developing a generalizable detector of when students game the system](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11257-007-9045-6)

- **Subcategory 5.2: Wheel-spinning — *persistence that predicts failure, not growth***
  - **Source:** Beck & Gong (2013); Gong & Beck (2015); Wan & Beck (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Beck and Gong coined **wheel-spinning** for a student who keeps practicing a skill without ever mastering it, and argued that mastery learning — which keeps assigning problems until the system judges the skill learned — rests on an untested assumption that the student can master it with the teaching the tutor has available.
      - They operationalized it as failing to produce three consecutive correct responses within the first 10 practice opportunities on a skill. Under that criterion, about 31% of **student-skill pairs** — one child on one specific skill, the counting unit throughout this literature — were wheel-spinning in Cognitive Tutor Algebra and 38% in ASSISTments. `[two tutoring systems, US middle- and high-school mathematics]`
      - Their central claim is directional: a student who does not master a skill quickly is likely to struggle and will probably never master it. They also reported connections to gaming the system and to disengagement.
      - Gong and Beck called it "a common problem affecting a significant number of students in different tutoring systems" and "negatively associated with learning," and reported that a model over 15 generic features — learning performance (correct response count, prior problem count with hint request), student "seriousness" (prior problem fast correct, prior problem slow incorrect), and general features such as skill identity — generalized to future students across two systems and detected it early with high accuracy. `["high accuracy" not quantified in the abstract]`
      - Wan and Beck binned ASSISTments students into five groups by prerequisite performance: the bottom 20% wheel-spun 50% of the time against 10% for the top 20%. Adding that prerequisite feature improved the model only modestly, R² from 0.264 to 0.268 and AUC from 0.884 to 0.888 (both model-accuracy measures).
      - **The opposite of the intuitive reading:** a path model found gaming was not causal of wheel-spinning. Weak prior knowledge produced wheel-spinning, which then produced gaming. `[reported secondhand by Wan and Beck; original study used non-Western students]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Effort is not evidence of progress. About a third of student-skill pairs in two large tutors were wheel-spinning, so this is the common case, not the margin. Failing early predicts never mastering, and the causal order runs backwards from intuition: weak prior knowledge produces wheel-spinning, which then produces gaming.
    - **Link to source:** [Wheel-spinning: students who fail to master a skill](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_44), [Towards detecting wheel-spinning: future failure in mastery learning](https://doi.org/10.1145/2724660.2724673), [Considering the influence of prerequisite performance on wheel spinning](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED560558.pdf)

- **Subcategory 5.3: Productive versus unproductive persistence — *the criterion itself is unstable***
  - **Source:** Kai, Almeda, Baker, C. Heffernan & N. Heffernan (2018); Zhang, Huang, Wang, Lu, Fang, Stamper, Fancsali, Holstein & Aleven (2019); Krumm, Beattie, Takahashi, D'Angelo, Feng & Cheng (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kai and colleagues began from "not all persistence is uniformly beneficial for learning" and ran a decision tree on ASSISTments logs. The three most predictive features were the minimum hints requested on any problem, the maximum **bottom-out hints** — the last hint in a sequence, which just gives the answer — requested across any 8 problems, and the standard deviation of time between problems of the same skill. `[ASSISTments skill builders, one online math platform]`
      - Two profiles emerged: students who skip hints on at least one problem but take more than one bottom-out hint across any 8; and students who never take two or more bottom-out hints across any 8, also skip hints on at least one problem, and leave relatively short delays between problems of the same skill. The second looks exactly like quiet diligence.
      - They concluded that spaced practice and sparing use of bottom-out hints likely help, and that the features predicted better combined than separately. `[correlational classification, not an intervention]`
      - Zhang and colleagues compared the three-correct-in-a-row criterion with the Predictive Stability++ policy over three datasets and more than 560,000 student-knowledge-component pairs (a **knowledge component** is one discrete skill the tutor tracks). Prevalence was 6.6%, 0.56%, and 10.2% under three-correct-in-a-row, and 24.2%, 2.17%, and 13.2% under Predictive Stability++. `[three datasets, all US mathematics tutors]`
      - Among pairs either criterion flagged, the two agreed on only 22.2%, 14.1%, and 41.6%; "the agreement on wheel spinning is generally less than 50%," a divergence the authors call "not desirable from either a practical or a theoretical perspective, as we do not know which one to apply or build on." `[authors' caveat]`
      - Detection is early but not immediate. At the fourth practice opportunity a Random Forest reached **precision** (share of flagged students really stuck) and **recall** (share of stuck students caught) of 77.2% and 63.5% under three-correct-in-a-row and 90.8% and 81.4% under Predictive Stability++; logistic regression on "Correct Response Percentage" alone hit 93.5% precision and 77.1% recall after 4 steps. **But** almost all models fell below 20% on both in the first three steps — a **cold start**, too little log data to judge anyone.
      - Krumm and colleagues note that the Carnegie Foundation and Community College Pathways define productive persistence as "tenacity plus the use of good strategies," measure it primarily by self-report with behavioral log measures only supplementing, and built those as improvement-science "practical measures," distinct from research and accountability measures and required to be usable by instructors deciding next steps. `[community college developmental mathematics; built for instructor action, not selection]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** No single log feature separates useful struggle from futile struggle, and one wheel-spinning profile looks exactly like quiet diligence. The field's two definitions disagree on more than half the cases they flag, with prevalence swinging from 0.56% to 24.2%. The originators measure the construct by self-report, for teachers rather than ranking.
    - **Link to source:** [Decision tree modeling of wheel-spinning and productive persistence in skill builders](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.3344810), [Early detection of wheel spinning: comparison across tutors, models, features, and operationalizations](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf), [Practical measurement and productive persistence](https://doi.org/10.18608/jla.2016.32.6)

- **Subcategory 5.4: Help-seeking — *abuse predicts learning loss, avoidance predicts nothing***
  - **Source:** Aleven, McLaren, Roll & Koedinger (2006); Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Aleven and colleagues modelled correct help-seeking as 57 production rules covering effective and ineffective behavior and ran it offline against Geometry Cognitive Tutor logs. The overall help-seeking error rate was 73%; a second dataset gave 64%. `[one geometry unit, 49 students, 58,732 tracked actions, ~7 hours of tutor use]`
      - The largest error category was Help Abuse at 36%, mostly Clicking Through Hints at 33%; next was Help Avoidance at 19%.
      - Overall error frequency predicted post-test performance at partial r = −0.65 with pre-test partialed out, t(2,38) = 5.2, p < 0.0005. **But** the categories split: Help Abuse (partial r = −0.48) and Miscellaneous Bugs (partial r = −0.38) were significant, while Help Avoidance (partial r = −0.12) and Try-Step Abuse (partial r = 0.265) did not predict learning at all.
      - A confound remains: help-seeking success and cognitive success rate correlated at r = 0.81, and only the cognitive rate survived a multiple regression. The authors judged their own 73% error rate "too high to be useful for tutoring." `[the two measures are largely confounded here; authors' caveat]`
      - Roll and colleagues deployed a Help Tutor as an adjunct agent in a commercial Geometry Cognitive Tutor across two classroom studies (N = 58 and N = 67). Help students made fewer errors during hint requests than Control — 13% versus 20% in Month 1 (Cohen's d = 1.2), 12% versus 19% in Month 3 (d = 1.47) — asked for the bottom-out hint on 45% of hint sequences versus 60% in Month 1 (d = 0.64) and 41% versus 53% in Month 3 (d = 0.59), and took longer reading hints.
      - The effects were asymmetric exactly as the diagnostic data predicts: no effect on error rate during solution attempts — the category containing help avoidance — and a marginal *increase* in inappropriate glossary searches.
      - In Study 2 the improved help-seeking transferred to new domain content the following month with the support switched off. An earlier version of the intervention reduced errors but reported: "we did not find evidence that they learned the intended help-seeking skills, or learned the domain knowledge better." `[Roll and colleagues, 2006; earlier version of the same intervention]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A 57-rule model flagged 73% of tutor actions as help-seeking errors, a rate its own authors called too high to be useful. The categories then split: help abuse predicted lower learning, help avoidance predicted nothing. Feedback moved abuse but not avoidance, and the gain transferred to new content a month later.
    - **Link to source:** [Toward meta-cognitive tutoring: a model of help seeking with a Cognitive Tutor](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~aleven/Papers/2006/Aleven_ea_IJAIED2006.pdf), [Improving students' help-seeking skills using metacognitive feedback](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2010.07.004)

- **Subcategory 5.5: Carelessness and the slip parameter — *careless errors cluster among the most engaged students***
  - **Source:** Corbett & Anderson (1995); San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo (2011); San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Corbett and Anderson's **Bayesian Knowledge Tracing** tracks a changing knowledge state with four parameters: the initial probability the student knows the skill; the probability of learning it at a given opportunity; **guess**, a correct response without knowing; and **slip**, a wrong response while knowing. `[originally the ACT Programming Tutor; the publisher dates the volume to 1994 while the field cites 1995]`
      - The **mastery threshold** decides when practice stops: under Cognitive Mastery, used in most Cognitive Tutors, a student keeps getting problems until the model's probability they know the skill passes a level typically set at 95%. That is what "failing to reach mastery" means in the wheel-spinning literature.
      - San Pedro and colleagues defined a careless error as a wrong answer from a student who knows the answer, using a Contextual Guess-and-Slip variant. Standard Bayesian Knowledge Tracing fixes slip and guess per skill; the contextual version estimates them for each action from features including action speed and help-seeking history, so it reads "wrong, fast, and not asking for help" as carelessness rather than ignorance.
      - The detectors reached r = 0.460 and r = 0.481 against the labels and A' = 0.821 and A' = 0.814 at predicting a correct answer on the next opportunity. The authors called them generalizable across tutor interfaces and countries. `[126 students, one public high school in Quezon City, Philippines, one 80-minute unit; transfer tested only between two versions of the same tutor and two countries]`
      - **The opposite of the intuitive reading:** carelessness correlated *positively* with **engaged concentration** — the observed state of a student absorbed in the work — at r = 0.58, p < 0.001 and r = 0.54, p < 0.001 across two conditions, and negatively with confusion (r = −0.38 and r = −0.32) and boredom (r = −0.35 in one condition). The authors called it surprising: "a highly engaged student may paradoxically become overconfident or impulsive, leading to more careless errors." `[Philippine high-schoolers; affect from field observation; correlational]`
      - Confused and bored students had lower learning overall, so their mistakes came from genuinely not knowing. The same log event — a wrong answer on a mastered skill — therefore means opposite things depending on the child's affective state.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Bayesian Knowledge Tracing's slip parameter is where careless errors live, and estimating it per action reads "wrong, fast, no help requested" as carelessness rather than ignorance. That inverts the intuitive reading of engagement telemetry: careless errors clustered among the most concentrated students, while confused and bored students were making errors of not knowing.
    - **Link to source:** [Knowledge tracing: modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821), [Detecting carelessness through contextual estimation of slip probabilities](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-21869-9_40), [Carelessness and affect in an intelligent tutoring system for mathematics](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-014-0015-y)

- **Subcategory 5.6: Off-task behavior from logs alone — *detectable, but acting on it can manufacture gamers***
  - **Source:** Baker (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The model detects off-task behavior from log files of system usage alone — no screen capture, audio, video, microphones, eye-trackers, or fMRI. `[five Cognitive Tutor studies, 2003–2005]`
      - Ground truth came from field observations in 20-second windows by one of three observers, with an inter-rater session giving **Cohen's κ** — agreement beyond chance, where 0 is chance — of 0.74.
      - The best multi-parameter Latent Response Model correlated 0.62 with observed off-task frequency and averaged 0.55 across test sets under 10-fold cross-validation, against 0.47 for a single-parameter model thresholding action duration. Where observers found student A off-task more often than student B, the model agreed 83% of the time.
      - The most informative single feature was very fast actions immediately before or after very slow ones, worth a cross-validated correlation of 0.483 alone. The rationale matters: consistently slow actions may mean careful thought or asking the teacher for help, whereas slow actions bracketed by fast ones indicate being off-task. Latency by itself is not disengagement.
      - Off-task behavior was significantly associated with disliking computers (r = 0.22), disliking mathematics (r = 0.27), and passive-aggressiveness (r = 0.20), and marginally with a lack of educational self-drive (r = 0.17). `[self-report questionnaires in two of the five studies]`
      - Whether it costs learning is unsettled: one study found no significant correlation with learning inside the software, while a later meta-analysis by the same group found a significant negative one. `[conflicting results within one research program]`
      - **But** the load-bearing point for any monitoring system is the author's warning that heavy-handed responses to off-task detection are likely counterproductive and may convert off-task students into gamers — learning to type any answer every 20 seconds so the system believes they are working. `[design warning, not an empirical result]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Off-task behavior is detectable from logs alone at 0.55 correlation to human observers, and its strongest feature is fast actions beside slow ones rather than long pauses, since a pause can mean thinking or asking the teacher. Whether it costs learning is unsettled. Cracking down may convert off-task students into gamers.
    - **Link to source:** [Modeling and understanding students' off-task behavior in intelligent tutoring systems](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240785)

- **Subcategory 5.7: Confusion — *helps only when it gets resolved***
  - **Source:** D'Mello & Graesser (2012); D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun & Graesser (2014); Pekrun (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - D'Mello and Graesser's model predicts that learners in engagement/flow hit cognitive disequilibrium and confusion when they meet contradictions, incongruities, anomalies, obstacles to goals, and other impasses; that they revert to flow if equilibrium is restored; and that failing to restore it triggers frustration, which if unresolved becomes boredom.
      - The hypotheses held in two studies of 32–35 minute tutoring sessions with affect tracked at roughly 110 points each, and time-series analysis confirmed all three predicted oscillations: confusion–engagement/flow, boredom–frustration, and confusion–frustration. `[laboratory sessions, adult conversational tutor, retrospective self-report affect protocol]`
      - D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun and Graesser then tested the claim that confusion "can be beneficial to learning if appropriately induced, regulated, and resolved," inducing it in two experiments (N = 64; N = 76) by having animated agents express incorrect or contradictory opinions. `[all three conditions required in the authors' formulation; lab trialogues on scientific reasoning]`
      - Self-reports of confusion were largely insensitive to the manipulations; confusion had to be inferred from more objective measures of learners' responses right after the contradictions. `[self-report failed as a measure here]`
      - The contradictions had no effect on learning when they failed to confuse learners, but multiple-choice posttest and transfer performance was substantially higher when they succeeded — "confused learners are more successful at applying knowledge to new situations."
      - The benefit is conditional on resolution. The authors separate confusion peripheral to the activity from contextually coupled confusion and state that "persistent confusion, which occurs when confusion resolution fails, is expected to accompany negligible or poor learning." A companion device-comprehension study found second-by-second that confusion peaking then dampening differed from confusion that kept rising, and that partial resolvers scored significantly better on the comprehension test.
      - Pekrun's **control-value theory** supplies the mechanism: achievement emotions arise from how much control a learner feels over a task and how much they value it. It covers activity emotions (enjoyment, frustration, boredom) and outcome emotions (hope, pride, anxiety, hopelessness, shame, anger), with corollaries on their multiplicity, domain specificity, antecedents, effects on engagement and achievement, regulation, development, and relative universality across genders and cultures. Domain specificity means observed frustration or boredom reflects the task, not a stable property of the child.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Confusion is a trajectory, not a state: learners hit an impasse and either resolve it back into flow or slide into frustration and then boredom. Induced confusion improved posttest and transfer scores only in learners it actually confused, and only when resolved. Self-report missed it entirely, and control-value theory makes the emotion a function of the task.
    - **Link to source:** [Dynamics of affective states during complex learning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.10.001), [Confusion can be beneficial for learning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2012.05.003), [The control-value theory of achievement emotions](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-006-9029-9)

- **Subcategory 5.8: Detector validity limits — *detectors do not survive a change of population***
  - **Source:** Ocumpaugh, Baker, Gowda, N. Heffernan & C. Heffernan (2014); Baker & Hawn (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Ocumpaugh and colleagues built detectors of boredom, confusion, engaged concentration, and frustration from middle-school mathematics logs and found that a detector trained mainly on one demographic grouping did not generalize to others, even ones counting as the same national or regional culture. `[three US subpopulations in one Northeastern region, one tutoring system]`
      - They performed best on their own training subpopulation (average κ = 0.26, A' = 0.67) and substantially worse elsewhere (average κ = 0.03, A' = 0.52) — **effectively chance agreement**.
      - Trained on all three subpopulations pooled, they reached κ = 0.18 for urban and κ = 0.16 for suburban students but only κ = 0.06, A' = 0.54 for rural students, "just barely" above chance. Pooled models beat single-group models yet still fall short of population validity, which the authors suspect may be unattainable.
      - Baker and Hawn's review documents algorithmic bias in education across race and ethnicity, gender, nationality, native language, urbanicity, parental educational background, socioeconomic status, disability, and having a parent in the military. Dropout and course-failure prediction was generally less effective for Black and Hispanic/Latino students, in one case producing different false-positive and false-negative profiles by racial group. One essay scorer gave 11th-grade Hispanic and Asian-American students significantly higher scores than human raters while marking down Arabic and Hindi speakers. Some algorithms were biased against female students and others against male, direction varying by study.
      - **The opposite of the intuitive fix:** nation-specific automated essay scoring models produced different skews between groups and *increased* bias compared with training on all groups together.
      - Their proposed "non-malleability test" holds that a knowledge detector's reduced accuracy on students who game is not bias as such, since gaming is chosen by the learner and influenceable by the designer, whereas race, national location, and gender are not.
      - Their own caveat: the evidence base "is highly sparse — in several cases, there is only a handful — or even just one — cases where a specific algorithmic bias has been documented," and the literature is America-centric. They found no work at all on bias by learner dialect, despite thinking some racial differences in essay scoring are probably dialectal. `[authors' caveat on their own review; dialect is a documented gap, not a documented bias]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Detectors do not survive a change of population. Trained on one of three US subpopulations, affect detectors fell from κ = 0.26 on their own group to κ = 0.03 on the others, which is chance — same country, same region, same software, same subject. The field's own review documents bias across nine demographic categories, and per-group training made essay-scoring bias worse.
    - **Link to source:** [Population validity for educational data mining models](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.12156), [Algorithmic bias in education](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-021-00285-9)

### Category 6: The dispositional constructs that "hustle" is usually meant to name, and how much of each survives meta-analysis

*Every trait invoked by the word "hustler" entered the literature large and left meta-analysis small; the two that held their size are a fixed trait and a teachable process, not a manufacturable virtue.*

- **Subcategory 6.1: Grit — *dissolves into conscientiousness***
  - **Source:** Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews & Kelly (2007); Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017); Duckworth (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Duckworth and colleagues defined grit as "perseverance and passion for long-term goals" and reported that it explained an average of 4% of the variance in success across six samples: educational attainment in two adult samples, Ivy League GPA, retention in two West Point classes, and National Spelling Bee ranking. (Throughout, *r* and *ρ* say how tightly two things move together — .10 is small, .50 large; *d* is a gap between groups in standard deviations — 0.20 small, 0.80 large.)
      - Grit correlated r = .77 with conscientiousness — the Big Five trait of being organized, disciplined and dependable — in Study 2 (N = 690), higher than with any other Big Five trait. At West Point it was unrelated to the academy's own Whole Candidate Score admissions composite (r = .02, ns) and to SAT score (r = .05, ns), yet cadets one standard deviation above average were more than 60% likelier to complete summer training (OR = 1.62, p < .001) — retention the composite did not predict at all — while predicting first-year GPA (r = .06) worse than self-control (r = .13) and far worse than the composite (r = .64).
      - **But** Credé, Tynan and Harms, pooling 584 effect sizes from 88 independent samples and 66,807 people (a meta-analysis combines many studies into one estimate), put grit's correlation with academic performance at ρ = .18 (k = 39, N = 13,141) and with overall GPA at ρ = .17 — against ρ = .05 with cognitive ability and ρ = .72 with self-control.
      - Grit correlated with conscientiousness at ρ = .84 (k = 22, N = 18,826) — perseverance facet ρ = .83, consistency facet ρ = .61 — close enough to raise the jangle fallacy, two names for one thing. Its higher-order structure (the claim that both facets belong to one underlying trait) was not confirmed, and the facets diverged: perseverance predicted performance at ρ = .26, consistency at ρ = .10, differing significantly (p < .001) on all four grade criteria.
      - Entering conscientiousness first, grit **adds nothing over conscientiousness**: incremental R = .000 for overall academic performance, .002 for high school GPA and .004 for college GPA, while perseverance of effort alone added .040, .085 and .023. (Incremental validity asks whether a new measure predicts anything the old one didn't.) Hence the authors' verdict that grit interventions "may only have weak effects," that its construct validity "is in question," and that its "primary utility ... may lie in the perseverance facet."
      - **The construct's own originator opposes this use.** Duckworth wrote that she worries she has "contributed, inadvertently, to an idea I vigorously oppose: high-stakes character assessment," and that schools should not be rewarded or punished on such measures; her published guidance says the scale "is not appropriate for selecting employees, admitting students to college, gauging the performance of teachers, or comparing schools or countries to each other," because "you can fake a higher grit score without much effort." She also reported eighth-graders at high-performing charter schools rating themselves lower on conscientiousness, self-control and grit than district-school peers. `T1` `[opinion writing, not a peer-reviewed retraction]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Grit launched as a 4%-of-variance predictor of success, strongest at predicting who stays rather than who achieves. Meta-analysis left it correlating ρ = .18 with achievement and ρ = .84 with conscientiousness, adding essentially zero over it; only the perseverance facet carries weight. Its originator now opposes high-stakes use, because the scale is fakeable and self-ratings fall in demanding schools.
    - **Link to source:** [Grit: perseverance and passion for long-term goals](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.6.1087), [Much ado about grit: a meta-analytic synthesis of the grit literature](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000102), [Don't grade schools on grit](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/03/27/opinion/sunday/dont-grade-schools-on-grit.html)

- **Subcategory 6.2: Growth mindset — *shrinks toward zero as study quality rises***
  - **Source:** Blackwell, Trzesniewski & Dweck (2007); Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara (2018); Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Blackwell and colleagues followed 373 seventh graders: believing intelligence is malleable predicted an upward trajectory in mathematics grades across two years of junior high, believing it fixed predicted a flat one. Their second study taught an incremental theory to 48 students against a control of 43; the control group's grades kept declining while the experimental group's decline reversed. `[intervention arm total sample 91 students]`
      - **But** Sisk and colleagues, across 273 effect sizes and 365,915 people, put the mindset–achievement correlation at r = .10, 95% CI [.08, .13], p < .001, with 157 (58%) of those effects not significantly different from zero, 16 (6%) significantly negative and 100 (37%) significantly positive. It faded with age: r = .19 for children, r = .15 for adolescents, r = .02 (p = .110) for adults.
      - Their meta-analysis of interventions (k = 43, N = 57,155) found an average effect of d = 0.08, 95% CI [0.02, 0.14], p = .010, with 37 of 43 (86%) not significantly different from zero. Against a passive control — a group given nothing — the effect was d = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.05, 0.10], p = .522; against an active control, a group given a comparable non-mindset activity, d = 0.08, p = .034.
      - Two subgroups ran against that null: low-SES households (7 effect sizes) at d = 0.34 versus d = 0.03 for middle- and upper-class students, Q(1) = 4.76, p = .029, and high-academic-risk students (8 effect sizes) at d = 0.19, p = .031. In the same paper, socioeconomic status did *not* moderate the mindset–achievement correlation, Q(1) = 1.48, p = .223.
      - Macnamara and Burgoyne, across 63 studies and 97,672 people, found interventions moved achievement by d̄ = 0.05, 95% CI [0.02, 0.09], nonsignificant after correcting for publication bias — the tendency for positive results to reach print while null ones do not. Authors with a financial incentive to report positive findings published significantly larger effects.
      - The two restrictions the theory most needs both come out null: among the 13 studies (N = 18,355) shown to have shifted students' mindsets as intended, d̄ = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.10]; among the 6 highest-quality studies (N = 13,571), d̄ = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.10]. No theoretically meaningful moderators were significant, and the apparent effects are "likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The founding study rests on 373 students and an intervention arm of 91. Pooled across 365,915 people, mindset correlates r = .10 with achievement and r = .02 in adults; interventions average d = 0.08, and d = 0.02 against a passive control. Quality-weighting cuts the estimate to 0.05, then 0.02 among the six best studies — **every tightening of method shrinks the effect**.
    - **Link to source:** [Implicit theories of intelligence predict achievement across an adolescent transition](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.00995.x), [To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement?](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704), [Do growth mindset interventions impact students' academic achievement?](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000352)

- **Subcategory 6.3: The residual growth-mindset effect — *real, small, and confined to lower achievers***
  - **Source:** Yeager et al. (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The trial drew a stratified random sample of 65 regular U.S. public schools and 12,490 ninth-grade adolescents, individually randomized to condition; the intervention was two self-administered online sessions of roughly 25 minutes each, about 20 days apart.
      - Among the pre-registered target group of lower-achieving students, it raised end-of-ninth-grade core course GPA by B = 0.10 grade points, 95% CI [0.04, 0.16], n = 6,320, P = 0.001, a standardized effect of 0.11. **Higher-achieving students showed nothing**: B = 0.01, n = 6,170, P = 0.634, standardized effect 0.01.
      - Beliefs moved three times as much as grades: fixed-mindset beliefs among lower achievers fell by a standardized 0.33, which puts the bottleneck in translating a changed belief into changed behavior rather than in changing the belief.
      - In low- and medium-achieving schools the treatment effect on core GPAs reached 0.14 grade points where the school sat in the third quartile of challenge-seeking norms and 0.18 in the highest quartile. That moderator was behavioral — control-group students chose between problems described as challenging and instructive or easy and uninstructive, and the school's norm was their average number of challenging choices — so **a behavioral measure carried information a belief measure did not**.
      - Downstream behavior moved too: taking advanced mathematics in tenth grade rose 3 percentage points, from 33% in control to 36% in treatment, n = 6,690, P = 0.001, and the model estimates a 5.3 percentage-point reduction (P = 0.005) in lower-achieving ninth graders finishing the year below a 2.0 GPA.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The largest pre-registered national test of a mindset intervention found a real but small effect, 0.10 grade points, and only among lower achievers. Beliefs moved three times as much as grades, and the effect reached 0.14–0.18 only where peers already valued challenge. A grades-only analysis would have missed the 3-point rise in advanced-math enrollment.
    - **Link to source:** [A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y)

- **Subcategory 6.4: Conscientiousness — *the magnitude held, but it is a trait, not a curriculum***
  - **Source:** Poropat (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Conscientiousness — the Big Five disposition to be organized, disciplined and dependable — correlated with academic performance at an observed r = .19 and a scale-corrected ρ = .22 across k = 138 samples and N = 70,926 participants, equivalent to d = 0.46.
      - It was strongest at the youngest level: ρ = .28 for primary education, ρ = .21 for secondary and ρ = .23 for tertiary. It was also the only Five-Factor Model dimension whose correlation with achievement did not decline from primary to later levels.
      - It is not a proxy for ability: its scale-corrected correlation with intelligence was slightly negative at −.03, while Openness had the highest correlation with intelligence (.15) without having the highest correlation with academic performance.
      - Holding secondary-level GPA constant, conscientiousness predicted tertiary GPA at a partial correlation of .17 — ahead of intelligence at .14, and far ahead of Agreeableness .05, Openness .03, Emotional Stability .01 and Extraversion .00.
      - Poropat concluded that conscientiousness "has the strongest association with academic performance of all the FFM dimensions; its association with academic performance rivaled that of intelligence except in primary education."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the construct meta-analysis did not shrink: ρ = .22 across 70,926 people, steady across levels of schooling and largest in the primary years. It is not a proxy for intelligence, and it out-predicts intelligence once prior grades are held constant. **The catch is the flip side of that stability** — a trait is not something a curriculum manufactures.
    - **Link to source:** [A meta-analysis of the five-factor model of personality and academic performance](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014996)

- **Subcategory 6.5: Deliberate practice — *large in closed domains, near the floor in education***
  - **Source:** Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993); Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, with 2018 corrigendum)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Ericsson and colleagues explained expert performance as "the end result of individuals' prolonged efforts to improve performance while negotiating motivational and external constraints," defining deliberate practice as effortful activity designed to optimize improvement and begun in childhood.
      - Their two load-bearing claims are that "individual differences, even among elite performers, are closely related to assessed amounts of deliberate practice," and that "many characteristics once believed to reflect innate talent are actually the result of intense practice extended for a minimum of 10 yrs."
      - **But** Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald found that practice quantity explains 12% of the variance in performance, 95% CI [9%, 15%], leaving 88% unexplained; a 2018 corrigendum revised this to 14%, 95% CI [11%, 18%].
      - The domain breakdown is the operative finding: 26% of variance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, **4% for education** (revised to 5% in the corrigendum) and less than 1% for professions. Domain was a significant moderator, Q(4) = 49.09, p < .001.
      - Their proposed mechanism is how predictable the task environment is: 24% of variance explained for highly predictable activities, 12% for moderately predictable and 4% for activities low in predictability — and education sits at the unpredictable end. Heterogeneity was high, I² = 84.90, so the point estimates hide wide variation between studies.
      - They concluded that "deliberate practice is important, but not as important as has been argued."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The 1993 framework recast expertise as accumulated structured effort, and it is the source of the belief that practice volume manufactures ability. Meta-analysis leaves practice explaining 12–14% of performance overall and **4–5% in education**, a fivefold gap below games and music. Predictable domains reward sheer volume; schooling is not one of them.
    - **Link to source:** [The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363), [Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810)

- **Subcategory 6.6: Self-regulated learning — *the one construct built to be taught***
  - **Source:** Zimmerman (2002); Dignath & Büttner (2008); Donker, de Boer, Kostons, Dignath van Ewijk & van der Werf (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Zimmerman defines self-regulated learning as a process students run rather than a quality they possess: "self-regulation is not a mental ability or an academic performance skill; rather it is the self-directive process by which learners transform their mental abilities into academic skills." It cycles through forethought, performance and self-reflection, the last combining self-judgment (self-evaluation and causal attribution) with self-reaction (self-satisfaction and adaptive or defensive responses).
      - The expert/novice contrast describes observable actions, not a self-reported disposition: novices "fail to engage in high-quality forethought and instead attempt to self-regulate their learning reactively," set no specific goals, do not self-monitor systematically, and "rely on comparisons with the performance of others to judge their learning effectiveness."
      - Attribution after failure is the hinge: blaming fixed ability "can be very damaging motivationally because it implies that efforts to improve on a future test will not be effective," whereas blaming a controllable process such as the wrong solution strategy "will sustain motivation." The branch that follows is observable — defensive reactions "protect one's self-image by withdrawing or avoiding opportunities to learn and perform," adaptive ones are "adjustments designed to increase the effectiveness of one's method of learning."
      - Dignath and Büttner, across 49 primary-school and 35 secondary-school studies and 357 effect sizes, found an average intervention effect of 0.69, with higher effects in mathematics than in reading/writing or other subjects and differential effects by theoretical background and strategy type at the two school levels. Restricted to academic performance, Donker and colleagues report "Cohen's d = 0.61 (0.05) for primary schools and 0.54 (0.11) for secondary schools."
      - Donker and colleagues' own meta-analysis of 58 studies, 95 interventions and 180 effect sizes found substantial effects in writing (Hedges' g = 1.25), science (.73), mathematics (.66) and comprehensive reading (.36), with metacognitive knowledge instruction "valuable in all" domains and "no differential effects were observed for students with different ability levels."
      - **Two findings temper the headline:** at both school levels, effects were higher when researchers rather than regular teachers ran the training, and "the effects were higher when self-developed tests were used than in the case of intervention-independent tests."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Self-regulation is defined as a process — plan, monitor, reflect — not a possessed quality, making it the only construct here a program can straightforwardly teach, and its steps are observable rather than self-reported. Interventions average 0.69, and 0.54–0.61 on achievement, an order of magnitude above mindset. **But** researcher-run training and researcher-built tests inflate those figures.
    - **Link to source:** [Becoming a self-regulated learner: an overview](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2), [Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x), [Effectiveness of learning strategy instruction on academic performance](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2013.11.002)

- **Subcategory 6.7: "Academic tenacity" and "productive persistence" — *umbrella labels whose credible evidence is modest***
  - **Source:** Dweck, Walton & Cohen (2014); Silva & White (2013); Yamada & Bryk (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Dweck, Walton and Cohen define academic tenacity as the mindsets and skills that let students "look beyond short-term concerns to longer-term or higher-order goals" and "withstand challenges and setbacks to persevere toward these goals" — "at its most basic level, academic tenacity is about working hard (and working smart) for a long time." `T1` `[foundation white paper, not peer-reviewed]`
      - Their causal claim is strong: interventions targeting these psychological factors "can transform students' experience and achievement in school, improving core academic outcomes such as GPA and test scores months and even years later." It was published before the meta-analyses that deflated its two main constituent constructs. `T1`
      - Silva and White's parallel program defines productive persistence as "the package of skills and tenacity that students need to succeed in an academic setting" — an "evidence-based package of practical student activities and faculty actions," bundling formalized team roles in group work, a contract committing to a healthy learning environment, and a reading on the malleability of the brain, so the mindset component cannot be isolated from the structural redesign. `T1`
      - Its headline figure is a before/after contrast: 5.9% of developmental mathematics students earned college-level mathematics credit within one year at baseline, rising to 23.5% after four years, while 51% completed the full pathway with a grade of C or better after a year of the program. `T1` `[before/after contrast, not a counterfactual]`
      - **The peer-reviewed number is smaller.** Yamada and Bryk matched 928 treatment against 4,549 comparison students on 44 characteristics across 17 community colleges in five states, and found "large effects of Statway on students attaining college math credit with persisting effects into the following year" — 5.57 college credits one year after completion against 4.08 — holding "for each gender and race/ethnicity groups and for students with different math placement levels." **But** students were not randomly assigned, so the design is nonexperimental.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Academic tenacity is the umbrella label, defined almost exactly as an informal notion of a hustler would be, and its strong causal claim comes from a foundation white paper predating the meta-analyses that deflated its constituents. Its operational cousin bundles mindset with structural redesign, so neither can be isolated. The credible figure is 5.57 against 4.08 credits, from a nonrandom design.
    - **Link to source:** [Academic tenacity: mindsets and skills that promote long-term learning](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED576649), [Pathways to improvement: using psychological strategies to help college students master developmental math](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED560149), [Assessing the first two years' effectiveness of Statway](https://doi.org/10.1177/0091552116643162)

- **Subcategory 6.8: The self-report measurement problem — *asking and observing measure different things***
  - **Source:** Duckworth & Kern (2011); West, Kraft, Finn, Martin, Duckworth, Gabrieli & Gabrieli (2016); Duckworth & Yeager (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Duckworth and Kern, across k = 282 samples and N = 33,564 participants, found that measures of self-control converge only moderately overall: r = .27, 95% CI [.24, .30].
      - The disagreement is systematic rather than noisy. Informant-report questionnaires agree with each other at r = .54 and self-reports at r = .50, but executive function tasks agree at r = .15 and delay-of-gratification tasks at r = .21; across families, delay tasks correlate with questionnaires at r = .15 and executive function tasks at r = .10, so asked and observed measures share on the order of 1–2% of their variance. Over half the heterogeneity came from measure type, and correlations did not vary by gender, sample type, publication year or publication status — self-control is "a coherent but multidimensional construct best assessed using multiple methods."
      - West and colleagues surveyed 1,368 eighth graders. At the student level, scales measuring conscientiousness, self-control, grit and growth mindset correlated positively with attendance, behavior and test-score gains between fourth and eighth grade, exactly as theory predicts. **But** conscientiousness, self-control and grit were unrelated to test-score gains at the school level, and students at over-subscribed charter schools scored 0.23 standard deviations lower on self-control than district-school students, and lower on conscientiousness and grit, **despite making larger test-score gains**.
      - Admissions-lottery estimates — random lottery assignment used to isolate the school's own causal effect — put one year of over-subscribed charter attendance at −0.211 (p < .05) on self-control, −0.119 (p < .05) on grit, −0.157 (p < .1) on conscientiousness and −0.030 (ns) on growth mindset, while absences fell by 1.42 days (p < .05) per year and suspensions were unchanged. Real declines, the authors note, would bring "corresponding increases in suspensions and absences rather than the null and negative effects we observe," so they attribute the pattern to reference bias — your self-rating depends on the standard around you.
      - Duckworth and Yeager conclude that "we do not believe any available measure is suitable for between-school accountability judgments": questionnaires "may potentially produce the opposite finding of the truth" in between-school or over-time comparisons, and existing questionnaire and task measures "are rarely sufficiently reliable to use for diagnosis and may produce group biases."
      - They state the trade-off without declaring a winner. Performance tasks "do not rely upon the subjective judgments of students or teachers," which "circumvents reference bias, social desirability bias, acquiescence bias, and faking," and they "could be more sensitive than questionnaires to subtle changes in behavior." **But** a one-time task assesses "the capacity of a student to perform a certain behavior when maximally motivated," not "their everyday behavior in typical life situations," and it carries practice effects and sensitivity to administration conditions. Their own remedy is "opportunistically mining students' online learning behavior or written communication in real time."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Measures of the same construct agree only at r = .27, and the split runs along method: questionnaires with questionnaires near .50, behavioral tasks with questionnaires at .10–.15. Lottery estimates make the consequence concrete — a year at a high-performing school lowered self-reported grit and self-control while absences fell. **The self-reports moved, not the students.**
    - **Link to source:** [A meta-analysis of the convergent validity of self-control measures](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2011.02.004), [Promise and paradox: measuring students' non-cognitive skills and the impact of schooling](https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373715597298), [Measurement matters: assessing personal qualities other than cognitive ability for educational purposes](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X15584327)

### Category 7: What adaptive and machine-delivered instruction has actually been measured to deliver

*Adaptive and machine tutoring measurably delivers roughly 0.4 to 0.8 standard deviations on tests aligned to what it taught, collapsing toward zero on standardized tests and weakest in K-12 — and it has never been measured against domain-general reasoning.*

- **Subcategory 7.1: Human versus machine tutoring — *the gap nearly closes, and finer interaction stops helping***
  - **Source:** VanLehn (2011); Kulik & Fletcher (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An **intelligent tutoring system** models what a learner knows and responds to their individual work. Results are **effect sizes** — written *d*, *g*, or "sigma" — standard deviations of gain over a comparison group; **two sigma** means two standard deviations.
      - Systems are ranked by how fine-grained the interaction is: **answer-based** reacts only to a final answer, **step-based** to each step of a solution, **substep-based** inside a single step. Most intelligent tutoring systems are step- or substep-based; most products labelled CAI, CBT, or CAL are answer-based — the tier that measured around 0.31, not the tier that measured 0.76.
      - VanLehn set out to test the widely held belief that answer-based systems, intelligent tutoring systems, and adult human tutors produce d = 0.3, 1.0, and 2.0 against no tutoring, and reported that the review "did not confirm these beliefs."
      - Measured, human tutoring came in at d = 0.79 and intelligent tutoring systems at d = 0.76 — the human-machine gap effectively disappears.
      - **But** grain stops paying below the step: in Kulik and Fletcher's independent re-analysis, step-based tutoring raised test scores 0.76 standard deviations while substep-based raised them only 0.40.
      - That re-analysis put the median human-tutoring effect at 0.79 standard deviations across the 10 studies in VanLehn's set — and at 0.68 with one study excluded. `[k = 10 studies; dropping one study moves the median 0.11]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Folklore had machines trailing humans by a wide margin; measurement says 0.76 against 0.79. Granularity pays once — answer-based to step-based — then plateaus and reverses. And the human benchmark is fragile: removing a single study from a ten-study set moves the median from 0.79 to 0.68, which undercuts every later human-versus-AI comparison built on it.
    - **Link to source:** [The relative effectiveness of human tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and other tutoring systems](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369), [Effectiveness of intelligent tutoring systems: a meta-analytic review](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315581420)

- **Subcategory 7.2: The four tutoring meta-analyses disagree — *and the K-12 result is the weakest***
  - **Source:** Kulik & Fletcher (2016); Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper (2013); Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper (2014); Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kulik and Fletcher, across 50 controlled evaluations, found a median effect of 0.66 standard deviations over conventional levels — the 50th to the 75th percentile.
      - Their strongest **moderator** — a feature that changes the measured effect — was the outcome test: a **local test** is written to match what the system taught, a **standardized test** is an off-the-shelf commercial or state test. Test type correlated r = −.63 with effect size: local, N = 38, M = 0.73; both combined, N = 3, M = 0.45; standardized, N = 9, M = 0.13. Inside one product line, 15 Cognitive Tutor studies averaged 0.76 local and 0.12 standardized.
      - Their other moderators cut against a children's product: 0.44 elementary and high school against 0.75 postsecondary; 0.40 mathematics against 0.72 other subjects; 0.78 for samples up to 80 participants against 0.53 for 81 to 250.
      - Steenbergen-Hu and Cooper, restricting to K-12 mathematics (26 reports, 34 independent samples, published 1997–2010), found "no negative and perhaps a small positive effect," g = 0.01 to g = 0.09: fixed-effect 0.05, 95% CI [.02, .09], p < .005; random-effects 0.09, 95% CI [−.03, .20], p = .136, not significantly different from zero. Of 26 unadjusted overall effect sizes, 17 were positive, 8 negative, 1 exactly 0.
      - Two moderators there are hostile to a long-running program: effects were greater when interventions ran less than a school year than a year or longer, and greater for the general population than for low achievers.
      - Their higher-education companion (35 reports, 39 studies) found g = .32 to g = .37, "less effective than human tutoring but outperformed all other instruction methods and learning activities," with earlier studies significantly stronger than recent ones. `[college students only]`
      - Ma, Adesope, Nesbit and Liu pooled 107 effect sizes from 14,321 participants: random-effects mean g = .41, above teacher-led large-group instruction (g = .42), non-ITS computer-based instruction (g = .57), and textbooks or workbooks (g = .35), with no significant difference from individualized human tutoring (g = −.11) or small-group instruction (g = .05).
      - **But** their effects held at all education levels and almost all subject domains **whether or not the system provided feedback or modeled student misconceptions** — the two features usually claimed as the mechanism — and 18 further moderators produced no substantive significant differences.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Four meta-analyses of one technology return 0.66, 0.01–0.09, 0.32–0.37, and 0.41, and the spread is structured rather than random. **The effect collapses from 0.73 to 0.13 when the test is standardized.** The K-12 analysis, the most directly relevant of the four, is also the weakest: indistinguishable from zero, shrinking as programs run longer, smaller for low achievers.
    - **Link to source:** [Effectiveness of intelligent tutoring systems: a meta-analytic review](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315581420), [Intelligent tutoring systems and K–12 students' mathematical learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032447), [Intelligent tutoring systems and college students' academic learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034752), [Intelligent tutoring systems and learning outcomes: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037123)

- **Subcategory 7.3: Bloom's two sigma — *never reproduced***
  - **Source:** Bloom (1984); Cohen, Kulik & Kulik (1982); Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (2024); von Hippel (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The claim rests on two doctoral students' dissertations comparing conventional classes of about 30, mastery learning at the same class size plus formative tests and corrective procedures, and tutoring at one tutor per one to three students plus that same formative-test cycle — four samples at grades four, five, and eight, two subject matters, 11 periods over a 3-week block each. `[3-week intervention; two narrow topics; no long-run or standardized outcome]`
      - "The average student under tutoring was about two standard deviations above the average of the control class" — but "the average student under mastery learning was about one standard deviation above the average of the control class," so half the headline arrives without tutoring.
      - The conditions differed on more than tutoring: time on task was 65% conventional, 75% mastery learning, 90%+ tutoring; aptitude–achievement correlations typically moved from +.60 conventional to +.85 mastery learning and +.25 tutoring.
      - Cohen, Kulik and Kulik — two years *before* Bloom's essay — reviewed 65 independent evaluations of school tutoring and found an average effect of .40 (SE .069) across the 52 reporting achievement results, meaning 66% of tutored pupils outperformed the average non-tutored pupil. The local-versus-standardized split appears here too: .84 across the 12 studies using instructor-developed tests against .27 across the 40 using commercial standardized tests, and the 6 studies with treatments of 0 to 4 weeks averaged .95 — short and novel maximizes the number.
      - The best modern benchmark, a systematic review and meta-analysis of preK-12 tutoring field experiments, pooled to 0.288 SD (SE = 0.029, p < .001). `[randomized experiments only]`
      - Its earlier working-paper version reported 0.37 SD. `T1` `[working paper; published estimate is a downward revision of about 0.08 SD]`
      - Bloom said he adapted his estimates from a paper published the prior month, whose tutoring estimate was 0.4 standard deviations — the figure he relabeled "peer and cross-age remedial tutoring" while entering 2.00 for "tutorial instruction." His own Table 1 lists the two separately, at .40 sigma and 2.00 sigma. `T1` `[practitioner magazine, not peer-reviewed; checkable against Bloom 1984 Table 1 and Walberg 1984]`
      - In one underlying dissertation, tutored students scored 11 percentage points higher on the retest than the initial test in week one, 20 points higher in week two, and 30 points higher in week three; roughly half their documented gains likely came from those extra quizzes and feedback, which mastery learning also used — the testing effect, not adaptivity. Of the 65 tutoring studies reviewed in 1982 only one reported a two-sigma effect; of the 96 in the modern review, **none did.** `T1` `[secondary reanalysis of an unpublished dissertation]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Bloom's evidence base is two unpublished dissertations, four samples, two unfamiliar topics, and eleven class periods over three weeks — and his own mastery-learning condition supplied about half the two sigma without tutoring at all. A meta-analysis published two years earlier already said 0.40; the best modern randomized benchmark is 0.288 SD. **No study in 96 has reproduced two sigma.**
    - **Link to source:** [The 2 sigma problem](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X013006004), [Educational outcomes of tutoring: a meta-analysis of findings](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312019002237), [The promise of tutoring for preK–12 learning](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312231208687), [Two-sigma tutoring: separating science fiction from science fact](https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/)

- **Subcategory 7.4: Which kinds of adaptivity have evidence — *knowledge yes, learning style no***
  - **Source:** Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu (2014); Wang, Huang, Sommer, Pei, Shidfar, Rehman, Ritzhaupt & Martin (2024); Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Adaptivity** means changing what a learner gets based on that learner. Aleven and colleagues sort it into a 3×5 grid crossing three timescales — **design-loop** (fixed when the material is authored), **task-loop** (choosing the next task), **step-loop** (responding within a task) — against five psychological realms: knowledge, path through an activity, affect and motivation, self-regulation, and learning styles. They report evidence of varying strength for 13 of the 15 cells. `[handbook chapter; narrative review, no pooled effect size]`
      - The comparison that best isolates adaptivity — intelligent tutoring against non-adaptive computer-based instruction — produced the largest single comparison in Ma and colleagues' meta-analysis, g = .57, above the advantage over teacher-led large-group instruction (g = .42) or textbooks and workbooks (g = .35); for scale, they note a second-order meta-analysis finding .31 for computer-based instruction as the primary means of instruction against traditional classroom teaching. `[a between-study moderator contrast, not a within-experiment manipulation]`
      - Wang and colleagues, searching 18 databases, identified 45 independent studies of AI-enabled adaptive learning and found an effect on cognitive learning outcomes of g = 0.70 (k = 47) against non-adaptive interventions.
      - Their effect was significantly moderated by publication type, origin of study, student classification level, student discipline, duration, and research design. **But** the type of AI in the adaptive engine did not moderate it — direct evidence against the idea that a more sophisticated model produces more learning.
      - On the task loop, evidence is called strong that mastery learning implemented in technology is practical and effective in real classrooms, with cognitive mastery task selection substantially improving effectiveness and efficiency over giving every student the same problem set.
      - Evidence for adapting to a learner's preferred style is weak. A review of computer-assisted instruction concluded that if **aptitude–treatment interactions** — where a method helps one kind of learner and hurts another — exist for cognitive or learning styles, "they seem to be infrequent and small in magnitude," with only 9 of 65 contrasts (14%) yielding a statistically significant interaction.
      - The 2025 edition reports empirical support for 12 of 15 forms of adaptivity and names the best return on implementation effort as step-loop adaptation to student strategies and errors, design-loop adaptivity generally, and task-loop adaptation to student knowledge.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Holding the medium constant and varying only whether the system models the learner gives g = .57, against .31 for plain computer instruction over classroom teaching, and 0.70 in the newest estimate. **But** the type of AI changed nothing, and the only review isolating specific forms supports adapting to what a learner knows, not to a diagnosed style.
    - **Link to source:** [Intelligent tutoring systems and learning outcomes: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037123), [Efficacy of AI-enabled adaptive learning systems](https://doi.org/10.1177/07356331241240459), [Instruction based on adaptive learning technologies](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~aleven/Papers/2016/Aleven_etal_Handbook2017_AdaptiveLearningTechnologies.pdf)

- **Subcategory 7.5: Learner control versus system control — *learners choose badly, and predictably***
  - **Source:** Kirschner & van Merriënboer (2013); Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger (2017), reporting Atkinson (1972)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kirschner and van Merriënboer identify three "urban legends" on the theme "that it is the learner who knows best and that she or he should be the controlling force in her or his learning"; the third is that learners are self-educators who should get maximum control over what they learn and their learning trajectory. They place full learner control at the end of the evidence continuum, where "there is a tiny bit of incomplete support — but the legend itself is false or at least a severe overgeneralization."
      - Citing a meta-analysis of learner preference for selecting instructional methods, they report preference was typically uncorrelated or negatively correlated with outcomes — learners who preferred a technique typically did not derive instructional benefit from experiencing it.
      - Given full control, low general-ability students kept practicing tasks they liked or were already proficient in and were reluctant to start new, unfamiliar tasks. An electronic development portfolio helped them choose better, **but only when combined with regular coaching meetings.**
      - Their recommendation is neither extreme: "to appropriately limit students' control in consideration of their" competence.
      - Of four task-selection approaches tested for learning German vocabulary, the two using student response history for automated item selection showed the greatest gain on a delayed posttest — more than both learner control and random selection. `[adult learners; single vocabulary domain; over 50 years old]`
      - Adapting to student self-regulation in the task loop can work "especially when an **open learner model** [is combined] with additional support for self-assessment and learner-controlled task selection" — that is, when the system shows the child its own picture of what they do and do not yet know.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Given free choice, low-ability learners drill what they already know and avoid what they do not — the exact inverse of a tool meant to target a child's weakest areas — and preference can be negatively related to benefit. Automated response-history selection beat learner choice on a *delayed* posttest. The evidenced middle: the system sequences, the child sees the model.
    - **Link to source:** [Do learners really know best? Urban legends in education](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.804395), [Instruction based on adaptive learning technologies](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~aleven/Papers/2016/Aleven_etal_Handbook2017_AdaptiveLearningTechnologies.pdf)

- **Subcategory 7.6: Language-model tutors at field scale — *real effects, no retention interval measured***
  - **Source:** De Simone, Tiberti, Barron Rodriguez, Manolio, Mosuro & Dikoru (2025); located absence across the trials assembled in this document
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A randomized controlled trial deployed a GPT-4-powered assistant for first-year senior secondary English language learning over six weeks — twelve 90-minute sessions in computer labs, students working in pairs under teacher supervision — across nine public secondary schools in Edo State, Nigeria. `T1` `[working paper, not peer-reviewed; supervised computer labs with paired students, not an autonomous tool]`
      - It produced 0.31 standard deviations on a composite assessment covering English, AI knowledge, and digital skills, and 0.23 standard deviations on English, the main outcome of interest. Counting every assigned student, effects by section were English 0.24 σ, digital skills 0.14 σ, AI skills 0.31 σ, composite 0.26 σ; on the third-term curricular exam, whose content spanned the entire year rather than only the six weeks of intervention, the effect was 0.21 standard deviations. `T1`
      - Cost-effectiveness analysis equated the gains to 1.5 to 2 years of "business-as-usual" schooling. `T1`
      - The program benefited students across the baseline ability distribution, **but the largest effects were for female students and those with higher initial academic performance** — the wrong direction for an equity claim, echoing the finding that tutoring systems helped general-population students more than low achievers. `T1`
      - **No post-withdrawal retention measure was located.** No trial assembled here reports a retention or transfer measure taken more than a few weeks after the intervention ended; the longest measured horizon is a third-term exam given shortly after a six-week program. `[absence located by targeted search across De Simone 2025, Kestin et al. 2025, and Bastani et al. 2025 — not a published null result, and not a claim no such study exists anywhere]`
      - As of early 2024, one review of the tutoring literature stated that "no evidence on the effects of chatbot tutoring has yet been published." `T1` `[true when written and since superseded; recorded to date the evidence base]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The largest school-age language-model trial located, and the only one measuring a broader outcome than the material taught: a whole-year exam still showed 0.21 σ. **But** it is a working paper, it ran in supervised labs with paired students rather than as an autonomous tool, and it helped stronger students most. Nothing here measures what survives months after withdrawal — the interval a durable-reasoning claim rests on.
    - **Link to source:** [From chalkboards to chatbots: evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11125)

- **Subcategory 7.7: Machine-generated reasoning items — *equivalent for adults, uncalibrated for children***
  - **Source:** Matzen, Benz, Dixon, Posey, Kroger & Speed (2010); Gierl, Lai, Pugh, Touchie, Boulais & De Champlain (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Automatic item generation** means producing test questions from software rules instead of writing each by hand. Matzen and colleagues analyzed the relation types in Raven's original Standard Progressive Matrices and built software that recombines those same relation types to user-chosen parameters, supporting six basic shapes with five fill patterns, five sizes, four or five orientations, and six numerosity variants — 12,900 possible shape variants.
      - The norming study used 840 representative generated 3×3 matrices; 80 undergraduates (mean age 20) each completed all 60 Standard Progressive Matrices items plus one of 20 counterbalanced lists of 42 generated matrices. `[adult undergraduate sample; no child sample]`
      - The generated items behaved like the originals. Cronbach's alpha was .73 for the comparable subset of Standard Progressive Matrices and .76 for the generated matrices; accuracy on the two sets correlated .69, and .93 once **corrected for attenuation** — what the correlation would be if both tests were perfectly reliable. Paired t tests found no significant accuracy difference for two-relation problems (p = .31) or one-relation problems (p = .09), and the generated matrices "both covered and expanded on the range of problem difficulties provided by the SPMs."
      - Difficulty was controllable by construction: a one-way ANOVA found no significant difference across the five transformation types but a significant effect of transformation direction, F(4,19) = 5.93, p < .01, with outward transformations significantly harder than any other direction.
      - Gierl and colleagues call their study "one of the first empirical studies designed to evaluate the psychometric properties of generated multiple-choice items," using results from a high-stakes national medical licensure examination; before it, because such items had seen limited operational use, "the psychometric characteristics of generated multiple-choice test items are largely unknown and undocumented." Item analysis found generated items measured performance across a broad range of ability levels with consistently strong **discrimination** — sharply separating stronger from weaker examinees — and that the incorrect options consistently differentiated low from high performers. `[medical licensure content; adult examinees]`
      - **No study was located** evaluating the psychometric equivalence of language-model-generated reasoning practice items for children. `[absence located by targeted search; located evidence covers medical licensure items and matrix items for adult undergraduates]`
      - **No reasoning-transfer outcome was located either.** None of the six tutoring-system reviews assembled in this category reports a fluid-reasoning or matrix-reasoning transfer measure; every reported outcome is a domain achievement test. `[absence located by targeted search across the sources assembled here]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** For matrix items — the closest thing here to a domain-general reasoning task — machine generation is demonstrably equivalent to human authoring: .76 against .73 reliability, .69 raw and .93 corrected correlation, no significant difficulty difference, and difficulty known before a child sees the item. **But** that was verified on adults. With no child calibration and no reasoning-transfer outcome located, a reasoning claim names an outcome this evidence base has never measured.
    - **Link to source:** [Recreating Raven's: software for systematically generating large numbers of Raven-like matrix problems](https://doi.org/10.3758/BRM.42.2.525), [Evaluating the psychometric characteristics of generated multiple-choice test items](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957347.2016.1171768)
