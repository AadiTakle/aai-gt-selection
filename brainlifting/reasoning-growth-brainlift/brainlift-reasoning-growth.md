# BrainLift: Training a Trait That Is Supposed to Be Fixed — What Moves Reasoning Ability, and What a Learning System Must Watch to Find the Children Who Move Fastest

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose

Reasoning ability is the part of a child's profile that instruction is least expected to touch. It is
the quantity a cognitive-abilities test is designed to measure *because* it is supposed to resist
coaching, and a program that claims to raise it is claiming to move the one thing the instrument was
built to hold still. This BrainLift asks two questions that are normally asked separately and that
in fact constrain each other: **what, if anything, has been shown to actually move measured reasoning
ability rather than merely move the score** — and **what a software learning environment can observe
about a child that distinguishes real, committed improvement from compliance, familiarity, or
gaming.** The organizing discipline on the instructional side is that *the training raised the score*,
*the training raised the underlying ability*, *the gain survived the removal of the training*, and
*the gain appeared on material the training never covered* are four separate claims resting on four
separate evidence bases, and that a program which cannot tell them apart will report the weakest one
in the language of the strongest. The same discipline applies on the measurement side: *the child is
working*, *the child is learning*, *the child is improving faster than comparable children*, and
*that faster improvement is a durable property of the child rather than of the material* are also
four claims, and a behavioral telemetry stream answers the first far more readily than the last.
It argues from external evidence and general principles only, and is written to be read with no
knowledge of any particular system or implementation.

### In Scope

- **Whether measured reasoning ability is movable at all, and by what** — the working-memory training
  controversy and its opposed meta-analyses, the quasi-experimental evidence that schooling itself
  raises measured intelligence, the fadeout literature, and the population-level environmental
  movement recorded by the Flynn effect and its reversal.
- **Programs that train reasoning directly, and the transfer problem they have to clear** — the
  inductive-reasoning training tradition and what it reports transferring to, the mediated-learning
  tradition and its evidence base, the formal taxonomy of transfer distance, and the case that
  transfer is rare enough to be treated as the exception.
- **How much of an apparent reasoning gain is bought by familiarity rather than learning** — coaching
  effects on aptitude tests and their relationship to contact time, and retest gains on cognitive
  ability tests in the absence of any instruction at all.
- **The instructional mechanisms with the strongest evidence, and the boundary condition where each
  one stops working or reverses** — retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, desirable difficulties,
  worked examples and expertise reversal, cognitive load, feedback's documented capacity to lower
  performance, self-explanation, and mastery learning against its own headline benchmark.
- **The performance/learning distinction as a measurement problem** — why improvement visible inside a
  session is a poor index of durable learning, and the field-experimental evidence that assistance
  which raises practice performance can lower performance once it is withdrawn.
- **What a learning system can actually observe, and what those observables have been shown to mean** —
  validated behavioral detectors built from real interaction logs: gaming the system, persistent
  practice that never reaches mastery, help avoidance and help abuse, carelessness, off-task
  behavior, and the affective states that precede disengagement.
- **The dispositional constructs that an effort-and-improvement profile is usually meant to name, and
  how much of each survives meta-analysis** — grit, growth mindset, conscientiousness, deliberate
  practice, and self-regulated learning, each taken at both its original claim and its subsequent
  meta-analytic revision.
- **What adaptive and machine-delivered instruction has been measured to deliver** — tutoring-system
  effect sizes against the benchmark they are usually compared to, and the current evidence on
  language-model tutors on both sides.
- **Provenance discipline throughout** — what is peer-reviewed, what is a preprint or a foundation
  report, what rests on adult or undergraduate rather than child samples, what is a single trial
  against a meta-analysis, and what was searched for and not located.

### Out of Scope

- **Scoring how fast an individual learns** — whether a rate of learning is a well-defined quantity,
  whether it can be estimated for one person, and whether that estimate is stable enough to order
  people are owned by the companion `learning-rate-scoring` BrainLift. This document asks what would
  make a child improve and what improvement looks like in a log, not how to turn that into a number
  fit to rank children.
- **Diagnosing which reasoning areas a particular child is weak in** — the measurement design that
  produces a per-child weakness profile is an assessment problem. This document begins after such a
  profile exists and asks what should be done with it.
- **Instrument-level measurement validity and tail quality** — owned by the companion
  `gifted-assessment-quality` BrainLift.
- **The statistical methodology for validating a new instrument against an established one** — owned
  by the companion `test-evaluation` BrainLift.
- **The structure and sequencing of a test experience** — adaptive routing, item ordering, and retake
  policy are owned by the companion `test-structure` BrainLift.
- **Which measured signals earn their place in an ability estimate** — owned by the companion
  `metric-evidence` BrainLift. Behavioral signals appear here as candidate observations about
  learning, not as candidate features in an ability composite.
- **Causal identification, and separating a program's effect from selection into it** — owned by the
  companion `gt-school-counterfactual` BrainLift. Nothing assembled here licenses a claim that an
  observed improvement was caused by a program.
- **Whether the observed work is the child's own** — owned by the companion `proctoring-integrity`
  BrainLift.
- **The ethics and governance of profiling children's effort and affect from interaction logs.** The
  documented fairness problems in behavioral detectors are recorded here as evidence; the policy
  question of what may be collected and retained about a child is a separate problem this document
  does not settle.
- **Any mapping of these findings onto a particular system, curriculum, configuration, or plan** —
  belongs in a separate design document, not this BrainLift.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

> **Owner-authored. Not yet written.**
>
> A SPOV is a prescriptive, debatable, evidence-backed, actionable stance — the answer to *"if my DOK 3
> insight is true, what best practice is wrong, and what new rule must we follow?"* The spikiness test is
> that twenty informed people would argue about it. These have to originate with the owner; a stance
> assembled by someone else is not defensible in a review, because the defense is the point.
>
> The research base below is assembled and citation-verified. `candidate-tensions.md` in this directory
> records every place two sources in that base contradict each other, with both sides named. A tension
> becomes an insight only after the owner has interrogated it, and an insight becomes a SPOV only after
> the owner has decided what must change.
>
> Provocation questions for the DOK 4 pass, once DOK 3 exists:
>
> - Schooling raises measured intelligence by 1–5 points a year and computerized training raises it by
>   nothing once the design is clean. What property does a year of school have that a training program
>   does not, and is that property something software can have — or is the honest conclusion that the
>   dose is the mechanism and no short program qualifies?
> - The strongest positive reasoning-training result measures transfer using tests built from the same
>   item classes the program trains. If that is disqualifying, what would count as a fair transfer
>   measure for a reasoning program — and does any product that also *sells* preparation for a reasoning
>   test have standing to run one?
> - Retest alone buys about a third of a standard deviation on reasoning tests, more in children, and
>   stops after the third sitting. What claim survives being told that a third of your measured gain
>   would have happened anyway?
> - Every disposition that "hustle" could name either collapses into conscientiousness or fails to
>   replicate — except self-regulated learning, which is defined as a trainable process rather than a
>   trait. Is that a fact about the constructs, or a fact about self-report, or both, and what follows
>   for a system that can observe behavior continuously?
> - The wheel-spinning literature says the most visible effort signal is frequently futile, and futility
>   is detectable by the fourth attempt but not the first three. If persistence is only sometimes the
>   thing you want, what is the observable that separates the two — and is it a property of the child at
>   all, or of the match between the child and the material?
> - A guardrailed AI tutor removed the harm and delivered no benefit while making students feel they had
>   learned more. What does that imply about what an AI layer is *for* in a learning product?

## Experts

> Chosen for productive tension. The ten below form five pairs, and the two members of each pair are on
> record against each other on a question this BrainLift has to answer: whether reasoning training
> transfers, whether any broad intervention moves measured ability, whether a machine tutor teaches or
> substitutes, whether an effort disposition is a real construct, and whether a behavioral record tells
> you what you want to know. A sixth live dispute — whether instructional mechanisms like interleaving
> and feedback help or harm — is carried in the Knowledge Tree rather than here, because it runs largely
> inside single research programs whose own authors have published the reversals.

- **Expert 1 — Karl Josef Klauer (1929–2023)**
  - **Who:** German psychologist and educational scientist; Professor of Education at RWTH Aachen University from 1976 until his retirement in 1994, previously Professor of Education at TU Braunschweig and Professor of special-needs pedagogy at Cologne, with a 1967 habilitation whose subject was the influence of training methods on the intellectual performance of weaker-ability children.
  - **Focus:** A prescriptive theory of inductive reasoning decomposed into six problem classes, and a family of age-graded training programs built to teach the comparison strategies that theory specifies.
  - **Why Follow:** He is the strongest positive pole in the entire debate. His meta-analysis of 74 training experiments with roughly 3,600 children reports about half a standard deviation of improvement on fluid-intelligence tests, effects that grew rather than faded across follow-ups of 3 to 15 months, and a near-zero result for alternatively trained placebo groups that rules out attention and social contact as the mechanism. Any claim that reasoning cannot be taught has to answer this result first. Two features of it are also the reasons to read him critically: the intelligence measures were composed wholly or partly of the same inductive item types the program trains, and the meta-analysis of the program was conducted by the person who built it.
  - **Where:** [Review of Educational Research meta-analysis (2008)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313402), [Extension of the meta-analysis (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123), [Biographical entry](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Josef_Klauer)

- **Expert 2 — Randall W. Engle**
  - **Who:** Professor of Psychology at the Georgia Institute of Technology, where he chaired the School of Psychology for 13 years and founded the Center for Advanced Brain Imaging; elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2020; former editor of *Current Directions in Psychological Science*.
  - **Focus:** Working memory capacity and attention control, the relationship between them and fluid intelligence, and the methodological standards a training study must meet before its transfer claim can be believed.
  - **Why Follow:** He is the pole against Expert 1, and his objection is methodological rather than theoretical. His group ran the placebo-controlled replication that found no transfer on any of 17 measures despite large gains on the trained task, and no dose-response relationship of the kind the founding study reported. The general form of the argument matters more than the specific study: the largest far-transfer effects come from small samples with passive controls, and the better the design, the smaller the effect. That is a claim about what a positive result is made of, and it applies to any program that will measure its own success.
  - **Where:** [Georgia Tech faculty page](https://psychology.gatech.edu/people/randall-w-engle), [Attention & Working Memory Lab](https://englelab.gatech.edu/engle), [No evidence of intelligence improvement after working memory training (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029082)

- **Expert 3 — Stuart J. Ritchie**
  - **Who:** Scottish psychologist; lecturer at the Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology and Neuroscience at King's College London since 2018, and author of *Science Fictions* (2020), a book about fraud, bias, negligence, and hype in research; he now works in research communications at Anthropic.
  - **Focus:** Human intelligence, the causes of individual differences in it, and the credibility of the evidence base underlying published psychological claims.
  - **Why Follow:** He supplies the one intervention that has been shown, across three independent quasi-experimental designs and more than 600,000 participants, to raise measured intelligence and keep it raised: schooling, at roughly 1 to 5 IQ points per additional year. That is the existence proof the training-skeptic literature does not dispute, and it reframes the question from whether measured ability can be moved to what dose and what kind of experience moves it. He is also unusually well positioned to be read against his own result, since his other body of work is about why published effects are smaller than they appear.
  - **Where:** [How much does education improve intelligence? (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618774253), [King's College London profile](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/people/stuart-ritchie), [Science Fictions](https://www.sciencefictions.org/about)

- **Expert 4 — Douglas K. Detterman**
  - **Who:** Professor Emeritus at Case Western Reserve University; founder of the journal *Intelligence* in 1977 and its editor-in-chief until 2016; founder of the International Society for Intelligence Research and its president until 2011.
  - **Focus:** The structure of intelligence, and the empirical status of transfer as an explanatory mechanism for it.
  - **Why Follow:** He is the restraint pole against Expert 3 and the most uncompromising skeptic of the premise on which any general-reasoning program rests. His position is that most studies fail to find transfer at all, that the ones claiming it produce it with hints and cues that tell the subject to transfer, and that transfer is rare and governed by similarity between situations. He is worth reading precisely because he states the strong version: that educational philosophies built on transfer are "totally lacking in empirical support." If he is right, a program that teaches reasoning strategies is teaching reasoning strategies, not reasoning.
  - **Where:** [The case for the prosecution: transfer as an epiphenomenon (1993)](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/1993-detterman.pdf), [Curriculum vitae](https://isironline.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Detterman-Vita-2019-1-1-2019.pdf), [Biographical entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Detterman)

- **Expert 5 — Hamsa Bastani**
  - **Who:** Associate Professor of Operations, Information, and Decisions, and of Statistics and Data Science, at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania, where she co-directs the Wharton Healthcare Analytics Lab; her work spans machine learning, operations research, and economics, with algorithms deployed at national scale for COVID-19 border screening and essential-medicine access.
  - **Focus:** Designing, deploying, and evaluating AI systems in consequential environments, including one of the first large field studies of generative AI tutors in high school mathematics.
  - **Why Follow:** She ran the experiment that separates the two things this whole document is about. With nearly a thousand students, an unguarded GPT-4 tutor raised practice performance 48% and then left students 17% *below* those who never had access once it was withdrawn. The guardrailed version eliminated the harm and produced no benefit. That is the cleanest existing demonstration that a visible improvement curve inside a learning tool can be uninformative about, and inversely related to, what the child retains — and it comes from someone whose research program is about deploying algorithms rather than debunking them.
  - **Where:** [Personal site](https://hamsabastani.github.io/), [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning (PNAS, 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), [Wharton faculty page](https://executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu/faculty/hamsa-bastani/)

- **Expert 6 — Greg Kestin**
  - **Who:** Physicist and Preceptor in the Department of Physics at Harvard University, with a Harvard PhD in physics; his work spans physics education research and the design of pedagogical AI tools.
  - **Focus:** Whether an AI tutor built deliberately around established instructional principles can outperform the best available classroom practice rather than merely replacing lecture.
  - **Why Follow:** He is the pole against Expert 5 and the disagreement is empirical rather than rhetorical. His randomized crossover trial found an AI tutor beating an active-learning class by 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations, in less time, with students reporting higher engagement. Read against the harm result, the pair defines the real question: his outcome was measured immediately after the session and his tutor was heavily scaffolded, so the two studies may be measuring different things rather than contradicting each other — and working out which is the interpretive problem any AI learning product inherits.
  - **Where:** [AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning (Scientific Reports, 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6), [Harvard Physics department](https://www.physics.harvard.edu/people/facpages/kestin)

- **Expert 7 — Angela Duckworth**
  - **Who:** Professor of Psychology at the University of Pennsylvania, MacArthur Fellow, and founder of Character Lab; author of the 2007 paper that introduced grit as a psychological construct.
  - **Focus:** Non-cognitive predictors of achievement — grit, self-control, and the measurement problems that attach to all of them.
  - **Why Follow:** She is the source of the idea that an effort disposition predicts success beyond talent, and she is also the most useful critic of the use this document is concerned with. Within a decade of publishing the Grit Scale she argued publicly against high-stakes character assessment, stated the scale is not appropriate for selection or for comparing institutions, and noted that it can be faked with little effort. Her methodological work is sharper still: self-report and behavioral measures of the same construct converge at around r = .10 to .15, and students placed in more demanding schools rate themselves *lower* on grit even as their attendance and scores improve. She is the expert who supplies both the construct and the reason not to measure it by asking.
  - **Where:** [Grit: perseverance and passion for long-term goals (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.6.1087), [Measurement matters (2015)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X15584327), [Personal site](https://angeladuckworth.com/)

- **Expert 8 — Marcus Credé**
  - **Who:** Associate Professor of Psychology at Iowa State University, working on meta-analytic evaluation of popular constructs in educational and organizational psychology.
  - **Focus:** Whether widely adopted non-cognitive constructs survive quantitative scrutiny, and specifically whether they add anything over the established traits they resemble.
  - **Why Follow:** He is the direct rebuttal pole against Expert 7, and it is not a hedge. His meta-analysis of 584 effect sizes from 66,807 people found grit correlating ρ = .84 with conscientiousness, the proposed higher-order structure unconfirmed, and overall grit adding incremental R = .000 over conscientiousness in predicting academic performance. His conclusion is that the construct may be a jangle fallacy and that its only defensible remainder is the perseverance facet. For a program that intends to identify a disposition from behavior, his result sets the bar: whatever is measured has to beat conscientiousness, which is cheap to measure and already well understood.
  - **Where:** [Much ado about grit (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000102), [What shall we do about grit? A critical review](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f4a39379-1728-482a-8e3f-9bd7cecba9d8/content), [Iowa State faculty page](https://www.psychology.iastate.edu/directory/marcus-crede/)

- **Expert 9 — Ryan S. Baker**
  - **Who:** Professor of Artificial Intelligence and Education at Adelaide University and Adjunct Professor in the Graduate School of Education at the University of Pennsylvania, where he directs the Penn Center for Learning Analytics; a founder of the educational data mining research community and creator of the first automated detector of student disengagement.
  - **Focus:** Mining the interaction logs of educational software to build real-time detectors of gaming the system, off-task behavior, carelessness, help misuse, self-regulated learning, and affect — using no sensor, video, or audio data.
  - **Why Follow:** He is the constructive pole on the question of what a learning system can actually know about a child, and the answer is more than intuition suggests: gaming, off-task behavior, and carelessness are detectable from clickstream data alone at usable accuracy, and gaming specifically predicts lower learning even after controlling for prior knowledge. He is also his own best critic. His work documents that affect detectors trained on one US subpopulation fall to near-chance on another, that responding aggressively to a detected behavior can convert it into a worse one, and — in his review of algorithmic bias in education — that these models fail unevenly across race, language, urbanicity, and disability.
  - **Where:** [Penn Center for Learning Analytics](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/index.html), [Developing a generalizable detector of when students game the system (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11257-007-9045-6), [Algorithmic bias in education (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-021-00285-9)

- **Expert 10 — Robert A. Bjork**
  - **Who:** Distinguished Research Professor in the Department of Psychology at UCLA, where he co-directs the Learning and Forgetting Lab with Elizabeth Ligon Bjork; Fellow of the National Academy of Sciences and of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences; past editor of *Psychological Review* and *Memory & Cognition*, and past president of the Association for Psychological Science and the Psychonomic Society.
  - **Focus:** Human learning, memory, and metacognition; specifically the relationship between forgetting and learning, and the gap between how people learn and how they think they learn.
  - **Why Follow:** He is the pole against Expert 9 on what a behavioral record means. Baker's detectors read the learner's current state; Bjork's central finding is that the current state is the wrong thing to read. His storage-strength/retrieval-strength distinction holds that the conditions which most rapidly improve observable performance are systematically different from the conditions that build durable learning, and that both learners and instructors are fooled by the difference. He coined "desirable difficulties" for the manipulations — spacing, interleaving, testing, varied practice — that slow apparent progress and improve retention. Any system that optimizes a real-time engagement or accuracy signal is optimizing exactly the quantity he identifies as misleading.
  - **Where:** [UCLA faculty page](https://www.psych.ucla.edu/faculty-page/bjork/), [Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/), [Learning versus performance: an integrative review (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

## DOK 3: Insights

> **Owner-authored. Not yet written.**
>
> A DOK 3 insight is a connection across sources that no single source states — the thing that is true
> once two findings are held next to each other and that neither author had reason to say. The litmus
> test is whether the claim appears explicitly in any one source; if it does, it belongs in the Knowledge
> Tree below rather than here.
>
> The raw material is in `candidate-tensions.md`, organised by category and numbering roughly eighty
> recorded contradictions. Nothing there is a conclusion. Each line names two sources and states what
> each found, and the work of turning a contradiction into an insight — deciding which side is right,
> or that both are right under different conditions, or that the disagreement reveals a third thing
> neither side is measuring — is the work this level exists to do.
>
> Provocation questions to start the DOK 3 pass:
>
> - **On the two literatures that never meet.** Education raises measured intelligence; cognitive
>   training does not. Both are well evidenced. What differs between them — duration, breadth, social
>   context, the fact that school teaches content while training teaches a task? Which of those
>   differences can a software program actually have, and which is it structurally barred from having?
> - **On measurement collapsing into intervention.** Category 2 says retest alone moves reasoning scores
>   by about a third of a standard deviation, more in younger children, and that the gain is
>   memory-loaded rather than g-loaded. Category 4 says performance under support is not learning.
>   Category 7 says every measured tutoring effect shrinks by a factor of six when the outcome test is
>   standardized rather than locally written. What do those three facts imply *together* about what an
>   improvement number produced inside a learning product can possibly mean?
> - **On effort as a signal.** Wheel-spinning is common, futile, and detectable early. Careless errors
>   cluster among the *most* engaged students. Gaming the system is rare but is the only off-task
>   behavior that predicts learning loss. Help avoidance predicts nothing while help abuse predicts a
>   lot. What single account explains why almost every intuitive reading of an effort signal turns out
>   to be backwards?
> - **On what the deflated constructs have in common.** Grit dissolves into conscientiousness, growth
>   mindset survives only in low-achieving and low-SES subgroups, deliberate practice explains 4–5% of
>   variance in education. The one construct that held its magnitude is defined as a process rather than
>   a possession. Is that a coincidence, or is it telling you which kind of thing is measurable at all?
> - **On the pair that may not disagree.** One AI tutor trial found a 17% loss and another found a
>   1.3-standard-deviation gain. The losing tutor was unguarded and measured after withdrawal; the
>   winning one was scaffolded and measured immediately. If the two results are compatible, what is the
>   single variable that reconciles them, and what does reconciling them cost you in claims?
> - **On the absence.** No adaptive-instruction meta-analysis assembled here uses a reasoning-transfer
>   outcome. No study located measures retention months after a language-model tutor is withdrawn. No
>   study establishes that generated reasoning items calibrate for children. What is the strongest claim
>   that can be made in the presence of three absences that specific?
> - **On who this identifies.** Every effect in Categories 6 and 7 that varies by starting ability
>   varies in the same direction: tutoring systems helped general-population students more than low
>   achievers, AI tutoring helped higher-performing students most, and mindset interventions helped only
>   the low achievers. What does a tool built to find rapid improvers do about the possibility that
>   improvement is easiest to produce in the children who needed it least?

### Category 1: Whether reasoning ability moves at all, and what has been shown to move it

*General idea: The question of whether a learning program can move "fixed" reasoning ability has been fought out twice over, in two literatures that reach opposite conclusions and rarely cite each other's design constraints. The cognitive-training literature — n-back and working-memory programs, the closest analogue to a software learning tool — produced one famous positive dose-response finding in 2008 and then a decade of failed replications, dueling meta-analyses whose headline effect sizes differ by a factor of five, and a consistent pattern where the better the control group, the smaller the effect. The education literature, using quasi-experimental designs that cannot be run in a lab, finds that an additional year of schooling raises measured intelligence by roughly 1 to 5 IQ points and that the effect persists across the life span. Against that, the fadeout literature shows that IQ gains produced by early interventions decline toward zero after the intervention ends, and that the decline happens because the treated children lose their advantage rather than because controls catch up. Population-level evidence establishes that reasoning scores are environmentally movable at the scale of decades, which bounds the debate: the trait is not fixed, but almost nothing that looks like a short computerized training program has been shown to move it. For a tool that must identify children who improve rapidly, the single most product-relevant finding — that transfer appeared only in the children who improved substantially on the training task — is also the finding most directly contradicted by meta-analytic mediation results.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: The founding n-back → Gf claim and its placebo-controlled non-replication**
  - **Source:** Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Perrig (2008); Redick, Shipstead, Harrison, Hicks, Fried, Hambrick, Kane & Engle (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Four experiments trained a total of 70 healthy young participants (mean age 25.6 years) on an adaptive dual n-back task, leaving 34 trained and 35 untrained controls after exclusion. *(Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Perrig, 2008, *PNAS* 105(19):6829–6833, Materials and Methods, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0801268105.)* `T2` `[healthy young adults, not children]`
      - The four training settings differed only in number of sessions between pre- and posttest: 8 days (n = 16), 12 days (n = 22), 17 days (n = 16), and 19 days (n = 15). *(Jaeggi et al., 2008, Materials and Methods.)* `T2`
      - Gf gain in the control groups was significant and attributed by the authors to retest effects (t(34) = 2.08, P < 0.05, Cohen's d = 0.25); gain in the trained groups was larger (t(33) = 5.53, P < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.65), with a significant group × test-session interaction (F(1,67) = 5.27, P < 0.05). *(Jaeggi et al., 2008, Results.)* `T2`
      - Transfer to Gf varied as a function of training time (F(3,30) = 9.25, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.48), with significance reached at 17 days and 19 days but not at 8 days. *(Jaeggi et al., 2008, Results.)* `T2`
      - Participants with initially lower Gf showed larger Gf gains, and gain in Gf did not depend on initial working-memory capacity measured by digit span or reading span. *(Jaeggi et al., 2008, Results.)* `T2`
      - The authors state: "Our study also does not permit us to know how long the training gain persists; longitudinal studies will be required to address that issue." *(Jaeggi et al., 2008, Discussion.)* `T2`
      - The individual studies of Jaeggi et al. (2008) "are also based on very small samples (e.g., n = 7, 8, or 11 in each group of the four studies)." *(Redick, Shipstead, Harrison, Hicks, Fried, Hambrick, Kane & Engle, 2013, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 142(2):359–379, Introduction, DOI 10.1037/a0029082.)* `T2`
      - Redick et al. compared 20 sessions of adaptive dual n-back training against an adaptive visual-search active placebo control and a no-contact control, leaving 73 participants aged 18–30 after exclusions. *(Redick et al., 2013, Method.)* `T2` `[young adults 18–30, not children]`
      - Both training groups improved substantially on their trained tasks; mean n rose from 2.3 in Session 1 to 4.1 in Session 20 for the n-back group. *(Redick et al., 2013, Results.)* `T2`
      - "Out of 17 ANOVAs, there were no significant Group × Session interactions." Reanalyses excluding the visual-search group, and excluding the midtest session, also found no transfer relative to either control group. *(Redick et al., 2013, Results.)* `T2`

      - "The models suggest that in impoverished families, 60% of the variance in IQ is accounted for by the shared environment, and the contribution of genes is close to zero; in affluent families, the result is almost exactly the reverse." *(Turkheimer et al., 2003, Abstract.)* `T2`
      - In a median-split analysis, the low-SES group's correlations were consistent with h² of .10 and c² of .58, while the high-SES group's were consistent with h² of .72 and c² of .15. *(Turkheimer et al., 2003, Results.)* `T2`
      - "Results demonstrate that the proportions of IQ variance attributable to genes and environment vary nonlinearly with SES." *(Turkheimer et al., 2003, Abstract.)* `T2`
      - "Intelligence can have an extremely high heritability, but also be malleable; a paradox that has been the source of continuous controversy." *(Sauce & Matzel, 2018, *Psychological Bulletin* 144(1):26–47, Abstract, DOI 10.1037/bul0000131.)* `T2` `[theoretical review synthesizing existing evidence, not new data]`
      - The authors argue that gene-environment interplay "is difficult to specify with current methods, and is underestimated in standard metrics of heritability (thus inflating estimates of 'genetic' effects)." *(Sauce & Matzel, 2018, Abstract.)* `T2`
      - The evidence they assemble for malleability existing "on top of heritability" covers "cognitive gains consequent to adoption/immigration, changes in IQ's heritability across life span and socioeconomic status, gains in IQ over time consequent to societal development (the Flynn effect), the slowdown of age-related cognitive decline, and the gains in intelligence from early education." *(Sauce & Matzel, 2018, Abstract.)* `T2`
      - They identify "the loss of initial benefits from early intervention programs (such as 'Head Start')" as one of the enduring problems their gene-environment account is meant to address. *(Sauce & Matzel, 2018, Abstract.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Heritability is a population-specific variance ratio, not a ceiling on change, and Turkheimer and colleagues demonstrated this empirically: in 319 pairs of 7-year-old twins, the heritability of full-scale IQ was approximately .10 in the low-SES half of the sample and .72 in the high-SES half, with shared environment accounting for about 60% of variance in the most impoverished families and close to zero in the most affluent. The heritability estimate therefore depends on which environments the sample occupies. Sauce and Matzel synthesize this with adoption and immigration gains, life-span and SES changes in heritability, the Flynn effect, and education effects to argue that standard heritability metrics understate gene-environment interplay and thereby inflate apparent genetic determination. They explicitly locate early-intervention fadeout as a phenomenon their account must explain rather than as evidence against malleability. The practical upshot is that "reasoning is highly heritable" is not a valid argument that a program cannot move it — and neither source demonstrates that any particular program does.
    - **Link to source:** [Socioeconomic status modifies heritability of IQ in young children](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.0956-7976.2003.psci_1475.x), [The paradox of intelligence: heritability and malleability coexist in hidden gene-environment interplay](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000131)

### Category 2: Training reasoning directly — the inductive-reasoning programs, and the transfer problem

*General idea: If the reasoning a cognitive-abilities test measures were truly fixed, no instructional program should be able to move it. Two program families claim otherwise: Klauer's inductive-reasoning training, which reports roughly half a standard deviation on fluid-intelligence tests across 74 child experiments, and Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment, whose meta-analytic effects are modest and concentrated on figural measures resembling its own materials. Against those claims sits a transfer literature that says the opposite — Detterman's assertion that transfer is rare, Barnett and Ceci's demonstration that "transfer" collapses nine distinct dimensions into one word, and second-order meta-analyses in which far-transfer effects vanish once placebo and publication bias are controlled. Two further literatures bound how much of any observed gain is real: SAT coaching studies show score movement of roughly 10 to 30 points that scales logarithmically with contact hours, and retest meta-analyses show about a third of a standard deviation appears on a second administration with no instruction at all. Together these set the measurement problem for any software tool that wants to identify children who genuinely improve rather than children who have simply seen the item format before.*

- **Subcategory 2.1: Klauer's taxonomy — what "inductive reasoning" is decomposed into before it is trained**
  - **Source:** Klauer & Phye (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Klauer's theory specifies six classes of inductive reasoning problems: generalization, discrimination, cross-classification, recognizing relationships, differentiating relationships, and system construction. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, *Review of Educational Research* 78(1):85–123, Table 1, DOI 10.3102/0034654307313402.)* `T2`
      - The six classes map onto item formats found on intelligence tests: generalization to class formation, class expansion, and finding common attributes; discrimination to identifying disturbing attributes; cross-classification to 4-fold, 6-fold, and 9-fold schemes; recognizing relationships to series completion, ordered series, and analogy; differentiating relationships to disturbed series; and system construction to matrices. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Table 1.)* `T2`
      - The six classes are generated by crossing two facets: whether the learner scrutinizes attributes of objects or relations among them, and whether the learner looks for similarities, differences, or both. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Figure 2.)* `T2`
      - Three training manuals exist: Program I for children age 5 through 8, Program II for children age 11 through 13, and Program III for youths age 14 through 16. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, p. 91.)* `T2`
      - The standard training episode is 10 lessons at two lessons per week over 5 weeks, with testing one week before and one week after training, for a total study duration of 7 weeks. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, p. 97.)* `T2`
      - The theory prescribes two solution strategies: an analytic strategy that checks objects predicate by predicate for commonalities and diversities, and a heuristic strategy in which the learner inspects globally, builds a hypothesis, and tests it by directed comparisons before falling back on the analytic strategy. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Figure 3.)* `T2`
      - The authors state the theory is prescriptive rather than descriptive: it specifies how to proceed to solve inductive problems efficiently, not what learners commonly do. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, pp. 90–91.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Klauer does not treat inductive reasoning as a single undifferentiated ability. He decomposes it into six problem classes generated by crossing two facets — attributes versus relations, and similarity versus difference versus both — and each class corresponds to recognizable intelligence-test item formats such as analogies, series, and matrices. The trained content is a comparison procedure, taught either as an exhaustive analytic scan or as a faster hypothesis-test heuristic. Three age-graded manuals cover ages 5–8, 11–13, and 14–16, and the standard dose is ten lessons across five weeks — a small enough intervention to be practical. The authors are explicit that the theory prescribes how to solve inductive problems rather than describing how learners actually do, which makes the trained object a strategy rather than a capacity.
    - **Link to source:** [Inductive reasoning: a training approach](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313402)

- **Subcategory 2.2: What Klauer's training transferred to, how far, and how long it lasted**
  - **Source:** Klauer & Phye (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The meta-analysis pooled 74 training experiments with nearly 3,600 children. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, *Review of Educational Research* 78(1):85–123, p. 85, DOI 10.3102/0034654307313402.)* `T2`
      - On intelligence test performance, 79 contrasts from 73 experiments (N = 3,595) yielded a weighted mean effect of d+ = 0.52, 95% CI [0.46, 0.59]; the unweighted mean was Mg = 0.59 ± 0.31 with individual effect sizes ranging from −0.05 to 1.30. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Table 4 and p. 102.)* `T2`
      - The authors characterize d+ = 0.52 as improving intelligence test performance by about half a standard deviation, corresponding to about 20 percentile ranks for the average participant. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, p. 103.)* `T2`
      - On academic learning of newly taught classroom subject matter, 38 contrasts (N = 1,723) yielded d+ = 0.69, 95% CI [0.59, 0.79], which the authors report as significantly larger than the 0.52 intelligence-test effect (p < .05) and as "in contrast to expectations." *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Table 5 and p. 105.)* `T2`
      - Durability: 22 experiments involving 1,094 students administered a follow-up posttest between 3 and 15 months after the first posttest; the partial correlation between follow-up score and elapsed months, with pretest partialed out, was r₁₂.₃ = .44 (df = 19), p = .045, which the authors interpret as training effects not diminishing over time and even increasing slightly. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, pp. 104–105.)* `T2`
      - Placebo test: nine three-group experiments comparing an alternatively trained group to a no-training control yielded d+ = 0.004 (n = 9, N = 230), not significantly different from zero. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, p. 104.)* `T2`
      - Program subgroup effects on intelligence tests were Program I d+ = 0.57 (n = 42), Program II d+ = 0.43 (n = 24), Program III d+ = 0.50 (n = 13); by setting, kindergarten 0.47, school kindergarten 0.43, primary school 0.61, secondary school 0.42, special education 0.54. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Table 4.)* `T2`
      - Training-condition effects were one-to-one d+ = 0.34 (n = 11), pairs 0.59 (n = 15), small groups 0.57 (n = 36), whole classes 0.51 (n = 17). *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Table 4.)* `T2`
      - Studies authored by Klauer's own staff yielded d+ = 0.57 (n = 35); studies by other authors yielded d+ = 0.49 (n = 44). *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Table 4.)* `T2`
      - The academic-learning effect was largest in special education samples, d+ = 0.94, 95% CI [0.74, 1.14] (n = 13, N = 434), and for Program III, d+ = 0.84 (n = 11). *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, Table 5.)* `T2`
      - The intelligence tests used consisted entirely of inductive reasoning items (Raven Coloured, Standard, and Advanced Progressive Matrices) or included inductive reasoning subtests (Cattell Culture Fair Tests, German version of the Cognitive Ability Test, German version of the Columbia Mental Maturity Test); the authors state the data refer to tests that "in part, or in their entirety, measure inductive reasoning." *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, pp. 97–98.)* `T2`
      - The authors tested the coaching hypothesis indirectly, via two criteria borrowed from Hasselhorn (1995) — durability of effects and transfer to other variables — and concluded both criteria were fulfilled, so results due solely to test-coaching procedures can be rejected. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, pp. 96 and 105–106.)* `T2`
      - Where pretest, posttest, and follow-up tests were identical, the authors note this "leads to considerable retest effects," and state that specific training effects are unconfounded with retest effects only where a no-training or alternatively trained control group was available. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, pp. 97–98.)* `T2`
      - Only a few of the 71 published articles in the data pool were published in English-language journals; the search terms included "Denktraining," the German label of the programs, and three of the 74 experiments were unpublished theses. *(Klauer & Phye, 2008, pp. 96–97.)* `T2` `[predominantly German-language and European school samples; the meta-analysis of a training program was conducted by the program's own author]`
      - A subsequent extension covering 109 studies and N = 4,668 reports effects of d = 0.60 on fluid intelligence, d = 0.68 on learning in classroom lessons, and d = 0.65 on further cognitive variables. *(Klauer, 2014, *Zeitschrift für Pädagogische Psychologie* 28(1–2):5–19, DOI 10.1024/1010-0652/a000123.)* `T2` `[German-language journal; extension authored by the program's originator]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Across 74 experiments and roughly 3,600 children, Klauer's inductive-reasoning training moved fluid-intelligence test scores by about half a standard deviation, which the authors translate to roughly 20 percentile ranks, and a later extension to 109 studies raises that to 0.60. Transfer to learning newly taught school subject matter was larger still at 0.69, a result the authors themselves flag as contrary to their transfer-distance expectation. Effects did not decay across follow-ups spanning 3 to 15 months and correlated positively with elapsed time. Alternative non-inductive training produced essentially nothing at 0.004, which rules out a pure attention-and-social-contact explanation and is the strongest single point in the program's favor. Three provenance limits are stated in the paper itself: the intelligence measures were wholly or partly composed of inductive reasoning items — the same six classes the program trains — the literature is overwhelmingly German-language, and Klauer's own staff produced somewhat larger effects than independent authors. The meta-analysis of the training program was conducted by the person who designed it.
    - **Link to source:** [Inductive reasoning: a training approach](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313402), [Extension of the 2008 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123)

- **Subcategory 2.3: An independent evaluation of the same program, at a fraction of the meta-analytic effect**
  - **Source:** Baumeister & Rindermann (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Two field studies compared the effects of Klauer's Program I with special-needs students, one using the paper-and-pencil version and one using the PC version. *(Baumeister & Rindermann, 2014, in Polman et al., Eds., *Learning and Becoming in Practice: Proceedings of the 11th International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS 2014)*, Vol. 1, pp. 38–45, DOI 10.22318/icls2014.38.)* `T2` `[refereed conference proceedings; small special-needs samples in Austria and Germany]`
      - The paper-and-pencil training version (N₁ = 34 students; mean age 8 years 9 months) resulted in no improvement of the students' cognitive abilities (CFT 1: d_mean = −0.17; β_mean Train→Int = −.08) or verbal abilities (HSET: d_mean = −0.02, β_mean Train→Verb = .04). *(Baumeister & Rindermann, 2014, p. 38.)* `T2`
      - The PC version (N₂ = 27; mean age 7 years 8 months) resulted in a small positive effect on intelligence (CFT 1: d_mean = 0.28; β_mean Train→Int = .13) and on verbal performance (HSET: d_mean = 0.25, β_mean Train→Verb = .12). *(Baumeister & Rindermann, 2014, p. 38.)* `T2`
      - The authors attribute the slight and relatively stable increases in cognitive and verbal ability observed in the paper-and-pencil study to naturally occurring developmental effects and to a retest or practice effect from getting used to working on the performance tests. *(Baumeister & Rindermann, 2014.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that, compared to childhood cognitive development, even the small training effects observed in the PC study are practically meaningful because they correspond to more than half a year of schooling and maturation. *(Baumeister & Rindermann, 2014, p. 38.)* `T2`
      - The authors cite the Klauer & Phye figures as d = 0.52 for intelligence gains across 78 evaluation studies and d = 0.69 across 38 studies for academic learning, and position their own studies as testing the program with a heterogeneous special-needs group rather than the homogeneous samples used previously. *(Baumeister & Rindermann, 2014.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the closest thing located to an independent field test of the specific program behind the headline reasoning-training result, conducted by authors who are not part of the program's originating group. Its findings are substantially weaker than the meta-analytic figure they themselves cite: the paper-and-pencil version produced a slightly negative effect on fluid intelligence at −0.17 and essentially nothing verbally, while the computer-delivered version produced 0.28 on intelligence and 0.25 on verbal performance — roughly half the 0.52 meta-analytic estimate at best. The authors' own interpretation of the first study is the important one for measurement purposes: they attribute what movement they saw to maturation and to retest familiarity rather than to the training. They also argue the smaller effects still matter, on the grounds that they equate to more than half a year of schooling and maturation, which is a defensible benchmark but a different claim from moving a fixed ability. The samples are small and specifically special-needs, so this does not overturn the meta-analysis; it does show what the program produced when a separate group ran it.
    - **Link to source:** [Klauer's inductive reasoning training as a cognitive apprenticeship approach for special-needs students](https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2014.38)

- **Subcategory 2.4: Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment — modest effects, concentrated on measures resembling the training materials**
  - **Source:** Romney & Samuels (2001); Savell, Twohig & Rachford (1986)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The meta-analysis examined 40 controlled studies of Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment comprising 47 different samples. *(Romney & Samuels, 2001, *Educational and Child Psychology* 18(4):19–34, DOI 10.53841/bpsecp.2001.18.4.19.)* `T2`
      - Significant but "modest" average effect sizes were found in all three claimed areas — achievement, ability, and behaviour — with the most extensive improvement in ability. *(Romney & Samuels, 2001.)* `T2`
      - Gains in spatial/perceptual ability were related to the length of the intervention measured in hours. *(Romney & Samuels, 2001.)* `T2`
      - Self-esteem was related to age, with older children showing increases and younger children showing decreases. *(Romney & Samuels, 2001.)* `T2`
      - The authors' stated motivation for the meta-analysis was that "some outcome studies have produced negative results." *(Romney & Samuels, 2001.)* `T2`
      - A separate review examined some 35 reports of empirical research on Instrumental Enrichment. *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986, *Review of Educational Research* 56(4):381–409, DOI 10.3102/00346543056004381.)* `T2`
      - The reviewers state that the effects observed have "for the most part" been on standard nonverbal measures of intelligence — PMA, Lorge-Thorndike, Cattell, Raven's — which they describe as largely measures of skill in processing figural and spatial information. *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986, Findings §2.)* `T2`
      - For measures of self-concept, classroom behavior, impulsivity, academic achievement, and course content, the reviewers report effects that have been "either absent, inconsistent, or difficult to interpret," with few demonstrations of effects in real-life, including academic, or simulated real-life situations. *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986, Findings §2.)* `T2`
      - Reviewing the original Israeli two-year field experiment, the authors observe that experimental/control differences were larger and more clear-cut on the measures most similar in content to the training materials used, and ask "whether the study produced anything more than near-transfer or 'practice effects'." *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986.)* `T2`
      - The reviewers report that studies tending to show statistically reliable treatment effects usually provided a greater dosage than studies tending not to, suggesting generally 80 hours or more of student exposure over a 1- or 2-year period, plus at least a week of instructor training with follow-up support. *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986, Findings §1.)* `T2`
      - The reviewers state there had been no research on dosage and that it is not clear what dosage is required to produce what kinds of effects, in what magnitude, in what kinds of subjects. *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986, Findings §1.)* `T2`
      - Effects were observed almost entirely in individuals who were in primary or secondary school and in the 12- to 18-year age range at the time of exposure. *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986, Findings §3.)* `T2` `[ages 12–18, not younger children]`
      - In the original Israeli experiment, experimental classes received approximately 45 minutes of the program 3–5 days a week for two years, an estimated total of 200–300 hours, as an adjunct to the usual curriculum. *(Savell, Twohig & Rachford, 1986.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The second major reasoning-training tradition produces a weaker and more equivocal record than Klauer's. Romney and Samuels pooled 40 controlled studies and 47 samples and found statistically significant but modest effects across achievement, ability, and classroom behaviour, with ability moving most — while acknowledging that the impetus for the meta-analysis was a primary literature containing negative results. Savell, Twohig and Rachford, reviewing roughly 35 studies, locate the reliable effects specifically on nonverbal figural and spatial intelligence measures and report that effects on achievement, self-concept, behavior and real-life situations were absent, inconsistent, or uninterpretable. Their most damaging observation concerns Feuerstein's own Israeli field experiment: the largest group differences appeared on precisely the measures most content-similar to the training instruments, prompting them to ask directly whether anything beyond near transfer or practice effects was shown. Dosage mattered — roughly 80 hours or more — but no study had manipulated it systematically, and effects clustered in the 12-to-18 age range rather than in younger children.
    - **Link to source:** [A meta-analytic evaluation of Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment program](https://doi.org/10.53841/bpsecp.2001.18.4.19), [Empirical status of Feuerstein's Instrumental Enrichment technique](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543056004381)

- **Subcategory 2.5: Teaching for broader intelligence raised achievement, but was not measured against an intelligence test**
  - **Source:** Sternberg, Torff & Grigorenko (1998)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Two studies tested triarchic instruction with 213 third-grade students taught a social-studies unit and 141 eighth-grade students taught a psychology unit. *(Sternberg, Torff & Grigorenko, 1998, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 90(3):374–384, DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.374.)* `T2`
      - Students were taught in one of three ways: traditional instruction (primarily memory-based), critical-thinking instruction (primarily analytically based), and triarchically based instruction infusing analytical, creative, and practical instruction. *(Sternberg, Torff & Grigorenko, 1998.)* `T2`
      - Performance was assessed with multiple-choice items measuring primarily memory and performance-based items measuring analytical, creative, and practical aspects of achievement; in general triarchic instruction was superior to the other modes, even on the multiple-choice memory-based items. *(Sternberg, Torff & Grigorenko, 1998.)* `T2`
      - The reported outcome variables are school achievement measures within the taught units, not scores on a standardized fluid-intelligence or cognitive-abilities test. *(Sternberg, Torff & Grigorenko, 1998.)* `T2` `[achievement outcomes only; no intelligence-test outcome reported in this study]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Sternberg, Torff and Grigorenko randomized 213 third graders and 141 eighth graders across three instructional modes and found that instruction infusing analytical, creative, and practical work outperformed both conventional memory-based teaching and critical-thinking teaching. The advantage held even on the memory-based multiple-choice items, which is the comparison least favorable to their own hypothesis and therefore the most persuasive part of the result. What the study does not do is measure a standardized intelligence outcome: the dependent variables are achievement within the specific taught units. - **Subcategory 2.6: "Transfer" names nine dimensions, not one, and has two distinct mechanisms**
  - **Source:** Barnett & Ceci (2002); Salomon & Perkins (1989)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors argue that a century of research on far transfer has made little progress toward resolution because of a failure to specify the various dimensions along which transfer can occur, resulting in comparisons of "apples and oranges." *(Barnett & Ceci, 2002, *Psychological Bulletin* 128(4):612–637, p. 612, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612.)* `T2`
      - The framework describes 9 relevant dimensions grouped into two global factors: content, meaning what is transferred, and context, meaning when and where it is transferred from and to. *(Barnett & Ceci, 2002, Figure 1, p. 621.)* `T2`
      - The three content dimensions are specificity of the learned skill, nature of the performance change (speed, accuracy, or approach), and memory demands of the transfer task. *(Barnett & Ceci, 2002, Figure 1, p. 621.)* `T2`
      - The six context dimensions, each a near-to-far continuum, are knowledge domain, physical context, temporal context, functional context, social context, and modality. *(Barnett & Ceci, 2002, Figure 1, p. 621.)* `T2`
      - The published near-to-far anchors include, for knowledge domain, mouse vs. rat (near) through science vs. cooking (far); for temporal context, same session through years later; and for functional context, both clearly academic through academic vs. at play. *(Barnett & Ceci, 2002, Figure 1, p. 621.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that estimation of a single effect size for far transfer is misguided in view of this complexity. *(Barnett & Ceci, 2002, p. 612.)* `T2`
      - The authors conclude that the past 100 years of research shows evidence for transfer under some conditions is substantial, but critical conditions for many key questions are untested. *(Barnett & Ceci, 2002, p. 612.)* `T2`
      - Low-road transfer depends on extensive, varied practice and occurs by the automatic triggering of well-learned behavior in a new context. *(Salomon & Perkins, 1989, *Educational Psychologist* 24(2):113–142, DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep2402_1.)* `T2`
      - High-road transfer occurs by intentional mindful abstraction of something from one context and application in a new context, subdivided into forward-reaching transfer, where one abstracts in anticipation of later application, and backward-reaching transfer, where one faces a new situation and deliberately searches for relevant prior knowledge. *(Salomon & Perkins, 1989.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that findings of transfer or nontransfer reflect whether the conditions for either low-road or high-road transfer were met. *(Salomon & Perkins, 1989.)* `T2`
      - In the associated instructional formulation, "hugging" means teaching so the learning experience approximates the target performance, and "bridging" means mediating abstraction, connection-searching, mindfulness, and metacognition. *(Perkins & Salomon, "Transfer of learning," *International Encyclopedia of Education*, 2nd ed.)* `[reference-work entry, not a peer-reviewed journal article]`
      - The encyclopedia formulation states that "a superficial look at how research on transfer casts its vote is discouraging" and that "the preponderance of studies suggest that transfer comes hard," but concludes that "education can achieve abundant transfer if it is designed to do so." *(Perkins & Salomon, *International Encyclopedia of Education*, 2nd ed.)* `[reference-work entry, not a peer-reviewed journal article]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Barnett and Ceci argue that the century-old stalemate over far transfer is a specification failure: studies claiming or denying transfer measure different things because they never state which dimensions their training and test differ on. They supply nine — three describing what transfers and six describing how far — each with published near-to-far anchors, so a study can be located at an intersection rather than labeled near or far. Their explicit methodological conclusion is that any single pooled far-transfer effect size is misguided. Salomon and Perkins add the mechanism half: low-road transfer is reflexive and bought by extensive varied practice, while high-road transfer is effortful and requires deliberate abstraction, so a finding of no transfer is evidence about which preconditions were absent rather than about transfer's existence. Their instructional corollaries — making practice resemble the target, and explicitly mediating abstraction — are the two levers a program actually has, and they conclude abundant transfer is achievable if instruction is designed for it.
    - **Link to source:** [When and where do we apply what we learn? A taxonomy for far transfer](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612), [Rocky roads to transfer: rethinking mechanisms of a neglected phenomenon](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep2402_1)

- **Subcategory 2.7: The case for the prosecution — transfer as rare, and far transfer as null after correction**
  - **Source:** Detterman (1993); Sala, Aksaylı, Tatlidil, Tatsumi, Gondo & Gobet (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Detterman defines transfer as "the degree to which a behavior will be repeated in a new situation." *(Detterman, 1993, "The case for the prosecution: transfer as an epiphenomenon," in Detterman & Sternberg, Eds., *Transfer on Trial: Intelligence, Cognition, and Instruction*, Norwood, NJ: Ablex, p. 4.)* `[edited academic book chapter, not a peer-reviewed journal article]`
      - His summary conclusion reads: "First, most studies fail to find transfer. Second, those studies claiming transfer can only be said to have found transfer by the most generous of criteria and would not meet the classical definition of transfer…. Transfer is rare, and its likelihood of occurrence is directly related to the similarity between two situations." *(Detterman, 1993, p. 15.)* `[edited academic book chapter]`
      - He states that in all the studies he is familiar with that claim transfer, transfer is produced by "tricks" — most often just telling the subject to transfer via hints or outright suggestions, or manipulations that call the subject's attention in obvious ways to what the experimenter expects. *(Detterman, 1993.)* `[edited academic book chapter]`
      - He writes that "Cognitive psychologists, and other people who should know better, continue to advocate a philosophy of education that is totally lacking in empirical support." *(Detterman, 1993, p. 16, as quoted in Barnett & Ceci, 2002, *Psychological Bulletin* 128(4):612–637.)* `[edited academic book chapter]`
      - He recounts Thorndike and Woodworth (1901): after 1,000 to 2,000 practice trials estimating rectangle areas, errors on a transfer series of non-rectangular shapes were about 90% as large as errors before training. *(Detterman, 1993.)* `[edited academic book chapter]`
      - He reports that Thorndike (1924) showed learning Latin and geometry were no more useful in improving reasoning than more utilitarian courses like bookkeeping. *(Detterman, 1993.)* `[edited academic book chapter]`
      - He argues that with formally identical isomorphic problems there is no significant transfer in one order and only some transfer in the reverse order when subjects are given hints about problem similarity, noting the subjects were college students. *(Detterman, 1993, citing Simon & Reed, 1976.)* `[edited academic book chapter; college-student samples]`

      - The authors state: "The lack of generalization of skills acquired by training is thus an invariant of human cognition." *(Sala et al., 2019.)* `T2`

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Detterman sets a deliberately strict bar — transfer is the degree to which a behavior is repeated in a new situation — and argues the literature cannot clear it: most studies fail to find transfer, and those claiming it either use generous criteria or manufacture it with hints that tell the subject to transfer. His historical anchors are Thorndike and Woodworth's subjects gaining almost nothing on untrained shapes after thousands of trials, and Thorndike's finding that Latin and geometry improved reasoning no more than bookkeeping. Sala and colleagues supply the modern quantitative version by pooling meta-analyses rather than studies. - **Subcategory 2.8: Coaching effects on aptitude and reasoning tests — how many points instruction actually buys**
  - **Source:** Messick & Jungeblut (1981); DerSimonian & Laird (1983); Powers & Rock (1999)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**

      - Studies comparing gains achieved by coached students to national norms yielded estimated coaching effects four to five times greater than corresponding effects estimated from matched or randomized evaluations. *(DerSimonian & Laird, 1983, *Harvard Educational Review* 53(1):1–15, DOI 10.17763/haer.53.1.n06j5h5356217648.)* `T2`

      - The authors conclude the data support a positive effect of coaching on SAT scores, but that the size of the coaching effect estimated from the matched or randomized studies — 10 points — "seems too small to be practically important." *(DerSimonian & Laird, 1983.)* `T2`
      - A College Board-sponsored survey of a nationally representative sample of 1995–96 SAT takers yielded a database of more than 4,000 examinees, about 500 of whom had attended formal coaching programs outside their schools. *(Powers & Rock, 1999, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 36(2):93–118, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1999.tb00549.x.)* `T2`
      - Discounting two outlier estimates, the authors state that on average coaching affects SAT I verbal scores by about 6–8 points and SAT I math scores by about 13–18 points, or about twice as much as for verbal, and that by commonly used standards these effects can be regarded as small. *(Powers & Rock, 1999; also ETS Research Report RR-98-35, DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.1998.tb01784.x.)* `T2`
      - The authors state all of the estimates suggested the effects of coaching are far less than is claimed by major commercial test preparation companies, and that the revised SAT does not appear to be any more coachable than its predecessor. *(Powers & Rock, 1999.)* `T2`
      - The What Works Clearinghouse records that this study does not meet its group design standards because equivalence of the analytic intervention and comparison groups prior to the intervention was necessary and not demonstrated. *(Institute of Education Sciences, *ACT/SAT Test Preparation and Coaching Programs* WWC intervention report.)* `[federal review document; the underlying study is an observational survey with no random assignment]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The coaching literature bounds how much deliberate instruction moves an aptitude score, and the bound is low. Messick and Jungeblut found the strongest regularity was not a fixed coaching effect but a logarithmic relationship between contact hours and score gain, correlating above .7, with the consequence that pushing average gains past 20 to 30 points on a 200–800 scale requires contact time approaching full-time schooling.     - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Simply taking a cognitive ability test twice raises the score by about a quarter to a third of a standard deviation, and taking it three times raises it by roughly half — moving a median scorer from the 50th to the 71st percentile with no instruction claimed. ### Category 3: The instructional mechanisms with the strongest evidence, and the boundary conditions where each stops working

*General idea: The instructional-mechanism literature is unusually well quantified, which makes it unusually easy to misread — nearly every headline effect size in this category is an average across conditions that includes conditions where the effect is zero or negative. Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, feedback, self-explanation, and mastery learning all have credible meta-analytic support in the 0.4 to 0.55 range, but each carries a documented reversal point: retrieval practice does nothing when initial retrieval fails without feedback; interleaving reverses sign for word learning; worked-example guidance reverses for experts; over 38% of feedback interventions decreased performance; and mastery learning's advantage appears on locally developed tests but not on standardized ones. The transfer literature is the most restrictive of all, since after correcting for publication bias the intercept often indicated no positive transfer at all when the key moderators were absent. Two structural facts about the evidence base matter for any claim about reasoning ability: the largest and cleanest effects come from verbal-recall laboratory paradigms with undergraduate samples, and the classroom effect at elementary-school level is the smallest of any education level measured. Where sources disagree on a number, both are recorded rather than reconciled.*

- **Subcategory 3.1: The canonical demonstration that testing is a retention effect, not an acquisition effect**
  - **Source:** Roediger & Karpicke (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Students studied prose passages and either took one or three immediate free-recall tests without feedback, or restudied the material the same number of times; a final retention test followed at 5 minutes, 2 days, or 1 week. *(Roediger & Karpicke, 2006, *Psychological Science* 17(3):249–255, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x.)* `T2` `[undergraduate laboratory samples; prose passages; free recall without feedback]`
      - In Experiment 1, on the 5-minute retention test restudying produced better recall than testing (81% vs. 75% of idea units), t(39) = 3.22, d = 0.52; the pattern reversed on the 2-day and 1-week tests. *(Roediger & Karpicke, 2006.)* `T2`
      - In Experiment 2, on the 5-minute test the repeated-study group recalled more than the study-test groups (83% vs. 78% vs. 71%); on the 1-week test the order inverted (61% vs. 56% vs. 40%). *(Roediger & Karpicke, 2006.)* `T2`
      - Repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material even though it produced worse delayed retention. *(Roediger & Karpicke, 2006.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the canonical demonstration that the testing effect is a retention effect rather than an acquisition effect. At a 5-minute delay restudying actually beat testing by 6 percentage points with a medium effect size, so a program measuring immediate post-session performance would conclude testing was the worse method. The direction of the difference flipped at 2 days and widened to 21 percentage points at 1 week. The authors also recorded a metacognitive inversion: the condition producing the worst delayed retention produced the highest confidence. Any product that optimizes for within-session gains or for learner-reported confidence will systematically select against the method with better durable retention.
    - **Link to source:** [Test-enhanced learning: taking memory tests improves long-term retention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The meta-analytic estimate, and the identifiable cell where it goes to zero**
  - **Source:** Rowland (2014); Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017); Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Rowland's meta-analysis comprised 159 effect sizes from 61 studies comparing testing against a restudy control; the overall effect was Hedges' g = 0.50. *(Rowland, 2014, *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6):1432–1463, DOI 10.1037/a0037559.)* `T2` `[laboratory studies with restudy control conditions only]`
      - In the high-exposure data set, initial recognition testing yielded the smallest effect (g = 0.36) while cued recall and free recall yielded g = 0.72 and g = 0.82; all multiple-choice tests were coded as recognition tests. *(Rowland, 2014.)* `T2`
      - Among studies not providing feedback, initial test performance of 50% or less did not produce a reliable testing effect: g = 0.03, 95% CI [−0.21, 0.27], p = .79. *(Rowland, 2014.)* `T2`
      - Among studies not providing feedback, initial retrieval success of 51%–75% yielded g = 0.29 and success above 75% yielded g = 0.56; studies with feedback, regardless of retrieval success, yielded g = 0.73, 95% CI [0.61, 0.86]. *(Rowland, 2014.)* `T2`
      - Matching the initial and final test formats did not contribute any increase in the magnitude of the testing effect, a result the author reports as inconsistent with transfer-appropriate processing theory. *(Rowland, 2014.)* `T2`
      - A larger meta-analysis of 272 independent effects from 188 experiments showed a weighted mean effect size of +0.51 for practice testing compared with restudying, and +0.93 compared with filler or no activities. *(Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan, 2017, *Review of Educational Research* 87(3):659–701, DOI 10.3102/0034654316689306.)* `T2`
      - Mixed-format and multiple-choice practice tests produced the largest effect sizes (g = 0.80 and g = 0.70), and multiple-choice tests were associated with a significantly higher effect size than short-answer tests (g = 0.70 vs. g = 0.48). *(Adesope et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - A classroom-restricted review integrated 48,478 students' data from 222 independent studies; the weighted mean effect size was g = 0.499, 95% CI [0.442, 0.557], p < .001. *(Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks, 2021, *Psychological Bulletin* 147(4):399–435, DOI 10.1037/bul0000309.)* `T2` `[classroom/applied settings, not laboratory]`
      - Of those effects, 82.9% were positive, 15.5% were negative, and 1.6% showed a null effect. *(Yang et al., 2021.)* `T2`
      - Test-enhanced learning generalized to elementary school (g = 0.328), middle school (g = 0.597), high school (g = 0.655), and university (g = 0.486); the elementary-school estimate was the smallest of the four. *(Yang et al., 2021.)* `T2`
      - Test format did not significantly modulate the benefits of classroom testing across seven formats examined. *(Yang et al., 2021.)* `T2`
      - The authors report that Rowland found corrective feedback almost doubled the testing benefit, whereas Adesope et al. found no moderating effect of feedback. *(Yang et al., 2021.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Three large meta-analyses converge on a headline of about 0.50 for practice testing over restudying, and the classroom-only estimate matches the laboratory estimates closely. The headline conceals a nearly complete cancellation in one identifiable cell: no feedback plus initial retrieval success at or below 50% produced g = 0.03 with a confidence interval straddling zero. Because item difficulty in an adaptive program is deliberately set to produce substantial failure, this is the modal condition such a system creates rather than an edge case, and the remedy visible in the same moderator analysis is corrective feedback, which lifted the effect to 0.73 regardless of retrieval success. The three analyses disagree sharply on format — recognition and multiple choice were worst in one, best in another, and irrelevant in the third — which means format guidance cannot be treated as settled. The finding most relevant to a children's product is the education-level gradient: elementary school returned 0.328, roughly half the high-school estimate, so the age band a children's reasoning program operates in is the band where the mechanism is weakest. Roughly one classroom effect in six was negative.
    - **Link to source:** [The effect of testing versus restudy on retention](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559), [Rethinking the use of tests: a meta-analysis of practice testing](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306), [Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309)

- **Subcategory 3.3: How far test-enhanced learning transfers — the boundary governing any reasoning-ability claim**
  - **Source:** Pan & Rickard (2018); Agarwal (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The review encompassed 192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments and 67 articles (N = 10,382); a random-effects model revealed d = 0.40, 95% CI [0.31, 0.50], relative to a nontesting reexposure control. *(Pan & Rickard, 2018, *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7):710–756, DOI 10.1037/bul0000151.)* `T2`
      - Transfer was greatest across test formats, to application and inference questions, to problems involving medical diagnoses, and to mediator and related word cues. *(Pan & Rickard, 2018.)* `T2`
      - Transfer was weakest to rearranged stimulus-response items, to untested materials seen during initial study, and to problems involving worked examples. *(Pan & Rickard, 2018.)* `T2`
      - When there was no response congruency between the initial and final tests the weighted effect size was d = 0.28; if response congruency held, the estimated effect size increased by d = 0.30, yielding d = 0.58. *(Pan & Rickard, 2018.)* `T2`
      - The presence of elaborated retrieval practice accounted for an increase of d = 0.23, and higher initial test performance was associated with greater likelihood of transfer. *(Pan & Rickard, 2018.)* `T2`
      - In two assessments for publication bias, the moderator effect sizes were minimally affected, but the intercept predictions were substantially reduced, "often indicating no positive transfer when none of the aforementioned moderators are present." *(Pan & Rickard, 2018.)* `T2`
      - Middle school and college students engaged in retrieval practice with fact questions, higher-order questions, or a mix, across laboratory and K-12 settings. *(Agarwal, 2019, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 111(2):189–209, DOI 10.1037/edu0000282.)* `T2` `[middle school and college samples; expository passages]`
      - Higher-order and mixed quizzes improved higher-order test performance, but fact quizzes did not; retrieval practice with higher-order questions did not enhance delayed fact test performance. *(Agarwal, 2019.)* `T2`
      - When the type of initial quizzes matched the type of final test, retrieval practice yielded benefits for both fact and higher-order learning to a greater extent than the mixed quiz condition. *(Agarwal, 2019.)* `T2`
      - The author states that building a foundation of knowledge via fact-based retrieval practice may be less potent than engaging in higher-order retrieval practice, contrary to popular intuition based on Bloom's taxonomy, and reports the results as most consistent with transfer-appropriate processing. *(Agarwal, 2019.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strictest boundary condition in the category and the one that most directly limits claims about improving a general reasoning capacity. Transfer of test-enhanced learning averages 0.40, meaningfully below the 0.50 for same-material retention, and the average is carried almost entirely by three moderators: response congruency, elaborated retrieval practice, and high initial test performance. After publication-bias correction, the intercept often indicated no positive transfer at all when those moderators were absent. Transfer was weakest to problems involving worked examples, which is the format a reasoning-item trainer would most naturally use, though transfer to application and inference questions was among the strongest categories — so the mechanism is not confined to fact recall. Agarwal's experiments add the sharper practical finding: practice on facts did not ladder up to higher-order performance, and practice on higher-order questions did not improve fact recall. The practice items must therefore be built at the level of reasoning the program intends to move, rather than assumed to build toward it from simpler items.
    - **Link to source:** [Transfer of test-enhanced learning: meta-analytic review and synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151), [Retrieval practice and Bloom's taxonomy](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000282)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Spacing — a robust effect whose optimal gap is a shrinking fraction of the retention interval**
  - **Source:** Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006); Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The review found 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments across 184 articles. *(Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer, 2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354.)* `T2` `[verbal memory tasks with recall as the performance measure only]`
      - Across all retention intervals, massed presentation yielded 36.7% accuracy versus 47.3% for spaced presentation, across 271 comparisons with 14,811 participants, t(540) = 6.6, p < .001. *(Cepeda et al., 2006.)* `T2`
      - Only 12 of 271 comparisons of massed and spaced performance showed no effect or a negative effect from spacing. *(Cepeda et al., 2006.)* `T2`
      - Within individual retention-interval bins, the massed-versus-spaced difference was not statistically significant at 10 minutes to less than 1 day (p = .535), at 1 day (p = .249), or at 2–7 days (p = .190). *(Cepeda et al., 2006.)* `T2`
      - The interstudy interval producing maximal retention increased as retention interval increased. *(Cepeda et al., 2006.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that the review discloses how sorely lacking the century-old distributed-practice literature is in the information most needed if serious practical benefits are to be derived from it. *(Cepeda et al., 2006.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up taught more than 1,350 individuals a set of facts, gave a review after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and tested at a further delay of up to 1 year. *(Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler, 2008, *Psychological Science* 19(11):1095–1102, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x.)* `T2` `[fact learning; internet-recruited adult sample]`
      - At any given test delay, an increase in the interstudy gap at first increased, and then gradually reduced, final test performance. *(Cepeda et al., 2008.)* `T2`
      - Measured as a proportion of test delay, the optimal gap declined from about 20%–40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5%–10% of a 1-year test delay. *(Cepeda et al., 2008.)* `T2`
      - Interpolated optimal gaps for recall were approximately 3, 8, 12, and 27 days, corresponding to 43%, 23%, 17%, and 8% of the 7-, 35-, 70-, and 350-day retention intervals. *(Cepeda et al., 2008.)* `T2`
      - The 7-, 35-, 70-, and 350-day retention intervals yielded 10%, 59%, 111%, and 77% improvement in recall for the optimal gap compared with the zero-day gap. *(Cepeda et al., 2008.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The spacing effect is the most robust mechanism in this category by count: only 12 of 271 massed-versus-spaced comparisons failed to favor spacing, and the aggregate difference was 36.7% against 47.3% accuracy across nearly 15,000 participants. The scope restriction is severe, since the analysis covered verbal memory tasks assessed by recall and excluded recognition and non-verbal tasks entirely. The individual retention-interval bins spanning 10 minutes to 7 days — the operating range of most software review schedulers — each contained fewer than 16 studies and none reached significance on its own. The follow-up supplies the actual scheduling rule, and it is a ratio that shrinks rather than a constant: the gap maximising retention grows in absolute terms as the horizon grows, roughly 3 days for a 1-week horizon and 27 for a 1-year horizon, but falls as a proportion of it. The payoff also depends on the horizon, buying only a 10% recall improvement at 7 days but 111% at 70. A scheduler therefore cannot be tuned without first fixing the retention horizon it optimizes for, and a program evaluated on a short horizon will substantially understate spacing's benefit.
    - **Link to source:** [Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: a review and quantitative synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354), [Spacing effects in learning: a temporal ridgeline of optimal retention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)

- **Subcategory 3.5: Interleaving — a moderate average effect that reverses sign depending on material type**
  - **Source:** Rohrer & Taylor (2007); Brunmair & Richter (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - College students learned to solve multiple types of problems, with practice problems either blocked by type or randomly mixed, and a test one week later. *(Rohrer & Taylor, 2007, *Instructional Science* 35(6):481–498, DOI 10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8.)* `T2` `[college students; small samples; mathematics volume-formula problems]`
      - Practice session performance was impeded by mixture: the blocked group averaged 89% versus the mixed group's 60%, t(16) = 3.14, p < .01, d = 1.06. *(Rohrer & Taylor, 2007.)* `T2`
      - The blocked group's advantage was due primarily to the first session (87% vs. 43%); in the second practice session the difference was not statistically significant (91% vs. 78%). *(Rohrer & Taylor, 2007.)* `T2`
      - On the test one week later, the mixed group averaged 63% versus the blocked group's 20%, t(14) = 2.64, p < .05, d = 1.34; the interaction between practice strategy and experiment phase was significant, F(1,16) = 35.08, p < .001. *(Rohrer & Taylor, 2007.)* `T2`
      - A meta-analysis based on 59 studies with 238 effect sizes nested in 158 samples revealed a moderate overall interleaving effect of Hedges' g = 0.42. *(Brunmair & Richter, 2019, *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11):1029–1052, DOI 10.1037/bul0000209.)* `T2`
      - Interleaved practice was best for studies using paintings (g = 0.67) and other visual materials, while mathematical tasks revealed a small effect, g = 0.34, 95% CI [0.11, 0.57]. *(Brunmair & Richter, 2019.)* `T2`
      - Results for expository texts were ambiguous with a nonsignificant estimate of g = 0.21, p = .119. *(Brunmair & Richter, 2019.)* `T2` `[verbal materials]`
      - An advantage of blocking compared with interleaving was found for studies based on words as learning materials, g = −0.39, 95% CI [−0.64, −0.14]. *(Brunmair & Richter, 2019.)* `T2`
      - A multiple meta-regression revealed stronger interleaving effects for learning material more similar between categories, less similar within categories, and more complex. *(Brunmair & Richter, 2019.)* `T2`
      - The authors conclude that interleaved learning "should be used with caution in certain conditions, especially for expository texts and words," while cautioning that the negative effect for words "might not be caused by the use of words but rather by the task types or the specific categories or other (unknown) variables that characterized the few studies using words." *(Brunmair & Richter, 2019.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Rohrer and Taylor produced the largest published performance-versus-learning dissociation in this category: blocked practice beat mixed practice by 29 percentage points during acquisition and lost to it by 43 percentage points one week later. The acquisition penalty was concentrated in the first practice session and had largely closed by the second, which means a program that abandons interleaving based on early session data would discard it precisely when its cost is highest and its benefit not yet measurable. The meta-analysis then documents the clearest sign reversal in the category: an overall 0.42 decomposes into paintings at 0.67, mathematics at 0.34, expository text at a nonsignificant 0.21, and words at −0.39, where blocking actively beat interleaving with a confidence interval entirely below zero. The mechanism the meta-regression identifies is discrimination — interleaving pays when categories are similar to each other and internally varied, which is a property of the material rather than of the schedule. For a program mixing visual reasoning items and verbal items, the same scheduling policy is predicted to help one item family and harm the other.
    - **Link to source:** [The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8), [Similarity matters: a meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)

- **Subcategory 3.6: Desirable difficulties and cognitive load — the two theories that predict when the same manipulation helps and when it harms**
  - **Source:** Bjork & Bjork (2011); Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors distinguish storage strength, which reflects how entrenched or interassociated a memory representation is, from retrieval strength, which reflects current accessibility and is heavily influenced by situational cues and recency. *(Bjork & Bjork, 2011, in Gernsbacher, Pew, Hough & Pomerantz (Eds.), *Psychology and the Real World*, pp. 56–64, Worth Publishers.)* `T1` `[book chapter; theoretical framing plus illustrative studies, not peer-reviewed]`
      - They assume that current performance is entirely a function of current retrieval strength, but that storage strength retards forgetting and enhances relearning. *(Bjork & Bjork, 2011.)* `T1`
      - The stated key idea is that "conditions that most rapidly increase retrieval strength differ from the conditions that maximize the gain of storage strength," so that learners who interpret current retrieval strength as storage strength become susceptible to preferring poorer conditions of learning to better ones. *(Bjork & Bjork, 2011.)* `T1`
      - The desirable difficulties enumerated are varying the conditions of learning, interleaving instruction on separate topics, spacing rather than massing, and using tests rather than presentations as study events. *(Bjork & Bjork, 2011.)* `T1`
      - The boundary is stated explicitly: desirable difficulties trigger encoding and retrieval processes that support learning, but "if, however, the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties." *(Bjork & Bjork, 2011.)* `T1`
      - Cognitive load theory emphasises that all novel information is first processed by a capacity- and duration-limited working memory and then stored in an unlimited long-term memory; once stored, "the capacity and duration limits of working memory disappear transforming our ability to function." *(Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 2019, *Educational Psychology Review* 31(2):261–292, DOI 10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5.)* `T2`
      - Intrinsic load refers to the complexity of the information and is related to element interactivity; extraneous load is determined by how information is presented and what the instructional procedure requires, and can be changed by changing procedures. *(Sweller et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - Complexity or element interactivity depends on a combination of both the nature of the information and the knowledge of the person processing it. *(Sweller et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - Departing from the 1998 formulation, the authors now assume that "germane cognitive load redistributes working memory resources from extraneous activities to activities directly relevant to learning," so that "only intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load are distinguished as basic categories of cognitive load." *(Sweller et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - The prior assumption that general working memory capacity is fixed "must be modified" in light of evidence that working memory depletion occurs after cognitive effort and recovers after rest. *(Sweller et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - The authors note that many experiments underlying the original cognitive load effects did not attempt to directly measure comparative cognitive load; the theory was used to generate instructional techniques, and if those produced the expected learning outcomes they were assumed to strengthen the theory. *(Sweller et al., 2019.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The desirable-difficulties claim is narrower than the popular slogan: it is not that difficulty helps, but that conditions optimising the rate of gain in retrieval strength differ from conditions optimising the gain in storage strength, and only retrieval strength is observable in the moment. The stated failure condition is a learner lacking the background knowledge to respond successfully, at which point the same manipulation becomes an undesirable difficulty — which lines up exactly with the meta-analytic finding that unfeedbacked retrieval below 50% success yields no benefit, giving the boundary both a theoretical statement and an empirical estimate. The 2019 cognitive-load restatement is materially different from the version usually cited: the three-way load partition is reduced to two, germane load is reconceptualised as redistribution rather than a third additive load, and the fixed-capacity assumption is softened. Element interactivity is defined as jointly determined by the material and by what the learner already knows, which makes load learner-relative rather than a property of content. The authors' own methodological caveat matters for anyone planning to instrument load in software: much of the supporting evidence inferred load from learning outcomes rather than measuring it.
    - **Link to source:** [Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf), [Cognitive architecture and instructional design: 20 years later](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5)

- **Subcategory 3.7: Worked examples, guidance fading, and the expertise reversal effect**
  - **Source:** Sweller & Cooper (1985); Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003); Renkl & Atkinson (2003); Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Five experiments studied algebra manipulation problems with Year 9, Year 11, and university mathematics students. *(Sweller & Cooper, 1985, *Cognition and Instruction* 2(1):59–89, DOI 10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3.)* `T2` `[algebra problems; secondary and university samples]`
      - Worked examples required considerably less time to process than conventional problems, and subsequent similar problems were solved more rapidly, with decreased solution time accompanied by a decrease in mathematical errors. *(Sweller & Cooper, 1985.)* `T2`
      - "Both of these findings were specific to problems identical in structure to the initial ones." *(Sweller & Cooper, 1985.)* `T2`
      - "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners. We call this phenomenon the expertise reversal effect." *(Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller, 2003, *Educational Psychologist* 38(1):23–31, DOI 10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4.)* `T2`
      - A large number of cognitive load theory effects "are, in fact, only applicable to learners with very limited experience. With additional experience, specific experimental effects can first disappear and then reverse." *(Kalyuga et al., 2003.)* `T2`
      - In reviewed spreadsheet experiments, more knowledgeable students found imagining procedures more beneficial than studying worked examples, whereas less knowledgeable students found imagining had a negative effect. *(Kalyuga et al., 2003.)* `T2`
      - In reviewed reading work, additions to a biology text intended to increase coherence were beneficial only for low-knowledge readers, while high-knowledge readers benefitted most from the original, minimally coherent text. *(Kalyuga et al., 2003.)* `T2`
      - The stated implication is that "to be efficient, instructional design should be tailored to the level of experience of intended learners. Without such tailoring, the effectiveness of instructional designs is likely to be random." *(Kalyuga et al., 2003.)* `T2`
      - Learning from worked-out examples is very effective during the initial stages of cognitive skill acquisition; "in later stages, however, solving problems is superior." *(Renkl & Atkinson, 2003, *Educational Psychologist* 38(1):15–22, DOI 10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3.)* `T2`
      - The authors propose a fading procedure in which problem-solving elements are successively integrated into example study; in their backward-fading implementation the first task is fully worked, the second omits the last step, the third omits the last two, and finally a full problem is presented. *(Renkl & Atkinson, 2003.)* `T2` `[the reported comparison involved two intact classrooms totalling 35 students, one physics topic, 2-day posttest]`
      - The authors call for future research on how to make the fading procedure adaptive to learners' prior knowledge levels. *(Renkl & Atkinson, 2003.)* `T2`
      - Although successively fading worked-out solution steps reliably fosters performance on near-transfer tasks relative to example–problem pairs, "this effect is not reliable on far-transfer tasks." *(Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill, 2003, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 95(4):774–783, DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774.)* `T2`
      - Combining fading with prompts encouraging learners to identify the underlying principle in each worked step produced medium to large effects on near and far transfer across two experiments, without requiring additional time on task. *(Atkinson et al., 2003.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The founding worked-example study reported both the benefit and its limit in the same abstract: studying worked examples cut processing time and errors, but both findings held only for problems identical in structure to those studied, which the authors read as evidence that novices extract narrow schemas rather than general rules. The expertise reversal effect is the sharpest statement in this category that a mechanism can invert on the same learner over time — guidance that reduces load for a novice becomes redundant information for a more knowledgeable learner, so the format that produced a gain in week one can produce a loss in week six. Two of the reviewed cases are full crossovers rather than attenuations. Renkl and Atkinson convert the warning into a schedule by removing worked steps one at a time from the last step backward, though their own empirical support is two intact classrooms and a 2-day posttest. The decisive qualification comes from Atkinson and colleagues: fading alone reliably improves near transfer but not far transfer, and the combination that did move far transfer added self-explanation prompts requiring the learner to name the principle behind each step.
    - **Link to source:** [The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3), [The expertise reversal effect](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4), [Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3), [Transitioning from studying examples to solving problems](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774)

- **Subcategory 3.8: Feedback's double edge — the mechanism with the best-documented harm rate**
  - **Source:** Kluger & DeNisi (1996); Hattie & Timperley (2007); Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Of 131 usable papers, 607 effect sizes were extracted, based on 12,652 participants and 23,663 observations. *(Kluger & DeNisi, 1996, *Psychological Bulletin* 119(2):254–284, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254.)* `T2`
      - "The weighted mean (weighted by sample size) of this distribution is 0.41, suggesting that, on average, FI has a moderate positive effect on performance. However, over 38% of the effects were negative." *(Kluger & DeNisi, 1996.)* `T2`
      - The finding that over one third of feedback interventions decreased performance "cannot be explained by sampling error, feedback sign, or existing theories." *(Kluger & DeNisi, 1996.)* `T2`
      - The weighted variance of the effect distribution was 0.97, whereas the estimate of sampling error variance was only 0.09. *(Kluger & DeNisi, 1996.)* `T2`
      - The central assumption of Feedback Intervention Theory is that feedback changes the locus of attention among three hierarchically organized levels — task learning, task motivation, and meta-tasks including self-related processes — and effectiveness decreases as attention moves closer to the self and away from the task. *(Kluger & DeNisi, 1996.)* `T2`
      - Approximately 37% of the papers considered manipulated feedback without a control group and 16% reported confounded treatments, so roughly two thirds of the empirical feedback literature cannot shed light on feedback effects on performance. *(Kluger & DeNisi, 1996.)* `T2`
      - A synthesis of 12 previous meta-analyses covering 196 studies reported an average effect size for feedback of 0.79. *(Hattie & Timperley, 2007, *Review of Educational Research* 77(1):81–112, DOI 10.3102/003465430298487.)* `T2` `[meta-synthesis of prior meta-analyses, not a primary meta-analysis]`
      - Effective feedback needs to address one of three questions: "Where am I going?", "How am I going?", and "Where to next?" *(Hattie & Timperley, 2007.)* `T2`
      - The model distinguishes four levels at which feedback can be directed — the task, the process of understanding the task, self-regulation, and the self — and feedback directed to the self is characterised as the least effective level. *(Hattie & Timperley, 2007.)* `T2`
      - A primary meta-analysis covering 435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000, reported a medium effect of d = 0.48 of feedback on student learning cleared of extreme values. *(Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie, 2020, *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:3087, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087.)* `T2`
      - "The integration of all study effects with the random-effects model leads to a weighted average effect size of d = 0.55. 17% of the effects were negative." *(Wisniewski et al., 2020.)* `T2`
      - By type: reinforcement or punishment, k = 39, d = 0.24; corrective feedback, k = 238, d = 0.46; high-information feedback, k = 42, d = 0.99, 95% CI [0.82, 1.15]. *(Wisniewski et al., 2020.)* `T2`
      - Feedback had higher impact on cognitive and motor skills outcomes than on motivational and behavioral outcomes, and the significant heterogeneity (I² = 86.47%) shows feedback cannot be understood as a single consistent form of treatment. *(Wisniewski et al., 2020.)* `T2`
      - The authors record that estimates of feedback effects range between 0.48 (their own), 0.70 (Hattie, 2009), and 0.79 (Hattie and Timperley, 2007), and characterise Kluger and DeNisi as reporting "an average effect of 0.38... about a third of the effects were negative." *(Wisniewski et al., 2020.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the single most important set of numbers for anyone building an always-on feedback surface: the mean effect is moderate at roughly 0.41 to 0.55, and between 17% and 38% of individual effects are negative depending on the analysis. The original authors explicitly rule out sampling error, noting that observed variance was roughly ten times the sampling-error estimate, so the harm is real heterogeneity rather than noise. Their proposed mechanism is attentional — feedback drawing attention toward the self and away from the task loses and then reverses its benefit — and the four-level taxonomy converges on the same point by placing praise and criticism directed at the person at the least effective level. The most operationally useful breakdown is by feedback type: high-information feedback reached 0.99 while reinforcement or punishment reached only 0.24, a fourfold spread with non-overlapping confidence intervals, and corrective feedback, the category software most naturally implements, sat in the middle at 0.46. Note that the widely cited 0.79 figure is a synthesis of meta-analyses that its own co-author later revised down to 0.48 on primary data.

- **Subcategory 3.9: Self-explanation — a large effect that depends on who actually explains**
  - **Source:** Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994); Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Without extensive training, 14 eighth-grade students were asked to self-explain after reading each line of a passage on the human circulatory system; 10 control students read the same text twice without prompts. *(Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher, 1994, *Cognitive Science* 18(3):439–477, DOI 10.1016/0364-0213(94)90016-7.)* `T2` `[24 eighth-grade students total; single expository text; study time equated]`
      - From pretest to posttest, the gain was greater for the prompted group (32%) than the unprompted group (22%), and the interaction between groups and tests was significant. *(Chi et al., 1994.)* `T2`
      - Prompted students who generated a large number of self-explanations learned with greater understanding than low explainers; high explainers generated 87 inferences and low explainers 29. *(Chi et al., 1994.)* `T2`

      - A systematic search identified 69 effect sizes from 64 research reports; a random effects analysis of 5,917 participants obtained an overall point estimate of g = .55, 95% CI [.45, .65], p < .001, favouring participants prompted to self-explain. *(Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne, 2018, *Educational Psychology Review* 30(3):703–725, DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x.)* `T2`
      - There was statistically detectable heterogeneity, Q = 196.63, p < .001, df = 68. *(Bisra et al., 2018.)* `T2`
      - Of 20 coded moderator variables, only three showed statistically detectable differences with implications for theory or practice. *(Bisra et al., 2018.)* `T2`

That flat moderator profile stands out sharply next to interleaving's material-type reversal or feedback's fourfold type spread. The founding experiment, however, locates the variance somewhere a product cannot directly control: the prompt-versus-no-prompt contrast produced a modest difference in gain, 32% against 22%, while the much larger separation was within the prompted group, where high explainers generated roughly three times as many inferences as low explainers and all of them reached the correct mental model. Issuing the prompt is cheap; the variance that predicts understanding lives in the quantity and quality of what the learner produces in response. ### Category 4: Performance during learning is not learning — why an app watching a child improve in real time may be watching nothing durable

*General idea: Every telemetry stream a learning app can collect is a measure of performance under support — accuracy, speed, streaks, session-over-session improvement — and performance under support is a known-unreliable proxy for the durable change the app claims to produce. Sixty years of learning research establishes that the two can move in opposite directions: manipulations that make acquisition feel fast and error-free often degrade long-term retention and transfer, while manipulations that slow acquisition and induce errors often improve it. The strongest recent demonstration is a field experiment in which a GPT-4 tutor raised high school students' practice-problem grades by 48% and then, once the tutor was removed, left them scoring 17% below students who never had access at all. A contrary result from a Harvard physics randomized trial found a scaffolded AI tutor outperforming an active-learning classroom by a large margin — but measured only immediately after the lesson, with the scaffold's removal never tested. Compounding the measurement problem, the child's own sense of mastery is generated by the same misleading signal, because ease of processing in the presence of support is systematically misread as knowledge. For a program that intends to identify children who improve rapidly, this means the improvement signal itself is the thing most in need of validation.*

- **Subcategory 4.1: The learning–performance distinction is the foundational measurement problem, and the two can move in opposite directions**
  - **Source:** Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors state that during instruction or training what can be observed and measured is performance, "which is often an unreliable index of whether the relatively long-term changes that constitute learning have taken place." *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10(2):176–199, p. 176, DOI 10.1177/1745691615569000.)* `T2`
      - Early animal and motor-skills research established that learning can occur even when no discernible changes in performance are observed. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, p. 176.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that the converse has more recently been shown: improvements in performance can fail to yield significant learning, and certain manipulations can have opposite effects on learning and performance. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, p. 176.)* `T2`
      - Reviewing Landauer and Bjork (1978), the authors note that a massed-testing condition — several tests administered in succession immediately after presentation of a name — showed nearly errorless performance during the acquisition phase, yet produced worse long-term learning than expanded-interval and equal-interval testing schedules. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Reviewing Shea and Morgan (1979), the authors report that blocked practice produced better performance during acquisition while random (interleaved) practice produced better retention when tested 10 minutes and 10 days later. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - In a reviewed badminton-serve study, the blocked-practice group performed better during training while the interleaved group showed better long-term retention, whether tested from the same or the opposite side of the court from which the serves were practiced. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Reviewing Goode, Geraci and Roediger (2008), the authors report that participants who practiced multiple variants of an anagram took longer to solve anagrams during practice — a short-term performance decrement — but solved relatively more anagrams on a later test than participants who repeatedly solved the exact anagram that was later tested. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Under Bjork and Bjork's new theory of disuse, as presented by the authors, conditions that increase current retrieval strength "might benefit performance in the short term but will fail to produce the type of permanent changes that characterize long-term learning." *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - The authors report that learners regularly exhibit a "stability bias" — the tendency to believe that current accessibility of retrieved information will remain stable across time, rather than appreciating factors that may impair or enhance later learning. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - The authors report a survey of 472 college students in which most reported using a rereading strategy, and in which 64% reported not revisiting material once they felt they had mastered it. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Soderstrom and Bjork define the central measurement problem directly: learning is a long-term change that must be inferred, while performance is what is observable during instruction, and performance is often an unreliable index of learning. The relationship is not merely noisy but can invert in both directions — learning can accrue with no visible performance change, and visible performance gains can accrue with no learning. Their reviewed evidence includes massed testing that produced nearly errorless acquisition and worse retention, blocked practice that beat interleaved practice during training and lost to it at 10-day retention, and variable anagram practice that was slower during acquisition and better on the later test. Their theoretical account holds that conditions increasing current retrieval strength can benefit short-term performance while failing to produce durable change. They further document that learners themselves exhibit a stability bias, and that 64% of surveyed students stopped revisiting material once it felt mastered. For a learning app, the implication is that the very telemetry that is easiest to collect is the measure this literature identifies as least trustworthy.
    - **Link to source:** [Learning versus performance: an integrative review](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

- **Subcategory 4.2: Whether a practice activity "worked" cannot be determined without specifying the test**
  - **Source:** Morris, Bransford & Franks (1977); connection drawn in Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Experiment 1 showed that semantic acquisition was superior to rhyme acquisition given a standard recognition test, whereas rhyme acquisition was superior to semantic acquisition given a rhyming recognition test. *(Morris, Bransford & Franks, 1977, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 16(5):519–533, p. 519, DOI 10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80016-9.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that the first finding supports, while the second finding contradicts, the levels-of-processing claim that depth of processing leads to stronger memory traces. *(Morris et al., 1977, p. 519.)* `T2`
      - Experiment 2 replicated these findings using both immediate and delayed recognition tests. *(Morris et al., 1977, p. 519.)* `T2`
      - Experiment 3 indicated that these effects were not dependent upon the number of times a rhyme sound was presented during acquisition. *(Morris et al., 1977, p. 519.)* `T2`
      - The authors interpret the results in terms of an alternate framework they term transfer appropriate processing. *(Morris et al., 1977, p. 519.)* `T2`
      - Desirable difficulties generally accord with the principle of transfer-appropriate processing and the related encoding specificity principle, both of which contend that memory will improve to the extent that the engaged study and test processes overlap. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10(2):176–199, DOI 10.1177/1745691615569000.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Morris, Bransford and Franks manipulated encoding task and test type independently and found that neither encoding operation was superior in absolute terms. Semantic encoding beat rhyme encoding on a standard recognition test, and rhyme encoding beat semantic encoding on a rhyming recognition test. The reversal held across immediate and delayed tests, and a third experiment ruled out an explanation based on rhyme-sound repetition frequency. The authors' transfer-appropriate-processing framework holds that the value of an encoding operation is not a property of the operation but of the match between study processing and test processing. Soderstrom and Bjork later connect this principle to desirable difficulties, noting that memory improves to the extent that study and test processes overlap. For an app, this means a reported "improvement" is undefined until the transfer target is specified: improvement on in-app items may not be improvement on anything the child will face outside the app.
    - **Link to source:** [Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371%2877%2980016-9)

- **Subcategory 4.3: How much help to give is a named, unsolved problem in instructional science — not a design detail**
  - **Source:** Koedinger & Aleven (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors reinterpret Cognitive Tutor experiments as partial answers to the general question: "How should learning environments balance information or assistance giving and withholding to achieve optimal student learning?" *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007, *Educational Psychology Review* 19(3):239–264, p. 239, DOI 10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0.)* `T2`
      - The authors name this problem the "assistance dilemma" and state that "How best to achieve this balance remains a fundamental open problem in instructional science." *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007, p. 239.)* `T2`
      - The authors emphasize the need for further science to yield specific conditions and parameters indicating when and to what extent to use information-giving versus information-withholding forms of interaction. *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007, p. 239.)* `T2`
      - The authors enumerate concrete instances of the dilemma, including whether feedback should be immediate or delayed, and whether instructional hints should be more specific or more general. *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that the assistance dilemma is related to Bjork's "desirable difficulties" and the notion that while assisting performance during instruction can sometimes improve learning, in some cases making performance more difficult during instruction improves learning. *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007.)* `T2`
      - In designing the Cognitive Tutor feedback strategy, the authors report that "an overriding concern was to minimize floundering on the part of students," because under ACT-R theory time spent pursuing incorrect paths does not contribute to learning. *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007.)* `T2`
      - The authors report that log data from the Geometry tutor indicate students frequently use bottom-out hints to obtain answers, without reading prior hints that explain the material. *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007.)* `T2`
      - The authors report that a study by Corbett and Anderson (1995) provided no support for the effectiveness of information withholding via simple forms of delayed feedback. *(Koedinger & Aleven, 2007.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Koedinger and Aleven give the field its standard name for the trade-off an adaptive learning system must resolve on every interaction: the assistance dilemma, or how to balance giving and withholding information to achieve optimal learning. They state explicitly that the optimal balance point remains a fundamental open problem in instructional science, and that the field lacks the specific conditions and parameters needed to decide when and how much to assist. They connect the dilemma directly to Bjork's desirable difficulties, acknowledging that easing performance during instruction sometimes helps learning and sometimes does not. Their own Cognitive Tutor design chose the assistance-giving side, with an overriding concern to minimize student floundering. They also report that students frequently exploit that assistance, using bottom-out hints to obtain answers without reading the explanatory hints preceding them. Notably, their evidence does not uniformly favor withholding either: Corbett and Anderson's delayed-feedback manipulation showed no benefit, which means an app cannot resolve the dilemma by simply removing support.
    - **Link to source:** [Exploring the assistance dilemma in experiments with Cognitive Tutors](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0)

- **Subcategory 4.4: A field experiment where an AI tutor raised practice performance and lowered exam performance once removed**
  - **Source:** Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman (2025); earlier working-paper version (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The study is a randomized controlled trial conducted at a large high school in Turkey during the Fall semester of the 2023–2024 academic year, comprising four 90-minute sessions for about fifty 9th, 10th and 11th grade classes and nearly 1,000 students; for each grade the sessions collectively comprised about 15% of the math curriculum covered during the semester. *(Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman, 2025, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 122(26):e2422633122, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122.)* `T2`
      - Students were randomized across three arms: a control arm with access to course notes and textbook only; "GPT Base," a simple GPT-4 chat interface similar to ChatGPT; and "GPT Tutor," which used the same interface but whose prompt instructed GPT-4 to provide hints without directly giving the answer and supplied teacher-provided problem-specific information including correct solutions and common student mistakes. *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - On the GPT-assisted practice sessions, intention-to-treat estimates show GPT Base and GPT Tutor increased student scores by 0.137 and 0.361 (out of 1) respectively, relative to a control-arm mean performance of 0.28 — implying performance increases of 48% and 127%. *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - On the subsequent unassisted exam, performance in the GPT Base arm degraded by 0.054 (out of 1) relative to the control arm — a 17% reduction relative to the average control student — which the authors describe as statistically significantly worse than control. *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - On the unassisted exam, GPT Tutor arm performance was statistically indistinguishable from the control arm, with a point estimate of −0.004 — smaller by an order of magnitude than the GPT Base estimate; the authors state that they "still do not observe a positive effect." *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - Querying GPT Base ten times for each of the 57 practice problems using students' most common message ("What is the answer?"), the authors found GPT Base gave a correct answer only 51% of the time on average, with logical errors 42% of the time and arithmetic errors 8% of the time. *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - GPT Base's logical errors affected performance on the practice problems, but the authors found no evidence that this effect spilled over to the corresponding exam problems, leading them to favor the "crutch" explanation over the "misled by errors" explanation. *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - Restricting to students' very first interaction with the tool in the first session, 56% of GPT Base and 42% of GPT Tutor messages either repeated the question text or asked for the answer; considering the first interaction across all problems in the first session, this figure increased to 67% for GPT Base and decreased for GPT Tutor. *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - Students interacted substantially less with GPT Base than with GPT Tutor in average number of messages per problem, which the authors state is consistent with their hypothesis that GPT Base simply provides students with solutions. *(Bastani et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - The work previously circulated as an SSRN working paper under the shorter title "Generative AI Can Harm Learning," SSRN abstract ID 4895486, dated 2024. *(Bastani et al., 2024, *SSRN Electronic Journal*, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4895486.)* `T1` `[working paper superseded by the peer-reviewed PNAS version; the retitling added "without guardrails," which materially narrows the claim]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the clearest available demonstration that an app can watch a child improve in real time and be watching nothing durable. In a randomized trial with nearly a thousand high school math students, a plain ChatGPT-style GPT-4 tutor raised practice-session grades by 48% and a guardrailed tutor raised them by 127% — both large, immediately visible gains of exactly the kind an app's dashboard would report. When access was removed for the unassisted exam, students who had used the plain tutor scored 17% below students who had never had access, a statistically significant harm rather than a null. The guardrailed tutor eliminated the harm, with a point estimate an order of magnitude smaller, but the authors are explicit that it produced no positive learning effect either, despite its far larger practice-time advantage. The mechanism analysis rules out the obvious alternative explanation: GPT Base was wrong 49% of the time, but its logical errors did not spill over to exam performance, pointing to crutch use rather than misinformation. Interaction logs support this, with 67% of first interactions across problems in the GPT Base arm consisting of repeating the question or asking for the answer. The design isolates precisely the variable that matters for a learning app: the size of the practice gain was uninformative about, and inversely related to, what remained when the support was withdrawn.
    - **Link to source:** [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), [Earlier working-paper version](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4895486)

- **Subcategory 4.5: The contrary result — a scaffolded AI tutor outperformed an active-learning classroom, measured immediately**
  - **Source:** Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti (2025); earlier preprint version (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The study is a randomized controlled crossover experiment in a large undergraduate physics course at Harvard University with N = 194 students; of 233 enrolled students, 194 were eligible based on consent, participation in both conditions, and completion of all pre- and post-tests. *(Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti, 2025, *Scientific Reports* 15:17458, DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6.)* `T2` `[undergraduates at a highly selective university, not children]`
      - In week one, group 1 used the AI tutor at home while group 2 participated in an in-class active-learning lesson; the conditions were reversed the following week, covering surface tension in the first week and fluid flow in the second. *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - Students in the AI group exhibited a higher median post-score (M = 4.5, N = 142) than those in the in-class active-learning group (M = 3.5, N = 174), relative to a combined pre-test baseline of M = 2.75 (N = 316); median learning gains in the AI-tutored group were over double those of the in-class group. *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - A two-sample rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) test comparing post-score distributions found a statistically significant difference, z = −5.6, p < 10⁻⁸. *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - The authors report a linear-regression effect size of 0.63, state that this is an underestimation due to a ceiling effect in post-test scores, and report a quantile-regression estimate in the range of 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations. *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - On time on task, in-class students spent 15 of a 75-minute period on the pre- and post-tests with an assumed 60 minutes of learning; the median time on task for the AI group was 49 minutes, and 70% of AI-group students spent less than 60 minutes. *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - The authors report no correlation between time on task and students' post-test scores, despite a wide range of measured times in the AI group. *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - The AI tutor was not an unmodified chatbot: the authors avoided relying solely on GPT-4 to generate solutions and enriched their prompts with comprehensive step-by-step answers, and the platform was designed to guide students sequentially through each part of each problem. *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - Learning was measured by post-tests administered following each lesson; the authors report no delayed retention test, and state in their discussion that studying a full course would allow "systematic integration of well-established retention-enhancing strategies (e.g., spacing)." *(Kestin et al., 2025.)* `T2`
      - An earlier version circulated as a Research Square preprint posted 14 May 2024 under the title "AI Tutoring Outperforms Active Learning," whose abstract claimed students "learn more than twice as much in less time when using an AI tutor." *(Kestin et al., 2024, Research Square preprint, DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-4243877/v1.)* `T1` `[preprint superseded by the peer-reviewed version]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Kestin and colleagues report the strongest published counter-case to the harm result: a randomized crossover trial in which an AI-tutored group reached a median post-test score of 4.5 against 3.5 for an in-class active-learning group, more than double the median gain over baseline, with a Mann–Whitney z of −5.6 and p below 10⁻⁸. The effect is large by conventional standards, estimated at 0.63 by linear regression and 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations by quantile regression once ceiling effects are accounted for. The AI group also spent less time, with a median of 49 minutes against an assumed 60 for the classroom, and time on task showed no correlation with post-test score. Two design features matter for interpretation. First, the tutor was heavily scaffolded — prompts contained step-by-step solutions and the platform sequenced students through problem parts — which places it closer to the guardrailed condition of the harm study than to a plain chatbot. Second, outcomes were measured by post-tests administered immediately after each lesson, with no delayed retention test reported, which is precisely the measurement window identified elsewhere in this category as an unreliable index of learning. The study therefore establishes a strong immediate-performance advantage; it does not test what happens when the tutor is taken away.
    - **Link to source:** [AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6), [Earlier preprint version](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-4243877/v1)

- **Subcategory 4.6: Offloading cognition to a tool changes what is retained internally, and creates a self-reinforcing drift**
  - **Source:** Risko & Gilbert (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors define cognitive offloading as "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand." *(Risko & Gilbert, 2016, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20(9):676–688, p. 676, DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that propensity to offload cognition is influenced by metacognitive evaluations of our mental abilities, not only by actual internal demand. *(Risko & Gilbert, 2016, p. 676.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that offloading memory demands in a transactive system is "not a 'free pass'" in terms of cognitive consequences. *(Risko & Gilbert, 2016.)* `T2`
      - The authors' framework predicts that offloading to a more accurate external artefact will likely reduce both internal memory for that content and metacognitive confidence in it, which in turn increases the likelihood of offloading again — "a kind of self-reinforcing pattern that will produce a drift away from reliance on internal capabilities when situated in an environment with effective cognitive technologies." *(Risko & Gilbert, 2016.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that any benefit of offloading will be contingent on the fact that the demand being offloaded is unnecessary with respect to the learning goal. *(Risko & Gilbert, 2016.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that long-term reliance on automated processes could lead to cognitive "skill decay," where a developed ability deteriorates over time. *(Risko & Gilbert, 2016.)* `T2`
      - The authors report that when participants knew they would subsequently have access to the Internet, they were more likely to answer "don't know" and reported lower feeling-of-knowing on trivia questions, reducing willingness to offer an answer based on their own knowledge. *(Risko & Gilbert, 2016.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Risko and Gilbert supply the mechanism that connects tool availability to degraded internal capability. They define cognitive offloading as physical action that reduces a task's information-processing demand, and establish that the decision to offload is driven partly by metacognitive beliefs about one's own abilities rather than by actual capacity limits. Their central caution is that offloading memory into a transactive system is not free. Their framework predicts a feedback loop: relying on an accurate external aid reduces both internal memory for the content and confidence in that memory, which raises the probability of offloading next time, producing what they call a drift away from reliance on internal capabilities. They also note that offloading benefits depend on the offloaded demand being unnecessary to the learning goal — a condition an app cannot verify from performance data alone. Applied to a children's reasoning program, the model predicts that a helpful scaffold used repeatedly will both lower what the child retains and lower the child's own sense that they could have done it unaided.
    - **Link to source:** [Cognitive offloading](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002)

- **Subcategory 4.7: The offloading effect measured directly, and the boundary condition that governs it**
  - **Source:** Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011); Schooler & Storm (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors report across four studies that when people expect to have future access to information, they have lower rates of recall of the information itself and enhanced recall instead for where to access it. *(Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, 2011, *Science* 333(6043):776–778, p. 776, DOI 10.1126/science.1207745.)* `T2` `[adult participants, trivia statements]`
      - In Experiment 2, participants who believed the computer erased what they typed had the best recall of the trivia statements, with Erase M = 0.31 (SD = 0.04) and Erase-Remember M = 0.29 (SD = 0.07), compared with Save M = 0.22 (SD = 0.07) and Save-Remember M = 0.19 (SD = 0.09); the omnibus test was F(3, 56) = 2.80, P < 0.05. *(Sparrow et al., 2011.)* `T2`
      - Explicitly instructing participants to try to remember the information did not produce a significant effect; only the saved/erased manipulation did. *(Sparrow et al., 2011.)* `T2`
      - The authors conclude that the Internet has become a primary form of external or transactive memory, where information is stored collectively outside ourselves. *(Sparrow et al., 2011, p. 776.)* `T2`
      - Given recent concerns about the robustness and replicability of the "Google Effect," two experiments were conducted seeking to replicate and extend the phenomenon by identifying a potential boundary condition. *(Schooler & Storm, 2021, *Memory* 29(9):1101–1110, DOI 10.1080/09658211.2021.1962356.)* `T2`
      - In Experiment 1 the authors replicated the Google Effect, but only when participants experienced a practice phase demonstrating the reliability of the saving process; no evidence of the effect was observed when the practice phase demonstrated the saving process to be unreliable. *(Schooler & Storm, 2021.)* `T2`
      - Experiment 2 replicated the reliable-condition results and demonstrated the effect to be robust across 10 different topics of trivia statements. *(Schooler & Storm, 2021.)* `T2`
      - The authors conclude that the Google Effect is a replicable phenomenon, but that the perceived reliability of the saving process is critical for determining whether it is observed. *(Schooler & Storm, 2021.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Sparrow, Liu and Wegner provide the direct experimental version of the offloading claim. Across four studies they find that expected future access to information lowers recall of the information itself while raising recall of where to find it. Experiment 2 isolates the manipulation cleanly: participants told their typed statements would be erased recalled roughly 0.31 of them, against roughly 0.22 for those told the statements would be saved. The instruction to try to remember had no significant effect, meaning the effect was driven by the availability expectation rather than by effort or intent. Schooler and Storm then address the replicability concerns attached to that finding rather than assuming them away, and find the effect replicates conditionally: it appears when participants have experienced the saving process working reliably, and disappears when they have experienced it failing. This sharpens rather than weakens the design implication. An app whose help is consistently accurate and consistently available is precisely the configuration under which suppression of internal memory is observed, and a child who knows a hint is one tap away is by that fact alone encoding less — independent of how hard the child is trying.
    - **Link to source:** [Google effects on memory](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745), [Saved information is remembered less well than deleted information](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2021.1962356)

- **Subcategory 4.8: The child's own confidence is generated by the same misleading signal, so self-report cannot correct the measure**
  - **Source:** Koriat & Bjork (2005); Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors state that monitoring of one's own knowledge during study "suffers from an inherent discrepancy between study and test situations: Judgments of learning (JOLs) are made in the presence of information that is absent but solicited during testing." *(Koriat & Bjork, 2005, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 31(2):187–194, p. 187, DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.31.2.187.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that failure to discount the effects of that information when making judgments of learning "can instill a sense of competence during learning that proves unwarranted during testing." *(Koriat & Bjork, 2005, p. 187.)* `T2`
      - Using a paired-associates task, the authors distinguish a priori association strength from a posteriori association strength, and argue that the former has the greater influence on later recall whereas the latter has the greater influence on judgments of learning. *(Koriat & Bjork, 2005, p. 187.)* `T2`
      - The authors define foresight bias as occurring when predictions about one's success in recalling the correct answer are made in the presence of that answer, and distinguish it from hindsight bias by noting that hindsight bias constitutes a memory distortion whereas foresight bias constitutes a metacognitive bias. *(Koriat & Bjork, 2005.)* `T2`
      - Research on learning, memory, and metacognitive processes "has provided evidence that people often have a faulty mental model of how they learn and remember, making them prone to both misassessing and mismanaging their own learning." *(Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013, *Annual Review of Psychology* 64:417–444, p. 417, DOI 10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823.)* `T2`
      - The authors review research on how people's ongoing assessments of their own learning are influenced by current performance and by "the subjective sense of fluency." *(Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013, p. 417.)* `T2`
      - The authors frame the problem as increasingly consequential because both the need and the opportunities for individuals to learn on their own outside of formal classroom settings have grown, with technological advances providing new opportunities for such learning. *(Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013, p. 417.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Koriat and Bjork identify a structural asymmetry between the study situation and the test situation: judgments of learning are made while the answer is present, and the test occurs when it is absent. Learners fail to discount the presence of the answer, which produces a sense of competence that does not survive testing. Their paired-associates work separates the two quantities that diverge — a priori association strength, which predicts later recall, and a posteriori association strength, which predicts the learner's confidence. Because judgments of learning track the wrong one, confidence is systematically miscalibrated toward overestimation, a pattern they name foresight bias and classify as metacognitive rather than a memory distortion. Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell generalize this into a claim about self-regulated learning as a whole: people hold a faulty mental model of how learning works and both misassess and mismanage it, with moment-to-moment assessments driven by current performance and by felt ease of processing. They explicitly frame this as a growing problem because more learning now happens unsupervised and technology-mediated. For an app, this rules out using the child's self-reported confidence as an independent check on the performance signal, because both are generated under the same answer-present conditions — and an interface optimized for smooth, easy interaction is directly amplifying the misleading cue.
    - **Link to source:** [Illusions of competence in monitoring one's knowledge during study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.2.187), [Self-regulated learning: beliefs, techniques, and illusions](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)

### Category 5: What a learning system can actually observe, and what those observables have been shown to mean

*General idea: Every keystroke, pause, hint click, and retry in an educational software system is a candidate signal, but only a small number of interaction-log behaviors have been operationalized precisely, validated against outcomes, and measured for prevalence in real classrooms. Two decades of work in intelligent tutoring systems, educational data mining, and affective computing have produced a small canon of such detectors — gaming the system, wheel-spinning, help abuse and help avoidance, contextual slip, off-task behavior, and sensor-free affect — each built from log features and each carrying a specific, bounded empirical claim. The most important finding for any program that wants to identify children who genuinely improve is that raw effort is not a signal: grinding away at a skill without reaching mastery is common, detectable within about four practice opportunities, and predicts continued failure rather than growth. A second finding is that the sign of a behavior is not fixed — some gaming is not associated with learning loss, confusion improves transfer when it is resolved, and careless errors cluster among the most engaged students rather than the least. A third finding, load-bearing for any selection use, is that these detectors do not reliably survive contact with populations they were not trained on, and the field's own leading reviewers have documented specific demographic biases in educational algorithms.*

- **Subcategory 5.1: Gaming the system — systematic exploitation of feedback and help, and its harmful and harmless varieties**
  - **Source:** Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner (2004); Baker, Corbett, Roll & Koedinger (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Gaming the system was defined as "behavior aimed at obtaining correct answers and advancing within the tutoring curriculum by taking advantage of regularities in the software's responses – systematically misusing the software's feedback or help instead of actively thinking about the material," with two named examples: repeatedly and quickly asking for help until the tutor supplies the answer, and entering answers quickly and systematically. *(Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner, 2004, *Proceedings of CHI 2004*, pp. 383–390, p. 383, DOI 10.1145/985692.985741.)* `T2` `[5 middle-school classrooms, 70 students aged ~12–14, one Cognitive Tutor unit]`
      - Behavior was coded by human observers in 563 observations of 20 seconds each, averaging 8.0 observations per student, using a six-category scheme. *(Baker et al., 2004, pp. 384–385.)* `T2` `[human field observation, not an automated detector]`
      - Students were on-task 82% of observed time; gaming accounted for 3% of observed time, against off-task conversation at 11%, inactivity at 3%, and off-task solitary behavior at 1%. *(Baker et al., 2004, p. 385.)* `T2`
      - 24% of students were observed gaming at least once and 11% were observed gaming more than 10% of the time, while 51% were observed engaging in some other off-task behavior at least once. *(Baker et al., 2004, pp. 385–386.)* `T2`
      - Gaming frequency was the only off-task behavior significantly correlated with post-test score, F(1,68) = 11.82, p < 0.01, r = −0.38, and the relationship held after controlling for pre-test and general academic achievement, partial r = −0.34. *(Baker et al., 2004, p. 385.)* `T2`

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Gaming is the behavioral route by which in-app correctness decouples from learning, defined operationally as systematically exploiting a tutor's help and feedback to advance without thinking. The tutor itself worked, which sharpens the point: inside a system producing real average gains, a subgroup was generating correct answers that carried no learning. The follow-up then split gaming into a harmful variety and a harmless one and found the two behaviorally separable, with prevalence varying sharply by lesson — non-harmful gaming was three times as common as harmful gaming in one lesson and nine times rarer in another. A machine-learned model over 26 per-action log features reached A' = 0.73 under cross-validation and transferred to held-out lessons, and was near chance at picking out low-learning students who did not game, which indicates it detected a behavior rather than low achievement. The authors declined to claim causality or cross-system generality for the harmful/harmless split.

- **Subcategory 5.2: Wheel-spinning — persistence that does not produce mastery**
  - **Source:** Beck & Gong (2013); Gong & Beck (2015); Wan & Beck (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The paper introduced the term "wheel-spinning" for students who do not succeed in mastering a skill in a timely manner, and argued that mastery learning contains an implicit and untested assumption that the student is capable of mastering the skill with the tutor's available teaching actions. *(Beck & Gong, 2013, *Artificial Intelligence in Education (AIED 2013), LNCS* 7926:431–440, p. 431, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_44.)* `T2`
      - Wheel-spinning was operationalized as failing to produce three consecutive correct responses within the first 10 practice opportunities on a skill. *(Beck & Gong, 2013, as restated in Wan & Beck, 2015, *Proceedings of EDM 2015*, pp. 129–135, p. 129, ERIC ED560558.)* `T2`
      - Under that criterion, approximately 31% of student-skill pairs in the Cognitive Tutor Algebra dataset and 38% in the ASSISTments dataset were classified as wheel-spinning. *(Wan & Beck, 2015, p. 129, reporting Beck & Gong 2013.)* `T2` `[two tutoring systems, US middle- and high-school mathematics]`
      - The paper's central empirical claim was that a student who does not master a skill quickly is likely to struggle and will probably never master it, and the authors reported connections between wheel-spinning and gaming the system and disengagement. *(Beck & Gong, 2013, abstract.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up described wheel-spinning as "a common problem affecting a significant number of students in different tutoring systems" and as "negatively associated with learning," and reported that a model built from generic features generalized well to future students across two systems and detected wheel-spinning at an early stage with high accuracy. *(Gong & Beck, 2015, *Proceedings of the Second ACM Conference on Learning @ Scale*, pp. 67–74, abstract, DOI 10.1145/2724660.2724673.)* `T2` `["high accuracy" not quantified in the abstract]`
      - The 15 features covered learning performance (correct response count, prior problem count with hint request), the "seriousness" of the student (prior problem fast correct, prior problem slow incorrect), and general features such as skill identity. *(Zhang, Huang, Wang, Lu, Fang, Stamper, Fancsali, Holstein & Aleven, 2019, *Proceedings of EDM 2019*, p. 2, describing the Gong & Beck feature set, ERIC ED594575.)* `T2`
      - Splitting ASSISTments students into five bins by prerequisite performance, students in the bottom 20% of prerequisite knowledge exhibited wheel-spinning 50% of the time, while students in the top 20% exhibited it 10% of the time. *(Wan & Beck, 2015, abstract and §4.2.)* `T2`
      - Adding the prerequisite-performance feature to the model produced a modest improvement, with R² rising from 0.264 to 0.268 and AUC from 0.884 to 0.888. *(Wan & Beck, 2015, abstract.)* `T2`
      - A path model constructed with non-Western students found that gaming was not causal of wheel-spinning, but rather that wheel-spinning was related to a lack of prior knowledge, which in turn led to gaming. *(Wan & Beck, 2015, p. 129, reporting Beck and Rodrigo.)* `T2` `[reported secondhand; original study used non-Western students]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the primary published basis for treating sustained unproductive effort as a distinct, measurable state rather than as evidence of persistence. Wheel-spinning is defined as failing to reach three consecutive correct answers within ten opportunities, and applied to two large tutoring systems it classified roughly a third of student-skill pairs — a common condition rather than a marginal one. The central finding is directional and negative: failure to master a skill early predicts that the student will probably never master it, so continued practice past that point is not on a trajectory toward mastery. It also co-occurs with gaming and disengagement, tying futile effort to the disengaged-behavior literature. Prerequisite knowledge stratifies it sharply, with the lowest quintile wheel-spinning five times as often as the highest, which gives a mechanistic account of who gets stuck rather than treating it as an unexplained trait. The reported path model reverses the naive causal reading: low prior knowledge produces wheel-spinning, which then produces gaming, rather than gaming producing failure.
    - **Link to source:** [Wheel-spinning: students who fail to master a skill](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_44), [Towards detecting wheel-spinning: future failure in mastery learning](https://doi.org/10.1145/2724660.2724673), [Considering the influence of prerequisite performance on wheel spinning](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED560558.pdf)

- **Subcategory 5.3: Separating productive from unproductive persistence, and the instability of the criterion**
  - **Source:** Kai, Almeda, Baker, C. Heffernan & N. Heffernan (2018); Zhang, Huang, Wang, Lu, Fang, Stamper, Fancsali, Holstein & Aleven (2019); Krumm, Beattie, Takahashi, D'Angelo, Feng & Cheng (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The paper framed its motivation as the finding that "not all persistence is uniformly beneficial for learning," and set out to distinguish productive persistence from wheel-spinning in ASSISTments log data using a decision tree classifier. *(Kai, Almeda, Baker, C. Heffernan & N. Heffernan, 2018, *Journal of Educational Data Mining* 10(1):36–71, abstract, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.3344810.)* `T2` `[ASSISTments skill builders, one online math platform]`
      - The three most predictive features were the minimum number of hints requested on any problem, the maximum number of bottom-out hints requested across any 8 problems, and the standard deviation of time between problems of the same skill. *(Kai et al., 2018, results.)* `T2`
      - Two distinct wheel-spinning profiles emerged: students who do not request any hints in at least one problem but request more than one bottom-out hint across any 8 problems; and students who never request two or more bottom-out hints across any 8 problems, do not request any hints in at least one problem, and engage in relatively short delays between solving problems of the same skill. *(Kai et al., 2018, abstract and results.)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that encouraging spaced practice and sparing use of bottom-out hints is likely helpful for reducing wheel-spinning, and reported more predictive power in the combination of features than in each feature alone. *(Kai et al., 2018, abstract and discussion.)* `T2` `[inference from correlational classification, not an intervention result]`
      - A comparison study evaluated the three-correct-in-a-row criterion against the Predictive Stability++ policy across three datasets totalling more than 560,000 student-knowledge-component pairs. *(Zhang, Huang, Wang, Lu, Fang, Stamper, Fancsali, Holstein & Aleven, 2019, *Proceedings of EDM 2019*, §2, ERIC ED594575.)* `T2` `[three datasets, all US mathematics tutors]`
      - Under the three-correct-in-a-row criterion, wheel-spinning frequency was 6.6%, 0.56%, and 10.2% across the three datasets; under Predictive Stability++, the same datasets gave 24.2%, 2.17%, and 13.2%. *(Zhang et al., 2019, §2.1.1–2.1.2.)* `T2`
      - Among pairs labeled wheel-spinning by either criterion, the two criteria agreed on only 22.2%, 14.1%, and 41.6%; the authors state that "the agreement on wheel spinning is generally less than 50%." *(Zhang et al., 2019, §3.1.1.)* `T2`
      - A Random Forest model at the fourth practice opportunity achieved precision and recall of 77.2% and 63.5% under the three-correct criterion and 90.8% and 81.4% under Predictive Stability++; a logistic regression on the single feature "Correct Response Percentage" reached 93.5% precision and 77.1% recall after just 4 steps. *(Zhang et al., 2019, §3.2.2 and §3.3.1.)* `T2`
      - Almost all models had lower than 20% precision and recall in the first three steps across all datasets, a cold-start limitation the authors attributed to insufficient accumulated features. *(Zhang et al., 2019, §3.3.2.)* `T2`
      - The authors describe the divergence between the two operationalizations as "not desirable from either a practical or a theoretical perspective, as we do not know which one to apply or build on." *(Zhang et al., 2019, §4.)* `T2` `[authors' explicit caveat]`
      - The Carnegie Foundation and its Community College Pathways network define productive persistence as "tenacity plus the use of good strategies." *(Krumm, Beattie, Takahashi, D'Angelo, Feng & Cheng, 2016, *Journal of Learning Analytics* 3(2):116–138, p. 121, DOI 10.18608/jla.2016.32.6.)* `T2` `[community college developmental mathematics]`
      - In that framework the construct is measured primarily through self-report instruments, with behavioral measures from digital learning system data intended to supplement rather than replace them. *(Krumm et al., 2016, abstract and p. 122.)* `T2`
      - The behavioral measures were developed as "practical measures" from improvement science, explicitly distinguished from research measures and accountability measures, and required to be usable by instructors to inform decisions and next steps. *(Krumm et al., 2016, pp. 119–120.)* `T2` `[designed for instructor action, not for selection or ranking]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is where the productive-versus-unproductive persistence distinction is attacked directly in log data, and the finding is that no single feature separates them. The three that mattered were minimum hint count on any problem, maximum bottom-out hint count across an eight-problem window, and the variability of inter-problem time on the same skill, and their combination produced two behaviorally distinct wheel-spinning profiles. The second profile is the one most likely to be mistaken for diligence, since it looks like a student working steadily and rarely asking for answers. The replication study then found that the two leading operationalizations of wheel-spinning disagree on more than half of the cases either flags, with prevalence estimates swinging from 0.56% to 24.2% depending on criterion and dataset — a range spanning nearly two orders of magnitude. Detection is nonetheless feasible and early, reaching 90.8% precision by the fourth opportunity, but all models suffer a severe cold start below 20% precision in the first three steps, so a system cannot label a child as stuck within the first few attempts. - **Subcategory 5.4: Help-seeking — abuse and avoidance are not equally diagnostic**
  - **Source:** Aleven, McLaren, Roll & Koedinger (2006); Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The help-seeking model was implemented as 57 production rules capturing both effective and ineffective help-seeking behavior, applied offline to logs from a Geometry Cognitive Tutor unit. *(Aleven, McLaren, Roll & Koedinger, 2006, *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education* 16(2):101–128, abstract and p. 112.)* `T2` `[one geometry unit, 49 students, 58,732 tracked actions, ~7 hours of tutor use]`
      - The overall help-seeking error rate was 73%, with a second analysis of a different dataset producing 64%. *(Aleven et al., 2006, pp. 113–114.)* `T2`
      - The most frequent metacognitive error category was Help Abuse at 36%, of which the majority was Clicking Through Hints at 33%; the next was Help Avoidance at 19%. *(Aleven et al., 2006, p. 114 and Fig. 4.)* `T2`
      - The overall frequency of help-seeking bugs correlated with post-test performance with pre-test partialed out at partial r = −0.65, t(2,38) = 5.2, p < 0.0005. *(Aleven et al., 2006, p. 115 and Table 3.)* `T2`
      - Broken out by category, Help Abuse correlated with post-test at partial r = −0.48 and Miscellaneous Bugs at partial r = −0.38, both significant, while Help Avoidance (partial r = −0.12) and Try-Step Abuse (partial r = 0.265) were not correlated with learning. *(Aleven et al., 2006, Table 3 and p. 116.)* `T2`
      - Success in help-seeking and success at the cognitive level were highly correlated at r = 0.81, and in a multiple regression only the contribution of the cognitive success rate was significant. *(Aleven et al., 2006, p. 115.)* `T2` `[the two measures are largely confounded in this dataset]`

      - Two classroom studies (N = 58 and N = 67) deployed a Help Tutor as an adjunct agent within a commercial Geometry Cognitive Tutor. *(Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger, 2011, *Learning and Instruction* 21(2):267–280, abstract, DOI 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2010.07.004.)* `T2`

      - The Help Tutor had no effect on students' help-seeking error rate during solution attempts, the category that includes help avoidance, and it marginally increased inappropriate glossary searches. *(Roll et al., 2011, results.)* `T2`
      - Help students asked to see the bottom-out hint on 45% of hint sequences versus 60% for Control in Month 1 (d = 0.64) and 41% versus 53% in Month 3 (d = 0.59), and took longer while reading hints. *(Roll et al., 2011, results.)* `T2`

- **Subcategory 5.5: Carelessness and the slip parameter — errors by students who know the skill**
  - **Source:** Corbett & Anderson (1995); San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo (2011); San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Bayesian Knowledge Tracing models a student's changing knowledge state using four parameters: the initial probability the student knows the skill; the probability of learning it at a given opportunity; the probability of a correct response when the student does not know it (guess); and the probability of an incorrect response when the student does know it (slip). *(Corbett & Anderson, 1995, *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction* 4(4):253–278, DOI 10.1007/BF01099821.)* `T2` `[originally developed for the ACT Programming Tutor; the publisher record dates the volume to 1994 while the field cites 1995]`
      - Under the Cognitive Mastery algorithm used in most Cognitive Tutors, a student is assigned additional problems on skills the system does not yet believe the student has learned, with the threshold typically set at 95% probability. *(Baker, Corbett & Aleven, 2008, *Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS 2008)*, pp. 406–415, p. 407, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-69132-7_44.)* `T2`
      - A careless error was operationalized as a case where a student's answer is wrong despite the fact that he or she knows the answer, using a Contextual Guess-and-Slip variant of Bayesian Knowledge Tracing. *(San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo, 2011, *Artificial Intelligence in Education (AIED 2011), LNCS* 6738:304–311, pp. 304–306, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-21869-9_40.)* `T2`
      - Unlike standard Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, in which slip and guess are fixed per skill, the contextual model estimates slip and guess probability for each individual action based on contextual features including the speed of the action and the student's history of help-seeking. *(San Pedro et al., 2011, p. 306.)* `T2`
      - Data came from 126 students at a large public high school in Quezon City, Philippines, using an 80-minute Cognitive Tutor unit. *(San Pedro et al., 2011, p. 306.)* `T2` `[single lesson, single Philippine school, 80 minutes]`
      - The detectors achieved r = 0.460 and r = 0.481 to the labels and A' = 0.821 and A' = 0.814 at predicting whether the student would answer correctly on the next opportunity. *(San Pedro et al., 2011, pp. 307–308.)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that the detectors are generalizable and can transfer across tutor interface differences and across school settings in different countries. *(San Pedro et al., 2011, p. 310.)* `T2` `[transfer tested only between two versions of the same tutor and two countries]`
      - Carelessness was positively correlated with engaged concentration at r = 0.58, p < 0.001 and r = 0.54, p < 0.001 across two conditions. *(San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo, 2014, *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education* 24(2):189–210, results, DOI 10.1007/s40593-014-0015-y.)* `T2` `[Philippine high school students; affect from field observation]`
      - Carelessness was negatively correlated with confusion at r = −0.38 and r = −0.32, and negatively correlated with boredom at r = −0.35 in one condition. *(San Pedro et al., 2014, results.)* `T2`
      - The authors described the finding as surprising and interpreted it as indicating that "a highly engaged student may paradoxically become overconfident or impulsive, leading to more careless errors." *(San Pedro et al., 2014, abstract.)* `T2` `[authors' interpretation of a correlational finding]`
      - Further analysis over time indicated that confused and bored students had lower learning overall, so their mistakes appeared to stem from a genuine lack of knowledge rather than carelessness. *(San Pedro et al., 2014, abstract.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Bayesian Knowledge Tracing is the substrate on which most behavioral detectors are built, and two of its four parameters — guess and slip — exist precisely to absorb the mismatch between what a student knows and what a student does on any given action. Slip is the formal home of the careless-error construct, and the mastery threshold sitting on top of the model is what defines when practice stops, and therefore what "failing to reach mastery" means operationally in the wheel-spinning literature. Carelessness is measured not as a separate behavior but as a re-parameterization in which the probability that a wrong answer is a slip is estimated per action rather than fixed per skill, driven by action speed and help-seeking history — so the detector reads "wrong but fast, from a student who was not asking for help" as carelessness rather than ignorance. The follow-up produced the finding that most complicates a naive reading of engagement telemetry: careless errors were most frequent among the students who most often displayed engaged concentration, while confusion and boredom went the other way because those students' errors were errors of not knowing. The same log event — a wrong answer on a mastered skill — therefore carries opposite meanings depending on the child's affective state.
    - **Link to source:** [Knowledge tracing: modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821), [Detecting carelessness through contextual estimation of slip probabilities](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-21869-9_40), [Carelessness and affect in an intelligent tutoring system for mathematics](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-014-0015-y)

  - **Source:** Baker (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The model detects when a student is off-task using only log files of system usage, with no screen capture, audio, video, microphones, eye-trackers, or fMRI. *(Baker, 2007, *Proceedings of CHI 2007*, pp. 1059–1068, abstract and conclusions, DOI 10.1145/1240624.1240785.)* `T2` `[five Cognitive Tutor studies conducted 2003–2005]`

Its strongest single feature is not simply long pauses but very fast actions adjacent to very slow ones, precisely because a long pause on its own may indicate careful thought or asking a teacher for help — a useful caution for any system tempted to read latency directly as disengagement. Off-task behavior correlated with disliking computers, disliking mathematics, passive-aggressiveness, and weakly with a lack of educational self-drive. The learning association is unsettled within the same research program, with one study finding no significant correlation and a later meta-analysis finding a significant negative one. - **Subcategory 5.7: Confusion, affect dynamics, and the conditions under which a negative state helps**
  - **Source:** D'Mello & Graesser (2012); D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun & Graesser (2014); Pekrun (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The model predicts that learners in a state of engagement/flow experience cognitive disequilibrium and confusion when they face contradictions, incongruities, anomalies, obstacles to goals, and other impasses; that they revert to the engaged/flow state if equilibrium is restored; and that failure to restore equilibrium triggers frustration, which if unresolved eventually leads to boredom. *(D'Mello & Graesser, 2012, *Learning and Instruction* 22(2):145–157, abstract, DOI 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.10.001.)* `T2`

      - Confusion was experimentally induced in two experiments (N = 64; N = 76) via a contradictory-information manipulation in which animated agents expressed incorrect or contradictory opinions. *(D'Mello et al., 2014, method sections.)* `T2` `[laboratory trialogues on scientific reasoning concepts]`

      - Control-value theory is based on the premise that appraisals of control and value are central to the arousal of achievement emotions, including activity-related emotions such as enjoyment, frustration, and boredom experienced during learning, and outcome emotions such as hope, pride, anxiety, hopelessness, shame, and anger. *(Pekrun, 2006, *Educational Psychology Review* 18(4):315–341, abstract, DOI 10.1007/s10648-006-9029-9.)* `T2`

### Category 6: The dispositional constructs that "hustle" is usually meant to name, and how much of each survives meta-analysis

*General idea: Every construct reached for when someone says "hustler" — grit, growth mindset, deliberate practice, academic tenacity — entered the literature with a large, memorable claim and left meta-analysis substantially smaller. The pattern is consistent enough to be a design constraint rather than a footnote: grit turned out to correlate ρ = .84 with conscientiousness and add incremental R = .000 over it; growth mindset's population correlation with achievement is r = .10 and its intervention effect collapses to d = 0.02 in the highest-quality studies; deliberate practice explains 4–5% of achievement variance in education specifically. The two constructs that held their magnitude are the least glamorous — conscientiousness, a stable trait a program cannot create, and self-regulated learning, which is defined as a trainable process rather than a possessed quality. Cutting across all of them is a measurement problem that matters more for software than for schools: self-report questionnaires converge with each other at about r = .50 but with behavioral tasks at r = .10–.21, and self-reported grit falls when students are placed in demanding environments even as their attendance and test scores rise. A program that observes behavior is therefore not measuring a noisier version of these constructs — it is measuring a different thing, and the literature does not establish that the two agree.*

- **Subcategory 6.1: Grit as first claimed, what survived the meta-analysis, and what its originator later said**
  - **Source:** Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews & Kelly (2007); Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017); Duckworth (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Grit was defined as "perseverance and passion for long-term goals" and accounted for an average of 4% of the variance in success outcomes across six studies. *(Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews & Kelly, 2007, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 92(6):1087–1101, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.92.6.1087.)* `T2`
      - The six samples were educational attainment among two adult samples, GPA among Ivy League undergraduates, retention in two West Point classes, and ranking in the National Spelling Bee. *(Duckworth et al., 2007.)* `T2`
      - In Study 2 (N = 690), grit correlated with Big Five Conscientiousness at r = .77, p < .001 — a stronger relation than with any other Big Five trait. *(Duckworth et al., 2007.)* `T2`
      - In the West Point sample, grit was unrelated to the Whole Candidate Score admissions composite (r = .02, ns) and to SAT score (r = .05, ns). *(Duckworth et al., 2007.)* `T2`
      - Cadets one standard deviation above average in grit were more than 60% more likely to complete summer training (OR = 1.62, p < .001), while the Whole Candidate Score did not predict summer retention. *(Duckworth et al., 2007.)* `T2`
      - Within the same sample, grit was a weaker predictor of cumulative first-year academic GPA (r = .06) than self-control (r = .13) and far weaker than the Whole Candidate Score (r = .64). *(Duckworth et al., 2007.)* `T2`
      - A meta-analysis synthesized 584 effect sizes from 88 independent samples representing 66,807 individuals. *(Credé, Tynan & Harms, 2017, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 113(3):492–511, DOI 10.1037/pspp0000102.)* `T2`
      - Overall grit correlated with overall academic performance at ρ = .18 (k = 39, N = 13,141) and with overall GPA at ρ = .17. *(Credé et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - Overall grit correlated with conscientiousness at ρ = .84 (k = 22, N = 18,826); the perseverance facet at ρ = .83 and the consistency facet at ρ = .61. *(Credé et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - The higher-order structure of grit was not confirmed. *(Credé et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - The perseverance-of-effort facet correlated with academic performance at ρ = .26 while the consistency-of-interest facet correlated at ρ = .10; the facets differed significantly (p < .001) on all four grade criteria. *(Credé et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - In hierarchical regressions on the meta-analytic correlation matrix, entering conscientiousness first, overall grit added incremental R = .000 for overall academic performance, .002 for high school GPA, and .004 for college GPA; perseverance of effort added .040, .085, and .023. *(Credé et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - Grit's relation with cognitive ability was ρ = .05, and with self-control ρ = .72. *(Credé et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that "interventions designed to enhance grit may only have weak effects on performance and success, that the construct validity of grit is in question, and that the primary utility of the grit construct may lie in the perseverance facet." *(Credé et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - The construct's originator later wrote: "I worry I've contributed, inadvertently, to an idea I vigorously oppose: high-stakes character assessment," and that "we shouldn't be rewarding or punishing schools for how students perform on these measures." *(Duckworth, 2016, "Don't grade schools on grit," *New York Times*, March 26, 2016.)* `T1` `[opinion writing, not a peer-reviewed retraction]`
      - Her published guidance states that the Grit Scale "is not appropriate for selecting employees, admitting students to college, gauging the performance of teachers, or comparing schools or countries to each other," and that "you can fake a higher grit score without much effort." *(Duckworth, n.d., Grit Scale limitations note.)* `T1`
      - She reported that in a study she co-authored, "eighth-graders at high-performing charter schools gave themselves lower scores on conscientiousness, self-control and grit than their counterparts at district schools." *(Duckworth, 2016.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The founding grit paper reported that a self-report trait explained roughly 4% of variance in success across six samples, with its strongest result being retention rather than achievement — at West Point a one-standard-deviation advantage raised the odds of surviving summer training by more than 60% while the academy's own admissions composite predicted nothing about who stayed. It also disclosed, in the same sample, that grit was the weakest of three predictors of first-year GPA and correlated .77 with conscientiousness. The meta-analysis across 66,807 people settled the relation to academic performance at ρ = .18 and found the correlation with conscientiousness at ρ = .84, high enough to question whether grit is distinct at all; the proposed higher-order structure was not confirmed, and the two facets behave differently enough to undermine treating them as one. Most consequentially, overall grit added essentially zero incremental prediction over conscientiousness while perseverance alone added a small but real amount. Within a decade the construct's originator publicly opposed any high-stakes use of it, on measurement grounds — the scale can be faked, reference bias confounds the score with the standard a person judges themselves against, and students in demanding schools rate themselves lower.
    - **Link to source:** [Grit: perseverance and passion for long-term goals](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.6.1087), [Much ado about grit: a meta-analytic synthesis of the grit literature](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000102), [Don't grade schools on grit](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/03/27/opinion/sunday/dont-grade-schools-on-grit.html)

- **Subcategory 6.2: Growth mindset as first claimed, and the two meta-analyses that deflated it**
  - **Source:** Blackwell, Trzesniewski & Dweck (2007); Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara (2018); Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a longitudinal study with 373 seventh graders, the belief that intelligence is malleable predicted an upward trajectory in mathematics grades over two years of junior high school, while a belief that intelligence is fixed predicted a flat trajectory. *(Blackwell, Trzesniewski & Dweck, 2007, *Child Development* 78(1):246–263, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.00995.x.)* `T2`
      - In a second study, an intervention teaching an incremental theory to seventh graders (N = 48) promoted positive change in classroom motivation relative to a control group (N = 43); the control group displayed a continuing downward trajectory in grades while this decline was reversed for the experimental group. *(Blackwell et al., 2007.)* `T2` `[intervention arm total sample 91 students]`
      - A meta-analysis (k = 273, N = 365,915) found the average correlation between growth mindset and academic achievement to be r = .10, 95% CI [.08, .13], p < .001. *(Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara, 2018, *Psychological Science* 29(4):549–571, DOI 10.1177/0956797617739704.)* `T2`
      - Of the 273 effect sizes, 157 (58%) were not significantly different from zero, 16 (6%) were significantly negative, and 100 (37%) were significantly positive. *(Sisk et al., 2018.)* `T2`
      - Developmental stage was a significant moderator: r = .19 for children, r = .15 for adolescents, and r = .02 (p = .110) for adults. *(Sisk et al., 2018.)* `T2`
      - A second meta-analysis of interventions (k = 43, N = 57,155) found an average effect of d = 0.08, 95% CI [0.02, 0.14], p = .010; 37 of the 43 effect sizes (86%) were not significantly different from zero. *(Sisk et al., 2018.)* `T2`
      - Against a passive control the intervention effect was d = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.05, 0.10], p = .522; against an active control it was d = 0.08, p = .034. *(Sisk et al., 2018.)* `T2`
      - Socioeconomic status was a significant moderator of intervention effects, Q(1) = 4.76, p = .029: for low-SES households (7 effect sizes) d = 0.34, while for middle- and upper-class students d = 0.03; high-academic-risk students (8 effect sizes) showed d = 0.19, p = .031. *(Sisk et al., 2018.)* `T2`
      - In the same paper, socioeconomic status was *not* a significant moderator of the mindset–achievement correlation, Q(1) = 1.48, p = .223. *(Sisk et al., 2018.)* `T2`
      - A later meta-analysis across 63 studies (N = 97,672) found the overall effect of growth mindset interventions on academic achievement to be d̄ = 0.05, 95% CI [0.02, 0.09], nonsignificant after correcting for potential publication bias. *(Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023, *Psychological Bulletin* 149(3-4):133–173, DOI 10.1037/bul0000352.)* `T2`
      - Authors with a financial incentive to report positive findings published significantly larger effects than authors without this incentive. *(Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023.)* `T2`
      - Restricting to studies that demonstrated the intervention influenced students' mindsets as intended (13 studies, N = 18,355), the effect was nonsignificant at d̄ = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.10]; restricting to the highest-quality evidence (6 studies, N = 13,571), it was d̄ = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.10]. *(Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023.)* `T2`
      - No theoretically meaningful moderators were significant, and the authors concluded that "apparent effects of growth mindset interventions on academic achievement are likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias." *(Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The foundational mindset-and-achievement paper rests on a longitudinal sample of 373 seventh graders and an intervention arm of 91, with the intervention finding being a reversal of decline rather than an absolute gain. Across 365,915 people the correlation between mindset and achievement is r = .10, with the majority of the 273 individual effect sizes not distinguishable from zero; the correlation is larger for children than adolescents and vanishes for adults. The intervention meta-analysis returns d = 0.08 with 86% of effects non-significant, essentially disappearing against passive controls. Two subgroup results run against that null — high-risk students at 0.19 and low-SES students at 0.34 — but both rest on fewer than ten effect sizes, and SES moderated the intervention effect while not moderating the correlation. The quality-weighted meta-analysis then reduces the estimate further to 0.05, which does not survive publication-bias correction, and the two decisive restrictions are the ones the theory needs most: when only studies that actually shifted mindsets are included the effect is 0.04 and not significant, and among the six highest-quality studies it is 0.02 and not significant.
    - **Link to source:** [Implicit theories of intelligence predict achievement across an adolescent transition](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.00995.x), [To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement?](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704), [Do growth mindset interventions impact students' academic achievement?](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000352)

- **Subcategory 6.3: The residual growth-mindset effect — small, targeted at low achievers, and dependent on peer norms**
  - **Source:** Yeager et al. (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Data came from a stratified random sample of 65 regular U.S. public schools including 12,490 ninth-grade adolescents individually randomized to condition; the intervention was two self-administered online sessions of approximately 25 minutes each, roughly 20 days apart. *(Yeager et al., 2019, *Nature* 573(7774):364–369, DOI 10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y.)* `T2`
      - Among the pre-registered target group of lower-achieving students, the intervention raised end-of-ninth-grade core course GPA by B = 0.10 grade points, 95% CI [0.04, 0.16], n = 6,320, P = 0.001, a standardized effect size of 0.11. *(Yeager et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - Higher-achieving students showed no significant treatment effect on core course GPAs, B = 0.01, n = 6,170, P = 0.634, standardized effect size 0.01. *(Yeager et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - Among lower-achieving adolescents the intervention reduced fixed-mindset beliefs by a standardized effect size of 0.33 — three times the size of the GPA effect. *(Yeager et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - Conditional average treatment effects on core GPAs in low- and medium-achieving schools were 0.14 grade points when the school was in the third quartile of behavioural challenge-seeking norms and 0.18 in the fourth and highest quartile. *(Yeager et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - The school-level moderator was a behavioural measure in which students chose between problems described as challenging and instructive or easy and uninstructive; the school norm was the average number of challenging problems chosen by control-condition students at that school. *(Yeager et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - The intervention increased the likelihood of taking advanced mathematics in tenth grade by 3 percentage points, n = 6,690, P = 0.001, from 33% in control to 36% in treatment. *(Yeager et al., 2019.)* `T2`
      - The model estimates a 5.3 percentage-point reduction (P = 0.005) in the rate at which lower-achieving ninth graders end the year with a GPA below 2.0. *(Yeager et al., 2019.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The largest pre-registered, nationally representative test of a mindset intervention found a real effect of 0.10 grade points, and found it only in the lower-achieving half of students, with higher achievers showing essentially nothing. The effect on beliefs was three times the size of the effect on grades, which locates the bottleneck in translation from belief to behavior rather than in belief change itself. The effect was conditional on school context: in low- and medium-achieving schools whose peer norms supported challenge-seeking, the conditional treatment effect rose to 0.14–0.18 grade points. That moderator was measured behaviorally, by what control-group students actually chose to work on in a worksheet task, not by asking them what they believed — which is a direct demonstration that a behavioral measure of challenge-seeking carried information a belief measure did not. The intervention also shifted a downstream behavioral outcome, a 3-percentage-point increase in advanced math enrollment, that a GPA-only analysis would have missed entirely.
    - **Link to source:** [A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y)

- **Subcategory 6.4: Conscientiousness — the unglamorous construct whose magnitude held**
  - **Source:** Poropat (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Conscientiousness correlated with academic performance at an observed r = .19 and a scale-corrected ρ = .22 across k = 138 samples and N = 70,926 participants, equivalent to d = 0.46. *(Poropat, 2009, *Psychological Bulletin* 135(2):322–338, DOI 10.1037/a0014996.)* `T2`
      - By academic level the scale-corrected correlations were ρ = .28 for primary education, ρ = .21 for secondary, and ρ = .23 for tertiary. *(Poropat, 2009.)* `T2`
      - Conscientiousness had a small negative scale-corrected correlation with intelligence (−.03); Openness had the highest correlation with intelligence (.15) but did not have the highest correlation with academic performance. *(Poropat, 2009.)* `T2`
      - Controlling for secondary-level GPA, the partial correlations with tertiary GPA were Conscientiousness r = .17, Agreeableness .05, Openness .03, Emotional Stability .01, and Extraversion .00; the corresponding partial correlation for intelligence was .14. *(Poropat, 2009.)* `T2`
      - The author concluded that Conscientiousness "has the strongest association with academic performance of all the FFM dimensions; its association with academic performance rivaled that of intelligence except in primary education." *(Poropat, 2009.)* `T2`
      - Correlations with every Five-Factor Model measure *except* Conscientiousness declined from primary to later levels. *(Poropat, 2009.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Conscientiousness is the construct in this category that did not shrink under meta-analysis: ρ = .22 across 70,926 people, stable rather than heterogeneous across levels of schooling. Its correlation with intelligence is effectively zero and slightly negative, so it is not a proxy for ability, and in the most rigorous test in the paper — holding prior academic performance constant — it predicted later GPA at r = .17 while intelligence predicted at r = .14. The correlation is largest at the primary level, which is the age band relevant to a children's program, and it is the only Big Five dimension whose relation to achievement did not decay as students got older. The uncomfortable implication for a program is the flip side of that stability: this is the best-validated disposition in the category and it is a trait, not something a curriculum manufactures.

- **Subcategory 6.5: Deliberate practice — substantial in closed domains, near the floor in education**
  - **Source:** Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993); Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, with 2018 corrigendum)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The framework explains expert performance as "the end result of individuals' prolonged efforts to improve performance while negotiating motivational and external constraints," with deliberate practice defined as effortful activities designed to optimize improvement begun in childhood. *(Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, 1993, *Psychological Review* 100(3):363–406, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that "individual differences, even among elite performers, are closely related to assessed amounts of deliberate practice." *(Ericsson et al., 1993.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that "many characteristics once believed to reflect innate talent are actually the result of intense practice extended for a minimum of 10 yrs." *(Ericsson et al., 1993.)* `T2`
      - Deliberate practice explained 12% of the variance in performance overall, 95% CI [9%, 15%], leaving 88% unexplained; a 2018 corrigendum revised this to 14%, 95% CI [11%, 18%]. *(Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014, *Psychological Science* 25(8):1608–1618, DOI 10.1177/0956797614535810; corrigendum 2018, DOI 10.1177/0956797618769891.)* `T2`
      - By domain, deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and less than 1% for professions. *(Macnamara et al., 2014.)* `T2`
      - The 2018 corrigendum revised the education figure from 4% to 5%. *(Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2018, corrigendum.)* `T2`
      - Domain was a statistically significant moderator, Q(4) = 49.09, p < .001, and heterogeneity was high, I² = 84.90. *(Macnamara et al., 2014.)* `T2`
      - Predictability of the task environment was also a significant moderator: 24% of variance explained for highly predictable activities, 12% for moderately predictable, and 4% for activities low in predictability. *(Macnamara et al., 2014.)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that "deliberate practice is important, but not as important as has been argued." *(Macnamara et al., 2014.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The 1993 framework reframed elite performance as an accumulation of structured, effortful practice rather than an expression of talent, and it is the source of the intuition that a program can manufacture ability through effort volume. Its two load-bearing claims are that practice quantity explains individual differences even at the top and that a ten-year minimum of intense practice underlies characteristics previously attributed to innate ability. The meta-analytic test found that practice quantity explains 12–14% of performance variance overall, leaving 86–88% elsewhere. The domain breakdown is the operative finding for an education product: 26% in games and 21% in music but 4–5% in education, a fivefold difference. The mechanism the authors propose for that gap is task predictability, with 24% of variance explained in highly predictable activities against 4% in unpredictable ones — and education sits at the unpredictable end. Heterogeneity was very high, so the point estimates conceal wide variation between studies.
    - **Link to source:** [The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363), [Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810)

- **Subcategory 6.6: Self-regulated learning — the one construct defined as a trainable process rather than a possessed quality**
  - **Source:** Zimmerman (2002); Dignath & Büttner (2008); Donker, de Boer, Kostons, Dignath van Ewijk & van der Werf (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Self-regulation is not a mental ability or an academic performance skill; rather it is the self-directive process by which learners transform their mental abilities into academic skills." *(Zimmerman, 2002, *Theory Into Practice* 41(2):64–70, p. 65, DOI 10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2.)* `T2`
      - The model has three cyclical phases: forethought, performance, and self-reflection; self-reflection comprises self-judgment (self-evaluation and causal attribution) and self-reaction (self-satisfaction and adaptive or defensive responses). *(Zimmerman, 2002.)* `T2`
      - Attributing a poor score to limitations in fixed ability "can be very damaging motivationally because it implies that efforts to improve on a future test will not be effective," whereas attributing it to a controllable process such as the wrong solution strategy "will sustain motivation." *(Zimmerman, 2002.)* `T2`
      - Novices "fail to engage in high-quality forethought and instead attempt to self-regulate their learning reactively"; they fail to set specific goals or self-monitor systematically and consequently rely on comparisons with the performance of others to judge their learning effectiveness. *(Zimmerman, 2002.)* `T2`
      - Defensive reactions are "efforts to protect one's self-image by withdrawing or avoiding opportunities to learn and perform," and adaptive reactions are "adjustments designed to increase the effectiveness of one's method of learning, such as discarding or modifying an ineffective learning strategy." *(Zimmerman, 2002.)* `T2`
      - Two meta-analyses covering 49 studies with primary school students and 35 with secondary school students analyzed 357 effect sizes; the average effect size of self-regulated learning interventions was 0.69. *(Dignath & Büttner, 2008, *Metacognition and Learning* 3(3):231–264, DOI 10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x.)* `T2`
      - For both school levels, effect sizes were higher when the training was conducted by researchers instead of by regular teachers. *(Dignath & Büttner, 2008.)* `T2`
      - Interventions attained higher effects in mathematics than in reading/writing or other subjects, and the theoretical background and instructed strategy type led to differential effects at the two school levels. *(Dignath & Büttner, 2008.)* `T2`
      - Restricted to academic performance, those results were "Cohen's d = 0.61 (0.05) for primary schools and 0.54 (0.11) for secondary schools." *(Donker, de Boer, Kostons, Dignath van Ewijk & van der Werf, 2014, *Educational Research Review* 11:1–26, DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2013.11.002.)* `T2`
      - A separate meta-analysis of 58 studies comprising 95 interventions and 180 effect sizes found effects substantial in writing (Hedges' g = 1.25), science (.73), mathematics (.66), and comprehensive reading (.36), with metacognitive knowledge instruction "valuable in all" domains. *(Donker et al., 2014.)* `T2`
      - "The effects were higher when self-developed tests were used than in the case of intervention-independent tests." *(Donker et al., 2014.)* `T2`
      - "No differential effects were observed for students with different ability levels." *(Donker et al., 2014.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Self-regulation is defined explicitly as a process rather than a trait or ability, which distinguishes it from every other construct in this category and makes it the only one a program can straightforwardly set out to teach. The three-phase cycle specifies observable actions rather than a self-reported disposition, and the expert/novice contrast is directly instrumentable: novices set no specific goals, do not self-monitor systematically, regulate reactively, and judge progress by comparison to others rather than to a standard or their own prior performance. The adaptive-versus-defensive branch after failure — modify the strategy or withdraw from the opportunity — is an observable decision point in any learning session. Interventions produce an average effect of 0.69 overall and roughly 0.54–0.61 on academic performance specifically, an order of magnitude larger than growth mindset interventions in the same settings, and the payoff varies by domain from 1.25 in writing down to .36 in reading comprehension. Two findings temper the headline: effects were larger when researchers rather than regular teachers ran the training, and larger when the outcome test was developed by the researchers than when it was independent of the intervention.
    - **Link to source:** [Becoming a self-regulated learner: an overview](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2), [Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x), [Effectiveness of learning strategy instruction on academic performance](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2013.11.002)

- **Subcategory 6.7: "Academic tenacity" and "productive persistence" — the umbrella terms and their evidence**
  - **Source:** Dweck, Walton & Cohen (2014); Silva & White (2013); Yamada & Bryk (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The report states: "At its most basic level, academic tenacity is about working hard (and working smart) for a long time." *(Dweck, Walton & Cohen, 2014, *Academic Tenacity: Mindsets and Skills that Promote Long-Term Learning*, Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, ERIC ED576649.)* `T1` `[foundation-commissioned white paper, not peer-reviewed]`
      - Academic tenacity is defined as the mindsets and skills that allow students "to look beyond short-term concerns to longer-term or higher-order goals" and "to withstand challenges and setbacks to persevere toward these goals." *(Dweck, Walton & Cohen, 2014.)* `T1`
      - The report states that "educational interventions and initiatives that target these psychological factors can transform students' experience and achievement in school, improving core academic outcomes such as GPA and test scores months and even years later." *(Dweck, Walton & Cohen, 2014.)* `T1`
      - A parallel program defines productive persistence as "the package of skills and tenacity that students need to succeed in an academic setting" and as "an evidence-based package of practical student activities and faculty actions integrated throughout the instructional system." *(Silva & White, 2013, *Pathways to Improvement*, Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching, ERIC ED560149; Carnegie Foundation program description.)* `T1`
      - Baseline data from network institutions showed that only 5.9% of developmental mathematics students received credit for college-level mathematics in one year, rising to 23.5% after four years; after a full year of the program, 51% of students had successfully completed the full pathway with a grade of C or better. *(Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching, program results announcement.)* `T1` `[before/after contrast, not a counterfactual]`
      - The change package includes formalizing team members' roles during group work, committing to a healthy learning environment via a contract, and completing a reading about the malleability of the brain. *(Carnegie Foundation, Statway propensity score matching report.)* `T1`
      - A peer-reviewed evaluation applied a multilevel model with propensity score matching on 44 student characteristics across 17 community colleges in five states, with 928 treatment and 4,549 comparison students in the first cohort. *(Yamada & Bryk, 2016, *Community College Review* 44(3):179–204, DOI 10.1177/0091552116643162.)* `T2`
      - The authors found "large effects of Statway on students attaining college math credit with persisting effects into the following year," with program students accumulating 5.57 college credits one year after completion versus 4.08 for comparison students. *(Yamada & Bryk, 2016.)* `T2`
      - Improved outcomes "emerged for each gender and race/ethnicity groups and for students with different math placement levels." *(Yamada & Bryk, 2016.)* `T2`
      - The design was nonexperimental: students were not randomly assigned. *(Yamada & Bryk, 2016.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** "Academic tenacity" is the umbrella label for the whole family of non-cognitive factors, and it is defined almost exactly the way an informal notion of a hustler would be — working hard and working smart over a long horizon, holding to longer-term goals, and persisting through setbacks. Its two components, a time-horizon component and a setback-resilience component, are a usable decomposition for behavioral instrumentation. The report's causal claim is strong and it is a foundation white paper published before the meta-analyses that deflated its two main constituent constructs. Its operationalized cousin, productive persistence, is a defined package of student activities and faculty actions rather than a trait, with a dramatic headline comparison of 51% program completion against a 5.9% one-year baseline. The package is a bundle — group-work role structures, a commitment contract, and a brain-malleability reading among others — so the mindset component cannot be isolated from the structural redesign. The peer-reviewed evaluation using propensity matching on 44 characteristics reports a more modest and more credible figure of 5.57 against 4.08 college credits, holding across gender, race/ethnicity, and placement level, from a design in which assignment was not random.
    - **Link to source:** [Academic tenacity: mindsets and skills that promote long-term learning](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED576649), [Pathways to improvement: using psychological strategies to help college students master developmental math](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED560149), [Assessing the first two years' effectiveness of Statway](https://doi.org/10.1177/0091552116643162)

- **Subcategory 6.8: The self-report measurement problem — why these constructs behave differently when behavior is observed instead of asked about**
  - **Source:** Duckworth & Kern (2011); West, Kraft, Finn, Martin, Duckworth, Gabrieli & Gabrieli (2016); Duckworth & Yeager (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across k = 282 samples and N = 33,564 participants, self-control measures demonstrated moderate convergence overall: r = .27, 95% CI [.24, .30]. *(Duckworth & Kern, 2011, *Journal of Research in Personality* 45(3):259–268, DOI 10.1016/j.jrp.2011.02.004.)* `T2`
      - Correlations among informant-report questionnaires (r = .54) were slightly higher than among self-report questionnaires (r = .50) and much higher than among executive function tasks (r = .15) or delay-of-gratification tasks (r = .21). *(Duckworth & Kern, 2011.)* `T2`
      - Delay tasks correlated with self-report questionnaires at r = .15, and executive function tasks at r = .10. *(Duckworth & Kern, 2011.)* `T2`
      - Over half of the heterogeneity in correlations was explained by the type of self-control measure used; correlations did not vary systematically by gender, sample type, publication year, or publication status. *(Duckworth & Kern, 2011.)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that "self-control is a coherent but multidimensional construct best assessed using multiple methods." *(Duckworth & Kern, 2011.)* `T2`
      - Self-report surveys from 1,368 eighth graders showed that at the student level, scales measuring conscientiousness, self-control, grit, and growth mindset were positively correlated with attendance, behavior, and test-score gains between fourth and eighth grade. *(West, Kraft, Finn, Martin, Duckworth, Gabrieli & Gabrieli, 2016, *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis* 38(1):148–170, DOI 10.3102/0162373715597298.)* `T2`
      - Conscientiousness, self-control, and grit were unrelated to test-score gains at the school level. *(West et al., 2016.)* `T2`
      - Students at over-subscribed charter schools scored 0.23 standard deviations lower on self-control than district-school students, and lower on conscientiousness and grit, despite making larger test-score gains. *(West et al., 2016.)* `T2`
      - Using admissions-lottery-based estimates, one year's attendance at an over-subscribed charter school produced effects of −0.211 (p < .05) on self-control, −0.119 (p < .05) on grit, −0.157 (p < .1) on conscientiousness, and −0.030 (ns) on growth mindset. *(West et al., 2016.)* `T2`
      - Over the same period there was no effect of charter attendance on suspensions, and absences fell by 1.42 days (p < .05) per year of attendance. *(West et al., 2016.)* `T2`
      - The authors wrote: "If over-subscribed charter schools were, in fact, decreasing students' conscientiousness, self-control, and grit, we would expect corresponding increases in suspensions and absences rather than the null and negative effects we observe," and attribute the pattern to reference bias. *(West et al., 2016.)* `T2`
      - "We do not believe any available measure is suitable for between-school accountability judgments." *(Duckworth & Yeager, 2015, *Educational Researcher* 44(4):237–251, DOI 10.3102/0013189X15584327.)* `T2`
      - Self-report and teacher-report questionnaire measures "may potentially produce the opposite finding of the truth if used for between-school or within-school over-time comparisons," and "existing questionnaire and performance task measures are rarely sufficiently reliable to use for diagnosis and may produce group biases." *(Duckworth & Yeager, 2015.)* `T2`
      - On behavioral measurement: "An advantage of performance tasks is that they do not rely upon the subjective judgments of students or teachers. This feature circumvents reference bias, social desirability bias, acquiescence bias, and faking. Relatedly, by assaying behavior at a moment in time, task measures could be more sensitive than questionnaires to subtle changes in behavior." *(Duckworth & Yeager, 2015.)* `T2`
      - The countervailing limitation: "a one-time performance task may be appropriate for assessing the capacity of a student to perform a certain behavior when maximally motivated to do so but not particularly diagnostic of their everyday behavior in typical life situations," and performance tasks are subject to practice effects and sensitive to administration conditions. *(Duckworth & Yeager, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Among promising future approaches the authors list "opportunistically mining students' online learning behavior or written communication in real time." *(Duckworth & Yeager, 2015.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Across 282 multi-method samples, different measures of self-control agree with each other at r = .27, and the disagreement is systematic rather than noisy: questionnaires converge with questionnaires at about .50, while behavioral tasks converge with questionnaires at .10 to .15, meaning the two families share on the order of 1–2% of their variance. The sharpest demonstration of what that implies comes from lottery-identified charter school estimates: within students, self-reported grit and conscientiousness correlate positively with attendance and test-score gains exactly as theory predicts, but a year at a high-performing school *lowered* self-reported self-control by 0.21 standard deviations and grit by 0.12 while absences fell by nearly a day and a half and suspensions were unchanged — so the self-reports moved rather than the students. The proposed mechanism is reference bias, where a demanding environment raises the internal standard students grade themselves against. Duckworth and Yeager lay out the trade-off without declaring a winner: behavioral tasks circumvent reference bias, social desirability, acquiescence, and faking and may be more sensitive to subtle change, but a one-time task measures maximal capacity rather than typical behavior and carries practice effects. Their own suggested remedy is repeated, naturalistic observation of learning behavior over time.
    - **Link to source:** [A meta-analysis of the convergent validity of self-control measures](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2011.02.004), [Promise and paradox: measuring students' non-cognitive skills and the impact of schooling](https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373715597298), [Measurement matters: assessing personal qualities other than cognitive ability for educational purposes](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X15584327)

### Category 7: What adaptive and machine-delivered instruction has actually been measured to deliver

*General idea: The commercial story about adaptive tutoring rests on a benchmark — Bloom's two sigma — that was produced by two unpublished 1980s dissertations running eleven class periods over three weeks, and that no subsequent meta-analysis has reproduced. What the measured record shows is far more modest and far more conditional: roughly 0.4 to 0.8 standard deviations on tests aligned to what the system taught, collapsing toward zero on standardized tests, and weaker in K-12 than in postsecondary settings. The meta-analyses do not agree with one another, and their disagreement is not noise — a median of 0.66 and a K-12 range of 0.01 to 0.09 describe the same technology under different inclusion rules and outcome measures. Evidence that adaptivity itself is the active ingredient exists but is thinner than the headline effect sizes imply, and the one review that isolates specific forms of adaptivity finds strong support for adapting to what a learner knows and essentially none for adapting to a learner's diagnosed style. Every quantity below was measured on domain achievement, not on domain-general reasoning, and that absence is itself one of this category's findings.*

- **Subcategory 7.1: The human-versus-machine tutoring gap is far smaller than the folklore, and interaction granularity plateaus**
  - **Source:** VanLehn (2011); Kulik & Fletcher (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The review states the widely held prior belief that, compared to no tutoring, effect sizes for answer-based tutoring systems, intelligent tutoring systems, and adult human tutors are d = 0.3, 1.0, and 2.0 respectively, and reports that the review "did not confirm these beliefs." *(VanLehn, 2011, *Educational Psychologist* 46(4):197–221, p. 197, DOI 10.1080/00461520.2011.611369.)* `T2`
      - The measured effect size of human tutoring versus no tutoring was d = 0.79, and the measured effect size of intelligent tutoring systems was d = 0.76. *(VanLehn, 2011, p. 197.)* `T2`
      - Computer tutoring systems were classified by granularity of user-interface interaction into answer-based, step-based, and substep-based systems; most intelligent tutoring systems are step-based or substep-based, while most systems called CAI, CBT, or CAL are answer-based. *(VanLehn, 2011, p. 197.)* `T2`
      - An independent re-analysis reports that the median effect of human tutoring across the 10 studies in VanLehn's human-tutoring set was 0.79 standard deviations, and that excluding one study the median falls to 0.68. *(Kulik & Fletcher, 2016, *Review of Educational Research* 86(1):42–78, DOI 10.3102/0034654315581420.)* `T2` `[k = 10 studies; single-study removal shifts the median by 0.11]`
      - The same re-analysis reports step-based tutoring raising test scores by 0.76 standard deviations whereas substep-based tutoring raised them by only 0.40. *(Kulik & Fletcher, 2016.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** VanLehn set out to test a specific belief structure — that tutoring gets more effective as the interaction gets more fine-grained, with human tutors at two sigma — and the data did not support it. Human tutoring came in at 0.79 rather than 2.0, and step-based machine tutoring at 0.76, close enough that the human-machine gap effectively disappears. The one place granularity mattered was the jump from answer-based to step-based systems; below the step level, effectiveness plateaued and substep tutoring measured lower than step-based. The classification matters for product claims, because most consumer adaptive-practice software is answer-based, which is the tier that measured around 0.31 rather than the tier that measured 0.76. The independent reproduction also exposes how fragile the human-tutoring benchmark is: removing a single study from a ten-study set drops the median from 0.79 to 0.68, and that single-study sensitivity is the load-bearing weakness in every downstream comparison of AI tutors against human tutors.
    - **Link to source:** [The relative effectiveness of human tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and other tutoring systems](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369), [Effectiveness of intelligent tutoring systems: a meta-analytic review](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315581420)

- **Subcategory 7.2: The four major tutoring-system meta-analyses disagree, and the K-12 result is the weakest**
  - **Source:** Kulik & Fletcher (2016); Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper (2013); Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper (2014); Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 50 controlled evaluations, the median effect was to raise test scores 0.66 standard deviations over conventional levels, or from the 50th to the 75th percentile. *(Kulik & Fletcher, 2016, *Review of Educational Research* 86(1):42–78, p. 42, DOI 10.3102/0034654315581420.)* `T2`
      - Test type was the study feature most strongly related to effect size (r = −.63): local tests, N = 38, M = 0.73; combination of local and standardized, N = 3, M = 0.45; standardized tests, N = 9, M = 0.13. *(Kulik & Fletcher, 2016, Table 3.)* `T2`
      - Effect sizes were 0.44 for elementary and high school evaluations and 0.75 for postsecondary; 0.40 for mathematics and 0.72 for other subjects; 0.78 for samples up to 80 participants and 0.53 for samples of 81 to 250. *(Kulik & Fletcher, 2016, Table 3.)* `T2`
      - Across 15 Cognitive Tutor studies, mean effect size was 0.76 on locally developed tests and 0.12 on standardized tests. *(Kulik & Fletcher, 2016, Table 4.)* `T2`
      - Across 26 reports containing 34 independent samples published 1997–2010, intelligent tutoring systems had "no negative and perhaps a small positive effect" on K-12 students' mathematical learning, with average effect sizes ranging from g = 0.01 to g = 0.09. *(Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2013, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 105(4):970–987, p. 970, DOI 10.1037/a0032447.)* `T2`
      - Under a fixed-effect model the average effect size was 0.05, 95% CI [.02, .09], p < .005; under a random-effects model it was 0.09, 95% CI [−.03, .20], p = .136, not significantly different from zero. *(Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2013.)* `T2`
      - Of the 26 unadjusted overall effect sizes, 17 were positive, 8 were negative, and 1 was exactly 0. *(Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2013.)* `T2`
      - Effects were greater when interventions lasted less than a school year than one school year or longer, and greater for students drawn from the general population than for low achievers. *(Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2013, p. 970.)* `T2`
      - Across 35 reports containing 39 studies in higher education, intelligent tutoring systems had a moderate positive effect on college students' academic learning, g = .32 to g = .37. *(Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(2):331–347, p. 331, DOI 10.1037/a0034752.)* `T2` `[college students only]`
      - In that analysis such systems "were less effective than human tutoring but outperformed all other instruction methods and learning activities," and effectiveness in earlier studies was significantly greater than in more recent studies. *(Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2014, p. 331.)* `T2`
      - A separate meta-analysis extracted 107 effect sizes involving 14,321 participants; the overall random-effects weighted mean effect size was g = .41. *(Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(4):901–918, p. 901, DOI 10.1037/a0037123.)* `T2`
      - Use was associated with greater achievement than teacher-led large-group instruction (g = .42), non-ITS computer-based instruction (g = .57), and textbooks or workbooks (g = .35), with no significant difference from individualized human tutoring (g = −.11) or small-group instruction (g = .05). *(Ma et al., 2014, p. 901.)* `T2`
      - Significant positive effect sizes were found at all levels of education and in almost all subject domains, whether or not the system provided feedback or modeled student misconceptions, and 18 further moderator variables produced no substantive significant differences. *(Ma et al., 2014, p. 901.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Four meta-analyses of the same technology return 0.66, 0.01–0.09, 0.32–0.37, and 0.41, and the spread is structured rather than random. The dominant moderator is what the outcome test measures: when the test was written locally to match what the tutor taught the effect averaged 0.73, and when it was a commercial or state test it averaged 0.13, with the same split appearing inside a single product line at 0.76 against 0.12. Two further moderators cut against a children's product, since K-12 evaluations averaged 0.44 against 0.75 for postsecondary and mathematics averaged 0.40 against 0.72 for everything else. The K-12-specific analysis is the weakest of the four and the most directly relevant, with a random-effects estimate not statistically distinguishable from zero, eight of twenty-six effect sizes pointing the wrong way, and two moderators hostile to a long-running adaptive program — effects shrank as interventions ran longer and were smaller for low achievers than for the general population. The most awkward finding for a product decision is that effects appeared regardless of whether the system provided feedback or modeled misconceptions, the two features usually claimed as the mechanism.
    - **Link to source:** [Effectiveness of intelligent tutoring systems: a meta-analytic review](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315581420), [Intelligent tutoring systems and K–12 students' mathematical learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032447), [Intelligent tutoring systems and college students' academic learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034752), [Intelligent tutoring systems and learning outcomes: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037123)

- **Subcategory 7.3: Bloom's two sigma — what was actually claimed, on what evidence, and why it has never been reproduced**
  - **Source:** Bloom (1984); Cohen, Kulik & Kulik (1982); Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (2024); von Hippel (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The two-sigma claim rests on dissertations by two doctoral students comparing conventional classes of about 30 students, mastery learning with the same class size plus formative tests and corrective procedures, and tutoring with one tutor per one to three students followed by the same formative-test cycle. *(Bloom, 1984, *Educational Researcher* 13(6):4–16, p. 4, DOI 10.3102/0013189X013006004.)* `T2`
      - The studies were replicated with four samples at grades four, five, and eight and two subject matters, and in each sub-study the instructional treatment was limited to 11 periods of instruction over a 3-week block. *(Bloom, 1984, p. 4.)* `T2` `[3-week intervention; two narrow topics; no long-run or standardized outcome]`
      - "The average student under tutoring was about two standard deviations above the average of the control class," and "the average student under mastery learning was about one standard deviation above the average of the control class." *(Bloom, 1984, p. 4.)* `T2`
      - Student time on task was 65% under conventional instruction, 75% under mastery learning, and 90%+ under tutoring. *(Bloom, 1984, p. 4.)* `T2`
      - Aptitude–achievement correlations typically changed from +.60 under conventional instruction to +.85 under mastery learning and +.25 under tutoring. *(Bloom, 1984, p. 4.)* `T2`
      - Bloom's own Table 1 lists "Tutorial instruction" at 2.00 sigma and "Peer and cross-age remedial tutoring" at .40 sigma as separate entries. *(Bloom, 1984, Table 1.)* `T2`
      - Across 65 independent evaluations of school tutoring programs, the average effect size in the 52 reporting achievement results was .40 (SE .069), meaning 66% of tutored pupils outperformed the average non-tutored pupil. *(Cohen, Kulik & Kulik, 1982, *American Educational Research Journal* 19(2):237–248, DOI 10.3102/00028312019002237.)* `T2`
      - Effects were .84 on the 12 studies using instructor-developed tests and .27 on the 40 studies using commercial standardized tests, and the 6 studies with treatments of 0 to 4 weeks averaged .95. *(Cohen, Kulik & Kulik, 1982, Table I.)* `T2`
      - A systematic review and meta-analysis of preK-12 tutoring field experiments found an overall pooled effect size of 0.288 SD (SE = 0.029, p < .001). *(Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan, 2024, *American Educational Research Journal* 61(1):74–107, p. 74, DOI 10.3102/00028312231208687.)* `T2` `[randomized experiments only]`
      - The earlier working-paper version of the same review reported an overall pooled effect size estimate of 0.37 SD. *(Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan, 2020, NBER Working Paper w27476, DOI 10.3386/w27476.)* `T1` `[working paper; the published estimate is a downward revision of about 0.08 SD]`
      - Bloom claimed to have adapted his estimates from a paper published the prior month, but that paper's corresponding tutoring estimate was 0.4 standard deviations, and Bloom relabeled that figure as the effect of "peer and cross-age remedial tutoring" while entering 2.00 for "tutorial instruction." *(von Hippel, 2024, *Education Next* 24(2):22–31.)* `T1` `[practitioner magazine, not peer-reviewed; the sourcing claim is checkable against Bloom 1984 Table 1 and Walberg 1984]`
      - In one of the underlying dissertations, tutored students scored 11 percentage points higher on the retest than the initial test in week one, 20 points higher in week two, and 30 points higher in week three; the author's analysis indicates roughly half of the documented gains among tutored students likely derived from the additional quizzes and feedback, which were also used with mastery learning. *(von Hippel, 2024, Figure 2.)* `T1` `[secondary reanalysis of an unpublished dissertation]`
      - Among the 65 tutoring studies reviewed in 1982, only one reported a two-sigma effect; among the 96 tutoring studies in the modern review, none produced a two-sigma effect. *(von Hippel, 2024.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Bloom's evidence base is two doctoral dissertations, four samples, two unfamiliar topics, and eleven class periods over three weeks. The tutoring condition was not tutoring alone but one-to-one instruction bundled with a mastery cycle of formative testing, corrective feedback, and retesting, and Bloom's own mastery-learning-without-tutoring condition already produced about one of that two sigma. Time on task also differed substantially across conditions, and his own table separately lists ordinary peer tutoring at 0.40. A meta-analysis published two years *before* Bloom's essay had already put the average tutoring effect at 0.40, with a local-versus-standardized split of 0.84 against 0.27 that anticipates the identical split later found for tutoring systems, and with three-week interventions averaging 0.95 — so a short intervention on a novel topic is exactly the design that produces the largest measured numbers. The best modern randomized benchmark is 0.288 SD. The reanalysis tracing Bloom's sourcing is a practitioner-magazine piece rather than peer-reviewed work, but its factual claims are checkable, and it attributes roughly half the tutoring gain to the extra quiz-and-feedback cycle — which is the testing effect, not adaptivity.
    - **Link to source:** [The 2 sigma problem](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X013006004), [Educational outcomes of tutoring: a meta-analysis of findings](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312019002237), [The promise of tutoring for preK–12 learning](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312231208687), [Two-sigma tutoring: separating science fiction from science fact](https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/)

- **Subcategory 7.4: Does adaptivity itself add value, and which kinds of adaptivity have evidence?**
  - **Source:** Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu (2014); Wang, Huang, Sommer, Pei, Shidfar, Rehman, Ritzhaupt & Martin (2024); Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The comparison isolating the adaptive component — intelligent tutoring versus non-adaptive computer-based instruction — produced the largest single comparison effect in the meta-analysis, g = .57, larger than the advantage over teacher-led large-group instruction (g = .42) or over textbooks and workbooks (g = .35). *(Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(4):901–918, p. 901, DOI 10.1037/a0037123.)* `T2` `[a between-study moderator contrast, not a within-experiment manipulation]`
      - The authors note that a second-order meta-analysis found an effect size of .31 for computer-based instruction as the primary means of instruction compared with traditional classroom teaching. *(Ma et al., 2014.)* `T2`
      - A meta-analysis searching 18 databases identified 45 independent studies of AI-enabled adaptive learning; compared with non-adaptive learning interventions, the overall effect on students' cognitive learning outcomes was g = 0.70 (k = 47). *(Wang, Huang, Sommer, Pei, Shidfar, Rehman, Ritzhaupt & Martin, 2024, *Journal of Educational Computing Research* 62(6), DOI 10.1177/07356331241240459.)* `T2`
      - The effect was significantly moderated by publication type, origin of study, student classification level, student discipline, duration, and research design. *(Wang et al., 2024.)* `T2`
      - The type of AI used in the adaptive engine did not moderate the effects. *(Wang et al., 2024.)* `T2`
      - A review organizes adaptivity into a 3×5 grid crossing three timescales (design-loop, task-loop, step-loop) with five psychological realms (knowledge, path through an activity, affect/motivation, self-regulation, learning styles), reporting evidence of effectiveness of varying strength for 13 of the 15 cells. *(Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger, 2017, in Mayer & Alexander (Eds.), *Handbook of Research on Learning and Instruction*, 2nd ed., pp. 522–560, Routledge.)* `T2` `[edited handbook chapter; narrative review with no pooled effect size]`
      - Regarding the task loop, the evidence is described as strong that mastery learning approaches implemented in technology can be practical and effective in actual classrooms, with cognitive mastery task selection substantially improving effectiveness and efficiency compared with giving all students the same problem set. *(Aleven et al., 2017.)* `T2`
      - Evidence that adapting to learners' preferred style of learning makes instruction more effective is characterized as weak, with a review focused on computer-assisted instruction concluding that if aptitude–treatment interactions with cognitive or learning styles exist "they seem to be infrequent and small in magnitude," with only 9 of 65 contrasts (14%) yielding a statistically significant interaction. *(Aleven et al., 2017, citing Cook 2012.)* `T2`
      - A 2025 edition of the chapter reports empirical support for 12 of 15 forms of adaptivity and identifies the strongest return relative to implementation effort as step-loop adaptation to student strategies and errors, design-loop adaptivity generally, and task-loop adaptations to student knowledge. *(Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger, 2025, "Instruction based on adaptive learning technologies," DOI 10.4324/9781003484776-26.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The comparison that best isolates adaptivity holds the delivery medium constant and varies only whether the system models the learner, and it produced g = .57 — larger than the effect of computers over teachers, and larger than the .31 that plain computer-based instruction earns over classroom teaching. The most recent meta-analysis whose comparison condition is explicitly non-adaptive instruction reports 0.70, higher than any of the older estimates, but with a null moderator that constrains any product claim: the type of AI in the adaptive engine did not change the effect, which is direct evidence against the proposition that a more sophisticated model produces more learning. The only source that reviews experiments isolating *specific forms* of adaptivity reaches the most useful verdict, which is not "adaptivity works" but "some forms work and one specific form does not." Adapting task selection to a model of what the learner knows has strong support; adapting to a learner's diagnosed style has essentially none, with only 14% of contrasts producing a significant interaction. That distinction matters directly for a tool that adapts to individually diagnosed weaknesses: if the diagnosis is a knowledge state, the evidence supports it; if it is a stable trait or style, the evidence does not.
    - **Link to source:** [Intelligent tutoring systems and learning outcomes: a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037123), [Efficacy of AI-enabled adaptive learning systems](https://doi.org/10.1177/07356331241240459), [Instruction based on adaptive learning technologies](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~aleven/Papers/2016/Aleven_etal_Handbook2017_AdaptiveLearningTechnologies.pdf)

- **Subcategory 7.5: Learner control versus system control — learners choose badly, and predictably**
  - **Source:** Kirschner & van Merriënboer (2013); Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger (2017), reporting Atkinson (1972)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The article identifies three "urban legends" variations on the theme "that it is the learner who knows best and that she or he should be the controlling force in her or his learning"; the third is that learners are self-educators who should be given maximum control over what they learn and their learning trajectory. *(Kirschner & van Merriënboer, 2013, *Educational Psychologist* 48(3):169–183, p. 169, DOI 10.1080/00461520.2013.804395.)* `T2`
      - The authors place the claim that giving learners full control will have positive effects at the end of the evidence continuum for which "there is a tiny bit of incomplete support — but the legend itself is false or at least a severe overgeneralization." *(Kirschner & van Merriënboer, 2013.)* `T2`
      - Citing a meta-analysis of studies using learner preference for selecting instructional methods, they report that learner preference was typically uncorrelated or negatively correlated with learning outcomes, such that learners who preferred a technique typically did not derive instructional benefit from experiencing it. *(Kirschner & van Merriënboer, 2013, citing Clark 1982.)* `T2`
      - Giving low general-ability students full learner control created a situation in which they continued to practice tasks they liked or were already proficient in and were reluctant to start new, unfamiliar tasks; an electronic development portfolio helped them make better task selections, but only when its use was combined with regular coaching meetings. *(Kirschner & van Merriënboer, 2013, citing Kicken et al. 2009.)* `T2`
      - The authors' recommendation is "to appropriately limit students' control in consideration of their" competence, rather than to eliminate or maximize it. *(Kirschner & van Merriënboer, 2013.)* `T2`
      - Of four task-selection approaches evaluated for learning German vocabulary, the two that used student response history for automated item selection showed the greatest gain on a delayed posttest — more than both the learner-control and the random-selection conditions. *(Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger, 2017, in *Handbook of Research on Learning and Instruction*, 2nd ed., citing Atkinson 1972, *Journal of Experimental Psychology* 96(1):124–129, DOI 10.1037/h0033475.)* `T2` `[adult learners; single vocabulary domain; result is over 50 years old]`
      - Studies of adapting to student self-regulation in a system's task loop can be effective "especially when an open learner model [is combined] with additional support for self-assessment and learner-controlled task selection." *(Aleven et al., 2017.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The specific failure mode documented here is precisely the one an adaptive reasoning product must design against: given free choice, low-ability learners practice what they are already good at and avoid what they are weak at, which is the exact inverse of the intended behavior of a tool that targets a child's weakest areas. The preference meta-analysis adds that preference is not merely uninformative but sometimes negatively related to benefit, so a system optimizing for what a child enjoys may be optimizing against learning. The cleanest head-to-head is a vocabulary experiment in which the same content was sequenced four different ways and automated response-history-driven selection beat both learner choice and random selection on a *delayed* posttest, which rules out the possibility that the advantage is only immediate performance. The remedy is not full system control either: the portfolio intervention worked only when paired with human coaching, and learner-controlled selection can work when the system exposes an open learner model and scaffolds self-assessment. The evidenced middle position is that the system owns the sequencing decision while showing the child the model of their own state.
    - **Link to source:** [Do learners really know best? Urban legends in education](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.804395), [Instruction based on adaptive learning technologies](http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~aleven/Papers/2016/Aleven_etal_Handbook2017_AdaptiveLearningTechnologies.pdf)

- **Subcategory 7.6: Language-model tutors at field scale, and the retention interval nobody has measured**
  - **Source:** De Simone, Tiberti, Barron Rodriguez, Manolio, Mosuro & Dikoru (2025); located absence across the trials assembled in this document
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A randomized controlled trial deployed a GPT-4-powered assistant to support first-year senior secondary students in English language learning over six weeks — twelve 90-minute sessions in computer labs, students working in pairs under teacher supervision — across nine public secondary schools in Edo State, Nigeria. *(De Simone, Tiberti, Barron Rodriguez, Manolio, Mosuro & Dikoru, 2025, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11125, DOI 10.1596/1813-9450-11125.)* `T1` `[working paper, not peer-reviewed; supervised computer labs with paired students, not an autonomous tool]`
      - The intervention produced a 0.31 standard deviation improvement on a composite assessment covering English, AI knowledge, and digital skills; the effect on English, the main outcome of interest, was 0.23 standard deviations. *(De Simone et al., 2025.)* `T1`
      - Intent-to-treat effects by section were English 0.24 σ, digital skills 0.14 σ, AI skills 0.31 σ, and composite 0.26 σ; on the third-term curricular exam, whose content spanned the entire year rather than only the six weeks of intervention, the effect was 0.21 standard deviations. *(De Simone et al., 2025.)* `T1`
      - Cost-effectiveness analysis equated the learning gains to 1.5 to 2 years of "business-as-usual" schooling. *(De Simone et al., 2025.)* `T1`
      - While the program benefited students across the baseline ability distribution, the largest effects were for female students and those with higher initial academic performance. *(De Simone et al., 2025.)* `T1`
      - Across the language-model tutoring trials assembled in this document, no study located reports a retention or transfer measure taken more than a few weeks after the intervention ended; the longest measured horizon located is a third-term curricular exam administered shortly after a six-week program. *(No such measure located in De Simone et al., 2025; Kestin et al., 2025; or Bastani et al., 2025.)* `[an absence located by targeted search across the trials assembled here — not a published null result, and not a claim that no such study exists anywhere]`
      - As of early 2024, one review of the tutoring literature stated that "no evidence on the effects of chatbot tutoring has yet been published." *(von Hippel, 2024, *Education Next* 24(2):22–31.)* `T1` `[true at time of writing and since superseded; recorded here to date the evidence base]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the largest field trial of language-model tutoring with school-age students located, and it is the one that measured a broader outcome than the material taught: the third-term curricular exam covered the whole year and still showed 0.21 standard deviations, which is meaningfully different from a local post-test and the closest thing in this evidence base to a transfer measure. Two limits travel with the number. It is a working paper rather than peer-reviewed work, and the heterogeneity finding runs the wrong direction for an equity claim, since the largest effects were among students with higher initial academic performance — echoing the separate finding that tutoring systems helped general-population students more than low achievers. The program also was not an autonomous tool: it ran in supervised computer labs with teacher facilitation and paired students. The more important finding for this document is the located absence. The entire language-model tutoring evidence base is roughly two years old, and no trial assembled here measures what remains months after the tool is withdrawn, which is precisely the interval on which a claim to have durably improved reasoning would have to rest.
    - **Link to source:** [From chalkboards to chatbots: evaluating the impact of generative AI on learning outcomes in Nigeria](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11125)

- **Subcategory 7.7: Automatically generated reasoning items — do machine-made items behave like human-made ones?**
  - **Source:** Matzen, Benz, Dixon, Posey, Kroger & Speed (2010); Gierl, Lai, Pugh, Touchie, Boulais & De Champlain (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors analyzed the relation types appearing in Raven's original Standard Progressive Matrices and built software that recombines the same relation types according to user-chosen parameters, supporting six basic shapes with five fill patterns, five sizes, four or five orientations, and six numerosity variants, yielding 12,900 possible shape variants. *(Matzen, Benz, Dixon, Posey, Kroger & Speed, 2010, *Behavior Research Methods* 42(2):525–541, DOI 10.3758/BRM.42.2.525.)* `T2`
      - The norming study used 840 representative generated 3×3 matrices; 80 undergraduates (mean age 20) each completed all 60 Standard Progressive Matrices items plus one of 20 counterbalanced lists of 42 generated matrices. *(Matzen et al., 2010.)* `T2` `[adult undergraduate sample; no child sample]`
      - Cronbach's alpha was .73 for the comparable subset of Standard Progressive Matrices and .76 for the generated matrices. *(Matzen et al., 2010.)* `T2`
      - The correlation between each participant's accuracy on the generated problems and on the Standard Progressive Matrices was .69, and the attenuation-corrected correlation was .93. *(Matzen et al., 2010.)* `T2`
      - Paired t tests found no significant difference in accuracy between generated and original matrices for two-relation problems (p = .31) or one-relation problems (p = .09); the generated matrices "both covered and expanded on the range of problem difficulties provided by the SPMs." *(Matzen et al., 2010.)* `T2`
      - Item difficulty was systematically controllable: a one-way ANOVA found no significant difference across the five transformation types but a significant effect of transformation direction, F(4,19) = 5.93, p < .01, with outward transformations significantly harder than any other direction. *(Matzen et al., 2010, Table 2.)* `T2`
      - A separate study describes itself as "one of the first empirical studies designed to evaluate the psychometric properties of generated multiple-choice items," using results from a high-stakes national medical licensure examination. *(Gierl, Lai, Pugh, Touchie, Boulais & De Champlain, 2016, *Applied Measurement in Education* 29(3):196–210, p. 196, DOI 10.1080/08957347.2016.1171768.)* `T2` `[medical licensure content; adult examinees]`
      - Prior to that work, because items generated using cognitive modeling procedures had received limited use in operational testing, "the psychometric characteristics of generated multiple-choice test items are largely unknown and undocumented." *(Gierl et al., 2016, p. 196.)* `T2`
      - Item analysis revealed that generated items measured examinees' performance across a broad range of ability levels while providing a consistently strong level of discrimination, and that results for the incorrect options consistently differentiated low from high performing examinees. *(Gierl et al., 2016, p. 196.)* `T2`
      - No study was located that evaluates the psychometric equivalence of language-model-generated reasoning practice items for children. *(No such source located across the automatic item generation literature assembled here.)* `[an absence located by targeted search; the located evidence covers medical licensure items and matrix items for adult undergraduates]`
      - None of the tutoring-system meta-analyses assembled in this category reports a fluid-reasoning or matrix-reasoning transfer measure as an outcome; all reported outcomes are domain achievement tests. *(No such outcome located in VanLehn, 2011; Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, 2013, 2014; Ma et al., 2014; Kulik & Fletcher, 2016; or Wang et al., 2024.)* `[an absence located by targeted search across the sources assembled here]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** For matrix reasoning specifically — the item type closest to a domain-general reasoning task — automatically generated items are demonstrably equivalent to human-authored ones on the properties that matter. Reliability was comparable at .76 against .73, the two sets correlated .69 raw and .93 corrected for attenuation, and item difficulty did not differ significantly for one- or two-relation problems. The result also establishes that generated-item difficulty is controllable by construction, since the number of relations drives difficulty predictably and direction of transformation is an independent measured lever — which is exactly what an adaptive practice engine needs, an unlimited item supply whose difficulty is known before the child sees the item. The high-stakes testing literature confirms that generated items can carry acceptable difficulty spread and discrimination in operational use, though from medical licensure content and adult examinees. The two located absences matter more than the positive findings: nothing verifies that generated reasoning items calibrate correctly for children, and no adaptive-instruction meta-analysis has ever used a reasoning transfer measure as its outcome. A product claiming to improve reasoning is therefore claiming an outcome this entire evidence base has never measured.
    - **Link to source:** [Recreating Raven's: software for systematically generating large numbers of Raven-like matrix problems](https://doi.org/10.3758/BRM.42.2.525), [Evaluating the psychometric characteristics of generated multiple-choice test items](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957347.2016.1171768)
