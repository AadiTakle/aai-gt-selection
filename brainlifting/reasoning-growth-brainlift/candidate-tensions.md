# Candidate Tensions — Training a Trait That Is Supposed to Be Fixed

Raw material for the owner's DOK 3 work. Every line records a place where two sources in the research
base disagree, and names both. Nothing here is a conclusion, a resolution, or a recommendation — a
tension becomes an insight only once the owner has interrogated it.

---

## 1. Whether measured reasoning ability moves at all

- Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Perrig report Gf transfer from dual n-back training at d = 0.65 with a monotonic dose-response across 8 to 19 training days, while Redick and colleagues ran 20 sessions of the same task with both an active placebo and a no-contact control and found no significant Group × Session interaction on any of 17 measures and no dose-response relationship.
- Au, Sheehan, Tsai, Duncan, Buschkuehl & Jaeggi report a significant positive effect of n-back training on Gf of g = 0.24, about 3–4 IQ points, while Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme report g = 0.05 with a confidence interval crossing zero on the same construct against treated controls.
- Au et al. find within their own data that passive-control studies yield g = 0.44 and active-control studies g = 0.06 at p < .01, yet conclude that control type "did not moderate the training effect" and attribute the gap to remuneration and geography, while Melby-Lervåg and colleagues treat treated-versus-untreated control status as the decisive design factor and find that difference statistically significant.
- Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Shah report a correlation of r = 0.42 between improvement on the training task and improvement on Gf in children, while Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme report a mediation analysis in which improvements in nonverbal ability were not significantly related to improvements on the near-transfer working memory tasks (β = −.01, p = .86).
- Jaeggi et al. found no whole-group transfer in 62 children and recovered a significant effect only after a post hoc median split of the 32 trained children into subgroups of 16, and state themselves that the median split "does not provide a predictive value in the sense of a clearly defined training criterion."
- Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme report an overall nonverbal-ability effect of g = 0.05 against treated controls, but within the same analysis find n-back training showing a significant g = 0.15 effect, which they then discount because the five largest n-back effect sizes all had fewer than 20 observations per group.
- Sala & Gobet report a significant far-transfer effect in typically developing children of g = 0.12, while the same paper reports g = 0.03 with a confidence interval crossing zero in the subset of randomized studies with active control groups.
- Melby-Lervåg & Hulme report an immediate nonverbal-ability effect of d = 0.19 whose trim-and-fill adjustment moves it *upward* to d = 0.34, while their moderator analysis states that studies with treated control groups had a mean effect close to zero — the publication-bias correction and the design-quality correction push the same literature in opposite directions.
- Ritchie & Tucker-Drob find that education raises intelligence test scores by 1 to 5 IQ points per year and that "the effects persisted across the life span," while Protzko finds intervention-induced IQ gains declining at 0.11 to 0.13 standard deviations per year until they reach zero, and Bailey, Duncan, Odgers & Yu find early-childhood cognitive effects falling from .23 to .10 within 12 months.
- Ritchie & Tucker-Drob report internally divergent estimates by test type: in one design the effect on composite tests was 1.876 IQ points against 0.836 on fluid tests, with the difference significant, whereas in another design the fluid and composite estimates were statistically indistinguishable.
- Trahan, Stuebing, Fletcher & Hiscock report that their estimate is "not consistent with the hypothesis that the Flynn effect is diminishing," while Pietschnig & Voracek report that gains "have decreased in more recent decades" and Bratsberg & Rogeberg report an outright reversal in Norwegian cohorts after 1975.
- Bratsberg & Rogeberg report two different magnitudes for the same within-family decline: the standard fixed-effects model recovers −0.08 IQ points per year within families against −0.23 across families, while their selection-corrected model recovers −0.33 against −0.34.
- Protzko states in the abstract that the analysis covers 39 randomized controlled trials while the Results section describes 23 randomized trials yielding 39 interventions.
- Bailey and colleagues report a geometric fadeout pattern averaged across 67 interventions, but note in the same section that one program's IQ impacts "persisted well beyond age 8," concluding that cross-study averages "are likely to conceal study-to-study variation."
- Turkheimer, Haley, Waldron, D'Onofrio & Gottesman report heritability of IQ at about .10 in the low-SES half of their twin sample against .72 in the high-SES half, so the heritability estimate is not a fixed property of the trait, while Sauce & Matzel argue standard heritability metrics inflate estimates of genetic effects.
- Simons and colleagues report that "none of the cited studies conformed to all of the best practices we identify," and Sala & Gobet state that "the better the design, the smaller the size of the effect," while Au and colleagues draw a positive conclusion from studies drawn substantially from the same literature.
- Population-level evidence establishes fluid reasoning as environmentally movable at 0.41 points per year, while the individual-level training literature finds corrected far transfer indistinguishable from zero — the trait moves at the scale of societies and refuses to move at the scale of a training program.

## 2. Whether training reasoning transfers to measured reasoning

- Klauer & Phye report a weighted mean of d+ = 0.52 on fluid-intelligence test performance from inductive-reasoning training, with effects increasing rather than decaying across 3-to-15-month follow-ups, while Sala, Aksaylı, Tatlidil, Tatsumi, Gondo & Gobet report far-transfer effects falling to 0.01 and then 0.00 once placebo and publication bias are controlled, and call the absence of generalization "an invariant of human cognition."
- Klauer & Phye explicitly reject the test-coaching explanation on the grounds that effects persisted and transferred to academic learning, while Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard report that adding coaching to repeated administration nearly triples the practice effect, and Savell, Twohig & Rachford ask of the parallel Feuerstein program "whether the study produced anything more than near-transfer or 'practice effects'" precisely because gains concentrated on measures content-similar to the training materials.
- Klauer & Phye report that their intelligence measures "in part, or in their entirety, measure inductive reasoning" — the same six item classes the program trains — while Barnett & Ceci argue transfer must be located on nine specified dimensions and that a single far-transfer effect size is misguided, which means the 0.52 figure may be a near-transfer quantity wearing a far-transfer label.
- Klauer & Phye predicted a smaller effect on academic learning than on intelligence tests on transfer-distance grounds, and found the reverse — 0.69 against 0.52, significantly larger — describing this themselves as "in contrast to expectations."
- Klauer & Phye's nine three-group placebo experiments yield d+ = 0.004 for alternatively trained groups, which rules out attention and social contact as the mechanism, while Sala et al.'s correction procedure attributes the far-transfer literature's apparent effects to precisely the placebo and publication-bias sources that Klauer's design appears to exclude.
- Klauer & Phye report studies by Klauer's own staff at d+ = 0.57 against d+ = 0.49 for independent authors, a gap the paper treats as reassuringly small, while the meta-analysis evaluating the training program was conducted by the person who designed and published the program.
- Klauer & Phye report d+ = 0.52 on intelligence tests across 74 experiments, while Baumeister & Rindermann, running the same Program I as an outside group, obtain d = −0.17 on fluid intelligence from the paper-and-pencil version and d = 0.28 from the PC version, and attribute the movement in the first study to maturation and retest familiarity rather than to the training.
- Baumeister & Rindermann argue their small effects are practically meaningful because they correspond to more than half a year of schooling and maturation, while Ritchie & Tucker-Drob establish schooling itself as the causal comparison — so the same number can be read as a training effect worth having or as the program merely reproducing what another year of ordinary school would have delivered anyway.
- Romney & Samuels report significant though modest effects in all three claimed areas of Instrumental Enrichment with ability improving most, while Savell, Twohig & Rachford report that effects on achievement, self-concept, classroom behavior and impulsivity were "absent, inconsistent, or difficult to interpret," with reliable effects confined largely to nonverbal figural and spatial measures.
- Detterman concludes that transfer is rare and directly related to similarity between situations, and that education policy advocating otherwise is "totally lacking in empirical support," while Perkins & Salomon conclude from the same evidence base that although "transfer comes hard," "education can achieve abundant transfer if it is designed to do so."
- DerSimonian & Laird estimate the SAT coaching effect at 10 points and call it too small to be practically important, while Messick & Jungeblut report that reaching gains beyond 20–30 points requires contact time approaching full-time schooling — so the two agree on the magnitude and disagree on whether a program with far more contact time than a coaching course inherits the ceiling or escapes it.
- Hausknecht et al. report that repeated testing produces genuine gains that also occur with alternate forms, suggesting they are not simply recall effects, while Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad report that the latent factor underlying retest scores is less saturated with g and more associated with memory, and that the shift eliminated the test's criterion-related validity.
- Scharfen, Peters & Holling find retest gains stop after the third administration, while Klauer & Phye report the opposite trajectory for trained effects, with follow-up performance correlating positively with elapsed months — so the two literatures predict opposite shapes for the same observable, repeated measurement over time.
- Scharfen, Peters & Holling find age negatively associated with retest gains, implying children gain more from mere repetition than the adults their sample is drawn from, while Klauer & Phye's samples are children aged 5 to 16 — the population in which the retest confound would be largest and the correction least well estimated.
- Sternberg, Torff & Grigorenko find broader instruction beating conventional teaching even on conventional memory items, while the outcome measured is achievement within taught units and no intelligence-test outcome is reported, so the result is silent on the question the other sources in this category are arguing about.

## 3. Which instructional mechanisms work, and where each one stops

- Rowland reports that initial recognition testing produced the smallest testing effect (g = 0.36) against cued recall (0.72) and free recall (0.82), with all multiple-choice tests coded as recognition, while Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan report that multiple-choice practice tests produced a significantly *higher* effect (0.70) than short-answer tests (0.48), and Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks report that format does not significantly modulate the benefit at all in classrooms.
- Rowland reports that corrective feedback yielded the largest testing effects at g = 0.73, while Yang and colleagues record that Adesope and colleagues found no moderating effect of feedback whatsoever.
- Rowland reports that matching initial and final test formats "did not contribute any increase in the magnitude of the testing effect" and calls the result inconsistent with transfer-appropriate processing, while Pan & Rickard identify response congruency as their single most substantive moderator (0.28 without it, 0.58 with it) and Agarwal reads her matched-format results as most consistent with transfer-appropriate processing.
- Pan & Rickard report an average transfer effect of d = 0.40, while the same analysis reports that after publication-bias correction the intercept "often indicat[es] no positive transfer when none of the aforementioned moderators are present."
- Agarwal finds that fact-based retrieval practice did not improve higher-order test performance and higher-order practice did not improve fact recall, while the popular reading of Bloom's taxonomy assumes lower-level practice builds a foundation for higher-order performance.
- Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer report that only 12 of 271 massed-versus-spaced comparisons failed to favour spacing, while the same paper reports that within every individual retention-interval bin from 10 minutes to 7 days none of the differences reached statistical significance.
- Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler report that the optimal gap is 43% of a 7-day retention interval and 8% of a 350-day one, so a scheduler tuned for one horizon is mistuned for the other, while most software schedulers operate on a fixed interval ladder.
- Brunmair & Richter report an overall interleaving effect of g = 0.42 and a *negative* effect for word materials of g = −0.39 favouring blocking, while Rohrer & Taylor report a mixed-practice advantage of 63% against 20% on a one-week test and Bjork & Bjork list interleaving among the desirable difficulties without a material-type restriction.
- Rohrer & Taylor report that blocked practice beat mixed practice by 29 points during acquisition and lost by 43 points a week later, so the same manipulation is the best and the worst option depending on when it is measured.
- Bjork & Bjork hold that conditions rapidly increasing retrieval strength differ from conditions maximising storage strength, while Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas hold that extraneous load should be eliminated and floundering minimised — two theories that prescribe opposite responses to the same observation of a struggling learner.
- Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas state that "only intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load are distinguished as basic categories," while Renkl & Atkinson, working within the same theory, argue that later-stage learner activities "constitute either germane or extraneous load."
- Sweller & Cooper report that worked-example advantages "were specific to problems identical in structure to the initial ones," while Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas state that studying worked examples "may facilitate knowledge construction and transfer performance more than actually solving the equivalent problems."
- Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller report that guidance helping novices actively harms more experienced learners and that without tailoring "the effectiveness of instructional designs is likely to be random," while Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill report that fading alone — the standard remedy — does not reliably improve far transfer.
- Kluger & DeNisi report a mean feedback effect of 0.41 with "over 38% of the effects" negative, while Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie characterise the same study as reporting "an average effect of 0.38... about a third of the effects were negative."
- Hattie & Timperley report an average feedback effect of 0.79 from a synthesis of meta-analyses, while Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie — a primary meta-analysis co-authored by Hattie — reports 0.48 and records the published range as 0.48, 0.70, and 0.79.
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie report d = 0.48 in the abstract and d = 0.55 in the results text for the random-effects integration of all study effects.
- Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher find the prompt-versus-no-prompt difference modest (32% against 22% gain) while the within-prompted-group difference between high and low explainers was large, so the intervention a product can deliver is not the variable that predicts the outcome.
- Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne find self-explanation at g = 0.55 with only three of 20 moderators detectable, while Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham rated self-explanation only moderate utility because its generalizability across materials, learners, and criterion tasks was unestablished.
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns report an average mastery-learning effect of 0.52 across 103 studies, while none of the five studies employing standardized criterion tests found a significant or even small effect on the standardized test — and the authors concede Slavin is correct that the discrepancy is real.
- Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks find the classroom testing effect at elementary level (0.328) roughly half the high-school level (0.655), so the age band a children's reasoning program serves is the band where the best-evidenced mechanism is weakest.

## 4. What an observed improvement is made of

- Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman report a GPT-4 tutor improving practice-problem grades by 48% and then reducing unassisted exam performance by 17% once access was removed, while Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti report a scaffolded AI tutor producing median learning gains over double an active-learning classroom's, at 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations.
- Bastani et al. measured the outcome after tutor access was removed on a separate unassisted exam, while Kestin et al. measured on a post-test administered immediately after each lesson and report no delayed retention test — so the two may not be measuring the same construct, and neither paper reconciles this.
- Kestin et al. describe a tutor whose prompts contained step-by-step solutions and which sequenced students through problem parts, which resembles Bastani et al.'s guardrailed "GPT Tutor" arm — the arm in which Bastani et al. found no learning harm but also no learning benefit over control.
- Bastani et al. report that the guardrailed tutor delivered the *larger* practice-time gain of the two AI arms (127% against 48%) while delivering no exam benefit at all, so within a single experiment the size of the visible practice gain ran opposite to the durable outcome.
- Soderstrom & Bjork hold that manipulations that speed acquisition and reduce error frequently fail to support retention and transfer, while Koedinger & Aleven report that an overriding concern in Cognitive Tutor design was minimizing student floundering, and that Corbett & Anderson found no support for information withholding via simple delayed feedback.
- Morris, Bransford & Franks establish that no encoding operation is superior in absolute terms, only relative to a specified test, while an app reporting that a child "improved" names no test against which the improvement was defined.
- Sparrow, Liu & Wegner report that expecting future access to information lowers recall of it, while Schooler & Storm report that the effect appears only when participants have experienced the external store as reliable and vanishes when they have experienced it failing.
- Risko & Gilbert state that any benefit of offloading is contingent on the offloaded demand being unnecessary to the learning goal, while Koedinger & Aleven report that giving more information after errors is helpful when it reduces floundering — and neither supplies the test that tells an automated system which case it is in.
- Koriat & Bjork find that judgments of learning made in the presence of the answer instill a sense of competence that does not survive testing, while Bastani et al. report that students using the unguarded tutor believed they had learned as much as their peers despite scoring materially worse unaided.

## 5. What a learning system can observe, and what the observation means

- Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner report gaming as a single construct uniformly associated with lower post-test performance at r = −0.38, while Baker, Corbett, Roll & Koedinger report that gaming splits into a harmful and a non-harmful type, and that in one lesson the non-harmful type was three times as prevalent as the harmful one while in another it was nine times rarer.
- Baker, Corbett, Roll & Koedinger report that harmful-gaming detectors transfer to new students and new lessons at A' = 0.80, and San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo report that carelessness detectors transfer across tutor versions and countries, while Ocumpaugh, Baker, Gowda, N. Heffernan & C. Heffernan report that affect detectors trained on one of three US subpopulations fall from κ = 0.26 to κ = 0.03 on the others.
- Beck & Gong report wheel-spinning prevalence of roughly 31% and 38% of student-skill pairs, while Zhang, Huang, Wang, Lu, Fang, Stamper, Fancsali, Holstein & Aleven report 6.6%, 0.56%, and 10.2% under the same criterion across three datasets — a range spanning nearly two orders of magnitude for the same construct.
- Zhang and colleagues report that the two leading operationalizations of wheel-spinning agree on fewer than half the pairs either one labels, and state that this divergence is "not desirable from either a practical or a theoretical perspective, as we do not know which one to apply or build on."
- Zhang and colleagues report that wheel-spinning is detectable at 90.8% precision by the fourth practice opportunity, while all models fall below 20% precision in the first three steps — so the signal exists but not at the moment a system would most want to act on it.
- Baker reports that one study found off-task behavior not significantly correlated with lower learning, while noting that a later meta-analysis by the same research group found a statistically significant negative correlation.
- Aleven, McLaren, Roll & Koedinger report that overall help-seeking bugs predicted lower learning at partial r = −0.65 and Help Abuse at −0.48, while Help Avoidance did not predict learning at all at −0.12 — so the two halves of the same construct carry opposite diagnostic value.
- Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger report that metacognitive feedback improved help-seeking with large effects that transferred to new content a month later, while an earlier version of the same intervention by the same team reduced help-seeking errors with "no evidence that they learned the intended help-seeking skills, or learned the domain knowledge better."
- Aleven and colleagues report help-seeking success and cognitive success rate correlating at r = 0.81 with only the cognitive rate surviving a joint regression, so the metacognitive signal may be a restatement of the knowledge signal rather than an addition to it.
- San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo report that careless errors are *most* common among students who frequently experience engaged concentration (r = 0.58) and least common among confused and bored students, while D'Mello & Graesser model engagement as the desirable steady state and confusion as the perturbation away from it.
- D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun & Graesser report substantially higher posttest and transfer performance when learners were successfully confused, while the same authors state that persistent unresolved confusion "is expected to accompany negligible or poor learning" — so the same detected state predicts opposite outcomes depending on what happens next.
- D'Mello and colleagues report that learners' self-reports of confusion were largely insensitive to a manipulation that behavioral measures showed had confused them, while Krumm, Beattie, Takahashi, D'Angelo, Feng & Cheng report that the productive-persistence framework measures its construct primarily through self-report.
- Baker & Hawn report that training nation-specific essay-scoring models *increased* algorithmic bias relative to a single pooled model, while Ocumpaugh and colleagues report that pooling training data across subpopulations improved but did not equalize detector performance.
- Baker warns that acting aggressively on detected off-task behavior may convert off-task students into gaming students, while gaming is the behavior most strongly associated with learning loss — so the intervention triggered by one detector can manufacture the condition another detector exists to catch.
- Krumm and colleagues describe their behavioral measures as "practical measures" explicitly designed for instructor action and explicitly distinguished from accountability measures, while the same behavioral signals are the natural raw material for a selection use.

## 6. What "hustle" names, and whether it survives measurement

- Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews & Kelly report grit showing incremental validity over IQ and conscientiousness, while Credé, Tynan & Harms report that after conscientiousness is entered first, overall grit adds incremental R = .000 for overall academic performance.
- Duckworth and colleagues present grit as a higher-order trait with two facets, while Credé and colleagues report that the higher-order structure is not confirmed and that the two facets differ significantly on all four grade criteria.
- Duckworth and colleagues validate and publish the Grit Scale as predictive of success, while Duckworth later states it "is not appropriate for selecting employees, admitting students to college, gauging the performance of teachers, or comparing schools" and that "you can fake a higher grit score without much effort."
- Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara report that socioeconomic status was *not* a significant moderator of the mindset–achievement correlation but *was* a significant moderator of intervention effects, within the same paper.
- Sisk and colleagues report that low-SES students (d = 0.34) and high-risk students (d = 0.19) may benefit from mindset interventions, while Macnamara & Burgoyne report across 63 studies that "no theoretically meaningful moderators were significant."
- Macnamara & Burgoyne report that among the six highest-quality studies the mindset intervention effect is 0.02 and nonsignificant, while Yeager and colleagues report a pre-registered, nationally representative effect of 0.10 grade points at p = 0.001 among lower-achieving students.
- Macnamara & Burgoyne report that restricting to studies where the intervention demonstrably changed mindsets yields a *nonsignificant* achievement effect, while Yeager and colleagues report that their intervention changed mindsets by 0.33 and grades by 0.11 — the belief effect three times the size of the outcome effect.
- Dweck, Walton & Cohen state that interventions targeting psychological factors "can transform students' experience and achievement... months and even years later," while Macnamara & Burgoyne conclude that apparent effects "are likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias."
- Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer state that individual differences even among elite performers are closely related to assessed amounts of deliberate practice, while Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald report that deliberate practice explains 4–5% of performance variance in education specifically.
- Dignath & Büttner report an average effect of 0.69 for self-regulated learning interventions in schools, while Sisk and colleagues report 0.08 and Macnamara & Burgoyne report 0.05 for growth mindset interventions in the same settings — an order of magnitude apart for two constructs routinely bundled together.
- Dignath & Büttner report that effects were higher when researchers rather than regular teachers ran the training, and Donker and colleagues report that effects were higher when the outcome test was self-developed rather than independent, while both present the aggregate figures as evidence the approach works at school scale.
- West, Kraft, Finn, Martin, Duckworth, Gabrieli & Gabrieli report that within students, self-reported grit and conscientiousness correlate positively with attendance and test-score gains, while their lottery-based estimates show a year at a high-performing school *lowering* self-reported self-control by 0.21 and grit by 0.12 even as absences fell and suspensions were unchanged.
- Duckworth & Kern report that self-report questionnaires of self-control converge with each other at r = .50 but with behavioral tasks at r = .10–.15, while the entire predictive literature on grit and conscientiousness rests almost entirely on questionnaire measures.
- Duckworth & Yeager state that performance tasks circumvent reference bias, social desirability, acquiescence, and faking and may be more sensitive to subtle change, while the same paper states a one-time task is "not particularly diagnostic of their everyday behavior in typical life situations."
- The Carnegie Foundation reports 51% of program students completing college-level math credit in one year against a 5.9% baseline, while the peer-reviewed propensity-matched evaluation of the same program reports 5.57 against 4.08 accumulated credits from a design with no random assignment.
- Poropat reports conscientiousness predicting academic performance at ρ = .22, stronger than grit's ρ = .18 — so the construct built to explain achievement "beyond talent" underperforms the ordinary trait it was proposed to supersede.
- Yeager and colleagues measured their decisive school-level moderator behaviorally, by what control students actually chose to work on, while every construct in this category is conventionally measured by asking.

## 7. What machine-delivered instruction has been measured to deliver

- Kulik & Fletcher report a median tutoring-system effect of 0.66 across 50 evaluations, while Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper report 0.01 to 0.09 for the same technology on K-12 mathematics, with the random-effects estimate not significantly different from zero.
- Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu report significant positive effects "at all levels of education" with no substantive differences across 18 moderators, while Kulik & Fletcher report 0.44 for K-12 against 0.75 for postsecondary and Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper report 0.32–0.37 for college against 0.01–0.09 for K-12.
- VanLehn reports tutoring systems (0.76) nearly as effective as human tutoring (0.79), and Ma and colleagues report no significant difference (g = −.11), while Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper report that such systems "were less effective than human tutoring" for college students.
- Bloom reports the average tutored student about two standard deviations above the control class, while Cohen, Kulik & Kulik — published two years earlier — report an average tutoring effect of 0.40, and Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan report 0.288 across randomized preK-12 experiments.
- Bloom attributes the two-sigma result to tutoring, while von Hippel reports that roughly half the gain likely derived from the additional quizzes and corrective feedback also present in the mastery-learning condition, and that Bloom's own cited source gave tutoring 0.4.
- Wang, Huang, Sommer, Pei, Shidfar, Rehman, Ritzhaupt & Martin report that adaptive learning produces 0.70 over non-adaptive instruction and that the type of AI in the engine did not moderate the effect, while Aleven, McLaughlin, Glenn & Koedinger report that support varies sharply by *form* of adaptivity — strong for task-loop adaptation to knowledge, essentially absent for adaptation to learning styles.
- Kulik & Fletcher report Cognitive Tutor effects of 0.76 on locally developed tests and 0.12 on standardized tests, and Cohen, Kulik & Kulik report 0.84 against 0.27 on the same split for human tutoring 34 years earlier — the same moderator, the same magnitude, two literatures apart.
- Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper report that tutoring-system effects were *greater* for students from the general population than for low achievers, and De Simone and colleagues report the largest AI-tutoring effects among students with higher initial academic performance, while both technologies are routinely framed as equity interventions.
- Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper report that effects were greater when interventions lasted less than a school year than one year or longer, while a program intending to produce durable change is by construction a long one.
- Kirschner & van Merriënboer report that given free choice, low-ability learners practice what they are already good at and avoid what they are weak at, while a tool that targets diagnosed weaknesses depends on the opposite behavior.
- Atkinson's four-way comparison found automated response-history selection beating both learner control and random selection on a *delayed* posttest, while Aleven and colleagues report that learner-controlled selection works when paired with an open learner model — so the evidence supports neither pure system control nor pure learner control.
- Matzen and colleagues establish that generated matrix items are psychometrically equivalent to human-authored ones and difficulty-controllable by construction, while the norming sample was 80 undergraduates and no located study establishes the same for children.
- No adaptive-instruction meta-analysis assembled here reports a reasoning-transfer outcome, while a product claiming to improve reasoning is claiming exactly that outcome.
