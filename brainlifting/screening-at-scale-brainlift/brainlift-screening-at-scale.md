# BrainLift: Screening at Scale — Why 100k Cannot Come From the Kids Already Found, and What a Screener Would Have to Do Instead

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose

GT wants 100,000 students who are ready for MIT by eighth grade. This BrainLift asks whether that number can come from the children the country has already identified as gifted, and if not, what has to be built instead.

It cannot. The identified pool is big enough on paper and unreachable in practice, because no school has ever enrolled more than about one percent of one state's identified children. So the students have to come from kids nobody has looked at. That leads to the next three questions. Why does the country find so few gifted children in the first place. Where would a screener have to live to reach the ones it misses. And how short can that screener be before the decision stops being trustworthy.

Two terms show up constantly below. **Sensitivity** is the share of qualifying children a process actually finds, so 30% sensitivity means it finds three in ten and misses seven. A **referral** is an adult putting a child forward to be tested, which is the step before the test, not the test itself. Almost everything in this document turns on that second one.

### In Scope

- How many children clear each bar, how many are identified, and what share of them 100,000 would require.
- What share of an identified population any school has ever actually enrolled.
- Why gifted identification misses most qualifying children, and how big the miss is.
- What an announced ability test measures besides ability: coaching, retesting, anxiety, and who showed up.
- What is known and not known about measuring ability inside a game.
- How few questions a yes-or-no decision needs, and how to tune it so it makes the error it can afford.

### Out of Scope

- Whether the education model works once a child is enrolled. `gt-school-counterfactual` and `placement-and-dosing` cover that.
- Pricing and funding. `market-sizing` covers the funding constraint.
- Building an original cognitive instrument. Everything here assumes existing item types and existing methods.
- Whether the work is the applicant's own (`proctoring-integrity`).

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

**SPOV 1: It is impossible to reach the 100K MIT-ready kids by 8th grade North Star with the current pool of identified gifted children.**

- **Elaboration:** GT's screening bar is the 95th percentile. About **1,787,336 American K-8 children** clear it, which is over 17.8x the 100,000 marker GT is aiming for (1.1). Even if not all necessarily clear GT's cutoff because gifted programs use different standards (1.2), 100,000 is still **5.09% of every identified child in the country**. Nobody has approached that capture rate. For instance, Pine View School holds 1,752 students, or **1.07% of Florida's 163,103 identified gifted children**, and it is the largest school for gifted children in America (1.3). The five best-known such schools together hold 0.077% of the national pool. Give GT Pine View's rate in every state at once and it reaches 21,120 students. Hitting 100,000 means beating the best capture rate on record by **4.7x**. Comparing the target with MIT's intake shows the scale of that outcome, but it does not shrink the 95th-percentile candidate pool (1.4). The recruitment funnel still closes the door from the other side. 100,000 seats needs about 455,000 potential applicants, which is 23% of the identified pool, while everything parents put forward on their own initiative comes to about 84,648 children across all nine grades (1.4). The counter is that a new online category can reach a share no existing gifted school has reached. The required lever is discovery.

**SPOV 2: Screeners are missing a majority of applicants because they're only looking for the gifted kids an adult has already noticed.**

- **Elaboration:** McBee, Peters and Miller modelled what a nomination step costs when you put it in front of a real test. At a 90th-percentile nomination cutoff, the system finds **45.3% of qualifying children with the TCS-2, 40.4% with the WISC-III, 28% with the CogAT** (2.2). Those are miss rates of 54.7%, 59.6% and 72.0%. **The 55% we quote is the mildest of the three**, and the paper's abstract says the miss rate "can easily exceed 60%" under commonly implemented conditions. Other studies report real-world systems at 30% to 40% sensitivity, which is 60% to 70% missed. Giving the same test to everyone with no gate finds **84.3%%** at a 90th-percentile cut and **81.5%** at the 95th. The instrument misses about 1/6 children, and the gate in front of it misses more than 1/2. As Mcbee, Peters and Miller state, a confirmatory test "can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives because those students that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory test. **Screeners cannot improve the sensitivity of an assessment system; they can only reduce it.**" Across all **705,074 Georgia elementary students, 9.9% were ever referred by any adult and parents put forward 0.4%** (2.1). Paid-lunch children were referred at **3.7x** the rate of free-lunch children, so the gap opens before anyone is measured. Nomination predicts the identifying tests at 32% overall, 31% for teachers, 30% for parents, and 33% for self reports (2.4). In another study, Broward County dropped the gate and tested every 2nd grader with the bar unchanged, and identification went from **3.3% to 5.5% overall and 1.4% to 4.3% among disadvantaged students. 20% of the newly found already had IQs of 130 or higher** (2.3). Rates fell when screening was defunded, which meanas purely raising awareness is not a contributing factor. Practitioners may say a child nobody notices is simply not be gifted, but Broward retorts that the kids found by testing everyone cleared the same bar.

**SPOV 3: Screeners that are stealthily integrated into existing applications/games are more effective at measuring gifted ability than traditional screeners.**

- **Elaboration:** The common misconception that motivation raises IQ scores was **retracted in May 2025** after a fraudulent study was pulled from it. The effect dropped from g = 0.64 to 0.47, and correcting for publication bias took it to **g = 0.16 with a confidence interval containing zero** (3.3). Kids do not just try harder at games but rather have score inflation through contamination. **Coaching raises aptitude and intelligence test scores by 0.43 standard deviations against only 0.15 on the SAT** (3.1). Taking a cognitive test a second time adds 0.26-0.29 and 0.46 on an identical form. In a study of 941 people, the retest factor was more about memory than general ability, and the shift "eliminated the test's criterion-related validity." Retest gains also run **0.12 for White applicants against 0.04 for Black applicants**, so preparation widens the gap screening is meant to close. Anxiety pushes the other way, correlating with achievement at **−.22 in grades 1-5 and −.25 in grades 6-8** across 53,617 children (3.2). An announced gifted test is not a clean instrument for measuring giftedness. Families can essentially buy their way into better scores, and that only identifies the kids whose parents signed them up. A measurement a child does not know is happening has none of the preparation nor the adult deciding who takes it. Teens spend **1 hour 27 minutes a day gaming** against a 0.4% referral rate (3.4). Game-based measures correlate with established tests at about **0.30 observed and 0.45 corrected**, so a hidden screener's contamination is measurable while an announced test's is not.

**SPOV 4: Using existing screening technology, we can estimate if a student would make a good GT applicant within 15 minutes.**

- **Elaboration:** Measuring a child's exact giftedness score and deciding which whether or not they meet a threshold are different problems. Reliability is around **90% at forty questions, 70% at ten, and 50-55% at five** (4.1). However, two-category adaptive classification hits roughly **95% correct decisions in 12.7 to 16.3 questions**, with the biggest lever being not precision but how wide the band around the cutoff exists. **For instance, the US nursing licensure exam works this way today.** It stops the moment it is 95% certain a candidate is clearly above or below the passing standard and only issues a score to people too close to call (4.2). Standard error is also the wrong target because a decision is not made of it. The professional standards say candidates far from a cutoff "can have considerable error in their observed scores without any effect on their classification decisions," and that the standard error "cannot be translated into the expected percentage of consistent or accurate decisions" (4.3). Once decision accuracy is the objective, the asymmetry no longer matters. This screener has two outputs but only one substantive decision: **recommend** makes a positive claim, while **no recommendation** is an abstention. A qualified child who receives no recommendation is still a missed referral for sensitivity, but the output cannot be used to deny an application or claim that the child is below GT's bar (2.2, 4.3, 4.5). On fixed-form tests at a selective bar, going from 40 questions to 15 barely touches specificity (**97% to 96%**) but drops sensitivity from **76% to 61%** (4.4). A short test buys its brevity out of exactly the error a screener cannot afford. This means a test should be quick to recommend and slow to abstain. A prototype that chooses each question for maximum information at the cutoff and requiring 75% confidence to recommend against 97% to rule out, gets **72.7% sensitivity and 97.1% specificity at a median of 8 questions** on 600 simulated candidates (4.5). Fifteen minutes can decide when there is enough evidence to recommend one and abstain otherwise.

## Experts

> Picked so they argue. The first pair says the referral is the problem and models it; the second pair proved it on real children. The third set disagrees about whether measurement can hide inside a game. The fourth set disagrees about whether short tests are safe at all, which is the sharpest live fight in the document.

### Matthew McBee, Scott Peters, Marcia Gentry

- **Who:** Gifted-education researchers who count how many able children the system fails to find.
- **Focus:** Miss rates, the referral step, who disappears, and what the denominator should be.
- **Why Follow:** Nearly every number in SPOV 2 comes from them, and they disagree with each other about how to count. McBee models the loss and gets 55% to 72%. Gentry counts millions "missing" against a fairness counterfactual. Peters sidesteps both by counting kids already working above their placed grade. Three methods, one direction. McBee's is worth quoting because it describes a process rather than a population, which means it names something you can change.
- **Where:** [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256) | [McBee (2006), Georgia census](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746049.pdf) | [McBee & Makel (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007) | [Peters & Johnson (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1177/23328584241258480)

### David Card and Laura Giuliano

- **Who:** Labour economists who studied a district that switched from referral to testing everyone, then switched back.
- **Focus:** What identification misses, measured on real children instead of modelled.
- **Why Follow:** The only natural experiment in the set, and the reversal is what makes it stick. Identification rose sharply with the standard provably unchanged, a fifth of the newly found kids already cleared the strictest threshold, and when the money stopped the rates went back. That last part kills the obvious objection that screening simply made parents more aware.
- **Where:** [Card & Giuliano (2016), PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113) | [Free full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED578122.pdf)

### Valerie Shute, against Marie Ohlms, Klaus Melchers and Russell Warne

- **Who:** The originator of stealth assessment, set against the people who tested its weakest assumptions.
- **Focus:** Whether ability can be measured inside a game without the child knowing, and what that measurement picks up.
- **Why Follow:** Both sides are right about something, which is why SPOV 3 needs qualifying. Shute's evidence-centred design is a real method with two decades behind it. Ohlms then showed game experience predicts game-based scores without predicting achievement, which is textbook bias. And Warne petitioned for the retraction that collapsed the field's favourite motivation finding to a confidence interval containing zero. Anyone arguing games measure better has to survive all three.
- **Where:** [Shute et al. (2023)](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/ETRD2023.pdf) | [Ohlms, Hohner & Melchers (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1111/apps.70038) | [Bipp et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence12120129) | [Duckworth et al. retraction (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2508040122)

### Theo Eggen and Gage Kingsbury, against Peter Kruyen

- **Who:** The classification-testing tradition, which says a decision needs far fewer questions than a score, against the personnel-selection tradition, which says short tests reject good people.
- **Focus:** How many questions a yes-or-no decision actually needs, and what shortness costs.
- **Why Follow:** They reach opposite conclusions from sound work, and the difference is design. Eggen hits 95% correct decisions in 12.7 to 16.3 questions by aiming every question at the cutoff. Kruyen finds that dropping from 40 to 15 questions at a selective bar costs sensitivity .76 to .61, and recommends at least 40. Both are right, because adaptive-at-the-cut and fixed-form-nested are not the same object and neither has tested the other. That gap is the honest limit on SPOV 4.
- **Where:** [Eggen (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1177/01466219922031365) | [Kingsbury & Weiss (1979)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED209336.pdf) | [Kruyen, Emons & Sijtsma (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15305058.2011.643517) | [NCLEX adaptive testing](https://www.nclex.com/computerized-adaptive-testing.page)

## DOK 3: Insights

**Insight 1 - At the 95th percentile, there are enough qualifying children to reach the 100K marker, but no institution has ever reached enough of them.**

- There are two different ways to measure the population of GT qualifying children in the US. First, we can globally apply GT's 95th-percentile CogAT cutoff to identify about 1,787,336 K-8 children that are GT eligible. This makes the 100,000 marker 5.59% of the full qualifying population (1.1). Alternatively, we can apply the national gifted-identification rate to produce a more generous upper estimate of 1,966,152 identified K-8 children. It is important to note that is not the same population: gifted programs use different standards, so not every identified child necessarily clears GT's cutoff, but it gives a good upper bound (1.2). Regardless of which method you use, its clear that GT's issue with reaching the 100K marker doesn't lie with the size of the qualifying population or the qualifyng bar (95th is generous if anything). Instead, the problem is found in how GT will capture this audience and get them into the ecosystem. Pine View, the largest gifted school in the country, holds 1.07% of Florida's identified population, and the best-known schools together hold 0.077% nationally. GT would need 4.7 times the best capture rate on record and on a national scale (1.3). Together, these statistics indicate that GT's screening and gifted identification systems need to be improved for much further reach. The screener exists to find children who already clear the 95th percentile but were never surfaced.

**Insight 2 - The screener should only make recommendation decisions. not giving a recommendation shouldn't be treated as a "not qualified" decision since that recreates the gate the product exists to remove.**

- The professional testing standards claim that just modest precision is good enough for a short screener since the result can be corrected/supported early in the process (4.3). However, this only works if qualifying kids don't get screened out of the program. In that case, the kid is no longer able to take the gating test and it therefore "can provide no protection whatsoever" in correcting the screening error (2.2). Thus, the screener should only ever be able to make positive claims about a child's gifted potential, allowing the screener to correct its decision for qualified kids recieving a no recommendation result by the screener through asking more screening questions. The outcome can still be treated as a missed referral internally to help with sensitivity improvement, but that should never be used to make a decision against a student. Thus, a successful screener should be evaluated based on sensitivity and how many additional qualified applicants it refers to GT's applications, not a recommend/not-recommend accuracy value. In this framework, specificity and precision are only used to control the stop condition, meaning our screener should choose questions that improve the accuracy of that decision rather than closing around the kids current ability estimate.

**Insight 3 - Universal screening is not just a fairer version of referral-based screening. It is the only design that measures the full population gifted identification claims to serve.**
- Traditional gifted identification methods start by asking adults to refer children they feel have gifted talent to narrow the population of kids that need to get screened. However, this one step loses identificaiton for a majority of gifted kids. In Georgia, it was shown that referrals by parents put forward only 0.4% of children and all referred kids only made up 9.9% of the gifted population. This also exposed demographical biases with the referral process, with paid-lunch children being referred 3.7 times more than free-lunch peers (2.1). Studies have also demonstrated that the issue lies within the referral system itself, as no matter the adult referring, these probles remain (2.4). Even worse, when gifted identification is gated on referral, system sensitivity falls to between .280 and .453, compared with .843 when the same test is given to everyone (2.2). Thus, we cannot trust gating gifted screening behind traditional methods. Instead to capture the full population of gifted children, universal screening should be employed to measure every eligible child first. This approach has been shown to increase overall identification from 3.3% to 5.5% and even close the demographic gaps seen when using referral gates (2.3). That isn't to say referrals are useless, as they can still identify gifted kids that were missed by universal screening (the approximately 15% of them) and recommend them to GT's programs, but it should never gate screening or other avenues of gifted identification.

**Insight 4 - The tests are good enough: removing the referral gate has over three times the potential upside of perfecting the test.**

- The .280 figure from McBee, Peters & Miller (2016) does not represent a failure of the CogAT. It represents the CogAT placed behind a referral screen since the same test given to everyone increases sensitivity to .843 (2.2). This statistic puts a ceiling on both product strategies. Making the test perfect could recover at most the remaining 15.7 percentage points, while removing the referral gate recovers 56.3 percentage points without changing the test at all. That means removing the referral gate could gain up to 3.6 times larger returns over test refinement. Broward shows the same ordering on real children: with the eligibility bar unchanged, universal screening raised identification from 3.3% to 5.5% overall and from 1.4% to 4.3% among disadvantaged students (2.3). Test quality still matters though, since .843 sensitivity misses roughly one qualifying child in six. However, if the goal is to find more gifted children, the next unit of effort should go toward getting the existing measurement in front of more children before squeezing a few more points from the instrument. Critics counter that wider reach can overwhelm the screening with false positives, which is why precision remains a cost constraint even when sensitivity and distribution come first (4.3).

**Insight 5 - Fifteen minutes is enough to make a gifted screening recommendation, even though it is not enough to measure a child's exact ability.**

A full CogAT takes two to three hours because it is trying to produce a detailed ability profile, and even its short form takes around thirty to forty minutes to produce a composite score (4.5). However, our screener does not need either of those outputs. It only needs enough information to determine whether a child should be recommended to GT's application. Existing classification methods have made roughly 95% correct decisions in 12.7 to 16.3 questions, and adaptive mastery testing has reduced test length by 30% to 81% while still matching the full test's decision for 96% of candidates (4.1). This approach is already used in the real world by the nursing licensure exam, which stops once it is 95% certain that a candidate is on one side of its passing standard (4.2). Thus, existing technology supports the idea that our screener can make a useful recommendation within fifteen minutes by choosing questions around GT's cutoff and stopping once the recommendation is confident enough. However, this has not been directly proven on K-8 children at GT's cutoff. Fixed-form research has also shown sensitivity falling from .76 to .61 when a selective test shrinks from forty questions to fifteen (4.4). Therefore, fifteen minutes is a reasonable target for the product, but it still needs to be validated on real children before we can treat it as a fact.

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

### Overall Summary

There are 35,746,725 American children in K-8, of whom **1,787,336 clear GT's 95th-percentile CogAT cutoff**. The population is adequate and the constraint is capture. About 1,966,152 K-8 children are already identified as gifted under varying state and local standards, so 100,000 is 5.09% of that generous pool, against a record capture of 1.07% by the largest school for gifted children in the country. The reason so few are found is the referral: in a census of 705,074 Georgia elementary students, 9.9% were ever referred and parents initiated for 0.4%, with paid-lunch children referred 3.7 times as often, and nomination predicts the identifying tests at only .32 regardless of who does it. A nomination gate at the 90th percentile finds .453 of qualifying children with the TCS-2, .404 with the WISC-III and .280 with the CogAT, against .843 for the same test given to everyone. Broward County proved it on real children and the effect reversed when screening was defunded. The screener therefore has one substantive decision: recommend applying. "No recommendation" must remain an abstention because the later assessment cannot recover a qualified child who is blocked at the gate. At the 95th-percentile base rate, recommending nobody is 95% accurate while finding nobody, so sensitivity and additional qualified applicants matter more than overall accuracy; specificity and precision control follow-up cost. An announced test is also contaminated in ways a hidden one is not: coaching is worth 0.43 standard deviations on ability tests against 0.15 on the SAT, retesting 0.26 to 0.29 while shifting the construct toward memory, and test anxiety correlates with achievement at −.22 to −.25 across grades 1-8. Game-embedded measurement is promising and unproven, correlating with established tests at about .30 observed, with nothing validating it for high-stakes K-8 identification, while tweens spend 1 hour 27 minutes a day gaming against a 0.4% referral rate. On length, a defensible score needs about forty questions and a two-category adaptive classification reaches 95% correct decisions in 12.7 to 16.3, which is how the US nursing licensure exam already works. Standard error is the wrong objective: one test produced correct-classification rates from .909 to .991 with measurement error flat at 7.3.

### Category 2: Why Screeners Miss the Children They Exist to Find

- **Subcategory 2.1: Who ever gets put forward, measured on a whole state**
  - **Source:** McBee (2006), population file of Georgia elementary students from the state education department
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - All Georgia public elementary students in grades 1-5, 2004, **N = 705,074**. A population dataset, not a sample.
      - Only **9.9% were ever referred** by anybody. **90.1% never entered the process.**
      - **Parents initiated for 0.4%** of students. Self-nomination reached 0.01%, peer nomination less than that. Teachers referred 4.0%. An automatic trigger on an existing test score at or above the 90th percentile referred 5.2%.
      - Of children who were identified, **57.1% arrived via the automatic score trigger**, 37.7% via a teacher, 3.0% via a parent.
      - By lunch status, referral ran **4.15% for free-and-reduced against 15.49% for paid**, a ratio of about **3.7 to 1**. For parent referrals alone it was 0.13% against 0.66%.
      - By race, any nomination reached **23.02% of Asian, 4.58% of Black and 3.34% of Hispanic students**.
      - The author calls referral the "gate keeper" and concludes that "inequalities in nomination, rather than assessment, may be the primary source of underrepresentation."
      - Provenance: read through a full-text secondary retrieval rather than the publisher's copy.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest single piece of evidence here, because it is a whole state and it measures what people did rather than modelling what they might do. Nine children in ten were never put forward by anyone. Parents put forward four in a thousand. The inequity shows up before any child is measured, at nearly four to one by lunch status, which puts it in the referral rather than the instrument. And the largest path into identification was not a human noticing anything. It was a computer reading a score the child had already produced, which is universal screening by accident.
    - **Link to source:** [McBee (2006)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746049.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.2: What the gate costs, and where the 55% comes from**
  - **Source:** McBee, Peters & Miller (2016), *Gifted Child Quarterly* 60(4); McBee & Makel (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Method: an analytic psychometric model solved by numerical integration. **No children were involved.**
      - With no nomination stage, a single test at high reliability finds **.843** of qualifying children at a 90th-percentile cutoff and **.815** at the 95th. The authors call the first "as efficacious as a single-assessment system can get in the real world."
      - Add a nomination stage at a 90th-percentile cutoff and the system finds **.453 with the TCS-2, .404 with the WISC-III, .280 with the CogAT** — miss rates of **.547, .596 and .720**. **The 55% figure is the .547 row**, the mildest of the three.
      - Lowering the nomination cutoff helps without fixing it. With the CogAT behind the gate: **.441 at the 80th, .556 at the 70th, .709 at the 50th**.
      - From the abstract: "Under commonly implemented conditions, the nomination stage can cause the false negative rate to easily exceed 60%."
      - The mechanism, verbatim: "the confirmatory assessment can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives because those students that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory test." As a rule: "**screeners cannot improve the sensitivity of an assessment system; they can only reduce it.**"
      - In a separate scenario the authors fix nomination validity at **.70**, "about as high of a validity coefficient as one could expect to encounter," adding that "informal teacher nominations are probably much worse than this." Even there a 90th-percentile gate gives **.428**.
      - A second group states it about real systems: "the sensitivity of a typical identification process is on the order of **30% to 40%**," and most systems with a nomination stage "have missed larger percentages of gifted students then they have actually successfully identified."
      - The authors' recommendation: "Consider abolishing the two-stage system completely."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The step before the test costs several times what the test costs. A good instrument protects strongly against admitting the wrong child and does nothing at all against rejecting the right one, because a child filtered out at nomination never reaches it. The 55% we quote is conservative: it is the best of three worked examples at a common cutoff, while the same paper's abstract puts the typical case above 60% and an independent group puts real systems at 60% to 70% missed. In public the right phrasing is "a referral gate plus a conventional test misses roughly 55% to 72% depending on which test sits behind the gate, and we quote the low end."
    - **Link to source:** [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256) | [McBee & Makel (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007)

- **Subcategory 2.3: The district that removed the gate, then put it back**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2016), *PNAS* 113(48)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A large Florida district gave a nonverbal ability test to **every second grader** from spring 2005, replacing parent-and-teacher referral. **Eligibility standards were unchanged.** Sample: **n = 78,065** third graders across four cohorts.
      - Identification rose from **3.3% to 5.5%** overall and from **1.4% to 4.3%** among low-income and English-learner students, a **174% increase in the odds** for that group and 45% overall.
      - "**A full 20% of the compliers have IQs of 130 or higher**," the threshold applied to everyone else. The authors add that "the traditional referral system also misses some high-ability nondisadvantaged students."
      - Newly identified children looked nothing like the old group: **79%** were on the disadvantaged pathway against 21%, and only **29%** had English-speaking parents against 74%.
      - Before screening, only **13%** of disadvantaged third graders had an IQ on record. Private IQ testing cost **$500 to $1,000** and produced qualification for 20% of advantaged children against under 1% of disadvantaged ones.
      - Screening was suspended in 2011 for budget reasons and "the gifted share of third graders had returned to the level of 2004-2005." The authors say this "suggests that increased awareness cannot explain" the rise.
      - Their conclusion on cause: underrepresentation was "caused by the failure of the traditional parent/teacher referral system."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Hold the bar exactly where it was, test everyone, and identification rises by two thirds overall and triples among disadvantaged children, with a fifth of the new kids already clearing the strict threshold. Then the money stopped and the numbers went back. That reversal is what makes this causal rather than suggestive, because a change that unwinds when a purchase order lapses was administrative, not developmental. The children were there before and after. The only thing that changed was whether anyone looked.
    - **Link to source:** [Card & Giuliano (2016), PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113) | [Free full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED578122.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.4: Asking an adult barely works, and it does not matter which adult**
  - **Source:** Marsili & Pellegrini (2022); Peters & Johnson (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 29 studies, nomination correlates with the traditional measures used to identify giftedness at **.32**, 95% CI [.25, .39].
      - By source: **teacher .31** on 97 effect sizes, **parent .30** on 27, **self .33** on 13, **peer .27** on 7. Nomination form was **not a significant moderator**.
      - The prediction interval runs **−.12 to .79** and includes zero, so individual settings can show no relationship at all.
      - This measured value is **less than half** the .70 the referral-gate model used as an optimistic ceiling, so the modelled losses in 2.2 are conservative.
      - Where it fails hardest: across **29,051 schools enrolling ten or more English learners, the median Representation Index was zero**, meaning the typical such school identifies no English learners as gifted at all. Same result for students with disabilities across 43,094 schools.
      - National representation indices for 2016: **Black .57, Latinx .70, English learners .27, students under IDEA .21**.
      - Schools that did reach proportional English-learner representation "all implemented universal screening in one or more grade levels."
      - Provenance: the meta-analysis's table and narrative disagree by .01 to .02 in places, and the self and peer estimates rest on 13 and 7 effect sizes.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Nomination predicts weakly, and changing who nominates does not help, since teachers, parents, peers and children all land within .06 of each other. The ceiling is the task of judging ability by watching a child, not whose watching it is, which closes off the obvious fix. Training teachers or adding parents does not repair a method whose limit is the method. And in the typical American school with a gifted programme and a substantial English-learner population, not one of those children is in it, which is absence rather than underrepresentation.
    - **Link to source:** [Marsili & Pellegrini (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1177/01430343221105398) | [Peters & Johnson (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1177/23328584241258480)

### Category 3: What an Announced Test Measures Besides Ability

- **Subcategory 3.1: Coaching and retesting**
  - **Source:** Kulik, Bangert-Drowns & Kulik (1984); Becker (1990); Hausknecht et al. (2007); Scharfen et al. (2018); Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad (2007); Schleicher et al. (2010)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Meta-analysis of **38 coaching studies**: coaching raised SAT scores **0.15 standard deviations** across 14 studies, and **other aptitude and intelligence tests 0.43 standard deviations** across 24. Among the 17 non-SAT studies using a pretest, the coaching-only estimate was **0.51**. A separate SAT-specific meta-analysis puts the SAT effect at about 0.09 verbal and 0.16 mathematics.
      - Retesting on cognitive ability tests is worth **d = .26** across 50 studies and N = 134,436, and about **.29** across 174 samples and N = 153,185. On an **identical form** it roughly doubles, corrected **d = 0.46 against 0.24**. Between **25% and 50%** of applicants retest.
      - In a study of **941** examinees, the retest factor was "**less saturated with g**" and "**more associated with memory**," and these changes "**eliminated the test's criterion-related validity**."
      - Retest gains by group on a verbal ability test: **d = 0.12 White, 0.04 Black, 0.06 Hispanic, 0.04 Asian**.
      - Between **9% and 60%** of a measured retest gain is regression toward the mean rather than real improvement, and gains plateau after the third administration.
      - Provenance: these are adult and adolescent studies, mostly job selection. The larger practice-effect meta-analysis excluded mean ages under 12, and **no practice-effect meta-analysis covering ages 5 to 11 was located**.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The test that markets itself as measuring reasoning rather than schooling is the more coachable of the two, by roughly three to one. That is the opposite of the intuition the category rests on, and it matters because a gifted screener at a high cutoff is exactly where a fraction of a standard deviation decides an outcome. Retesting compounds it and does something worse than inflate: the second score is measurably less about general ability and more about remembering the test, to the point that in one large study it stopped predicting anything. The gain is also larger for children whose families are already advantaged. None of it has been measured on elementary-age children, which is the honest limit.
    - **Link to source:** [Kulik, Bangert-Drowns & Kulik (1984)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.95.2.179) | [Becker (1990)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060003373) | [Hausknecht et al. (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373) | [Scharfen et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003) | [Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672) | [Schleicher et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018920)

- **Subcategory 3.2: Test anxiety, measured on the right children**
  - **Source:** von der Embse, Jester, Roy & Patrick (2018); Robson et al. (2023); Hembree (1988)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A meta-analysis of roughly **238 studies** puts the correlation between test anxiety and achievement at about **−.22 across grades 1-5** and **−.25 across grades 6-8**.
      - A twenty-year meta-analysis of **53,617 children aged 5-12** confirms the relationship in primary school specifically.
      - The foundational synthesis of **562 studies** found the "worry" component matters more for performance than physical "emotionality."
      - Item order affects it: difficult-before-easy ordering raises physiologically measured stress and state anxiety in adjacent populations.
      - In children aged 7-9 (n = 44), time pressure **without a visible timer** produced higher evaluation anxiety and lower maths scores, while showing the timer **reduced** anticipatory anxiety with no significant performance change.
      - For calibration, the classic stereotype-threat effect shrinks to about **−.14 standard deviations** under realistic operational testing conditions.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Anxiety is a known downward push on the score, measured on exactly the age band a gifted screener serves, and the relationship is consistent rather than marginal. So the framing around a test is not neutral packaging. It moves the number. Anything structural that lowers anxiety can therefore raise measurement quality without making the test easier, which is rare. The reverse also holds: telling an eight-year-old this test decides which school they attend is an intervention on the score, not just on the child.
    - **Link to source:** [von der Embse et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.11.048) | [Robson et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2023.02.003) | [Hembree (1988)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001047)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Effort, and the honest state of the motivation evidence**
  - **Source:** Wise & DeMars (2005); Wise & Kong (2005); Wise & DeMars (2006); the 2025 retraction of Duckworth et al. (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across **12 studies and 25 comparisons**, motivated examinees outscored less-motivated ones by an average of **g = 0.59**, ranging from −0.04 to 1.49, with 24 of 25 favouring the motivated group.
      - Response Time Effort classifies each answer as a real attempt or a rapid guess by comparing response time to a threshold. Internal consistency **α = .97**; it correlates about .25 with self-reported effort and **near zero with SAT scores**, so it captures effort rather than ability.
      - Rapid guesses are answered too fast for the item to have been read and are correct only at chance rates. Treating them as missing **lowered reliability but raised validity**, and an effort-conditioned item-response model produced better fit and higher convergent validity than the standard model. Items with more text, or later in the test, produced more rapid guessing.
      - The best-known evidence that incentives raise IQ scores, a 2011 meta-analysis reporting **g = 0.64**, was **retracted on 1 May 2025** after a fraudulent 1978 study contributing 24% of its sample was itself retracted. Removing it dropped the effect to **g = 0.47**, and correcting for now-strong publication bias dropped it to **g = 0.16, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.34**, which includes zero.
      - A separate synthesis found interventions designed to improve test-taking effort raised effort and performance by only about **0.13 standard deviations** each.
      - Cutting the other way: deleting unmotivated examinees **inflated the true mean by as much as 0.42 standard deviations** when ability and careless responding were related, so effort filtering can overcorrect. And nearly all of this evidence comes from college and adult low-stakes testing, not children.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The strong version of the motivation argument is gone. The headline finding that incentives add nine IQ points was built partly on fabricated data, and the corrected estimate cannot be told apart from zero. What survives is narrower and still useful: people who are trying score about half a standard deviation higher than people who are not, and disengagement leaves a fingerprint in response times that can be filtered out. Engagement is best defended as a way of keeping noise out of the estimate and children in the sample, not as a way of raising anyone's ceiling. Anyone about to argue that a game measures better because kids try harder should hear about the retraction first.
    - **Link to source:** [Wise & DeMars (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326977ea1001_1) | [Wise & Kong (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2) | [Wise & DeMars (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x) | [Retraction for Duckworth et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2508040122)

- **Subcategory 3.4: What game-embedded measurement can and cannot show, and where children already are**
  - **Source:** Shute and colleagues on stealth assessment; Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond (2003); Bipp et al. (2024); Aneni et al. (2023); Ohlms, Hohner & Melchers (2025); Slattery et al. (2025); Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Common Sense Census 2021
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Stealth assessment embeds measurement inside a game and scores how the child plays rather than only the final answer. It runs on evidence-centred design, which works backwards from the claim you want to make to the behaviours that would justify it.
      - Across the stealth-assessment literature, correlations between in-game estimates and external measures of the same competency "yield values between **0.1 and 0.6**."
      - A meta-analysis of **52 samples and over 6,100 adults** puts the correlation between game-based cognitive measures and established ability tests at about **.30 observed, or .45 corrected**, ranging −0.35 to +0.75. The authors say this "raises questions about whether" the two capture the same construct.
      - A systematic review of child game-based assessment covering **19 articles, 20 studies, 18 games and 378 correlations** found 75% statistically significant, of which 80% were low-to-medium.
      - Against it: **video-game experience predicts game-based assessment scores without predicting academic performance**. Enjoyment significantly affects at least one key scoring feature. A cluster randomised trial of **885 children** found no overall effect of a game intervention on spatial thinking.
      - Tangible performance-contingent rewards **reduce** children's freely chosen interest by **0.28 to 0.40 standard deviations** and are "more detrimental for children than college students," so points and badges tied to performance are contraindicated in a children's screener.
      - The load-bearing gap: **no located study validates game-embedded measurement for high-stakes K-8 gifted identification.** The field positions it as a formative, low-stakes tool, and gamifying an existing task creates a new validity argument rather than inheriting the old one.
      - On reach: **tweens aged 8-12 spent an average of 1 hour 27 minutes a day gaming** in 2021, with **64% playing online video games** on a typical day. Boys averaged 6 hours 11 minutes of total screen media a day against 4 hours 55 minutes for girls.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The method is legitimate and two decades old, the convergence is real but moderate, and this particular application has never been validated. A correlation of .30 means related but not interchangeable, which is fine for something that only recommends and fatal for anything that decides. The serious problem is not weak convergence but the new bias: if game experience predicts the score without predicting achievement, a game screener has swapped a bias against children whose parents do not refer them for a bias against children who do not play. What the channel buys is not in dispute, though. An hour and a half a day of voluntary participation against four referrals per thousand is a different order of reach, and the gender skew inside it is the warning attached.
    - **Link to source:** [Shute et al. (2023)](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/ETRD2023.pdf) | [Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15366359MEA0101_02) | [Bipp et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence12120129) | [Aneni et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.pbr.2023.02.002) | [Ohlms, Hohner & Melchers (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1111/apps.70038) | [Slattery et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1002/rev3.70035) | [Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627) | [Common Sense Census 2021](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/8-18-census-integrated-report-final-web_0.pdf)

### Category 4: Deciding at the Cutoff Instead of Measuring a Score

- **Subcategory 4.1: How few questions a decision needs, against how many a score needs**
  - **Source:** Eggen (1999); Thompson (2011); Eggen & Straetmans (2000); Weiss (1982); Kingsbury & Weiss (1979); Kruyen, Emons & Sijtsma (2012)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A trustworthy score is expensive. With realistic item parameters, coefficient alpha runs about **.90 at 40 items, .70 at 10, and .50 to .55 at 5**. Forcing a 5-item test to .70 would need item discriminations of 1.6 to 2.9, which the authors call "unrealistically high."
      - A trustworthy decision is cheap. Simulating 5,000 examinees on a 250-item bank, a two-category classification needed **12.7 to 13.2 items for 94.8% to 95.3% correct decisions** at 10% error rates, and **15.9 to 16.3 items for 94.6% to 95.6%** at 5%. All five item-selection methods landed in that range.
      - The biggest lever on length was not precision but the width of the indifference zone, the band around the cutoff you agree not to resolve. Widening it means "shorter tests can be used to make a decision."
      - Simulating 10,000 examinees, sequential classification reached **95.7% correct in 37.6 to 39.3 items** against **93.5% for a 50-item fixed test**. The author's summary: "the 50-item fixed test entailed approximately as many items as the variable-length methods, but with notably decreased accuracy."
      - Adaptive mastery testing cut average length by **30% to 81%** "while reaching the same decision as the conventional procedure for **96%** of the trainees." For high-confidence candidates the modal length was **3 items**.
      - Adaptive testing generally needs about **half the items** of a conventional test for equal reliability. Replacing a paper placement test with an adaptive one gave "**a reduction of at least 22%**," and those authors concluded "statistical testing is a promising alternative to statistical estimation."
      - The counter-recommendation from the fixed-form literature: at least **20 items** for group-level decision quality and **40** for individual decisions.
      - Provenance: all simulations, on adult or unspecified populations. **No study of a short adaptive classification test on children at a 95th-percentile cut was located.** That is the biggest gap under SPOV 4.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Measuring how able a child is and deciding which side of a line they fall on carry price tags about an order of magnitude apart. Ten questions cannot give you a defensible score. Thirteen can give you a 95%-correct decision, because a question aimed at the cutoff carries far more decision-relevant information than one aimed at estimating a value. The two literatures disagree about design rather than arithmetic: adaptive classification targets the cut and stops early, while the fixed-form warning tests nested subsets of a full-length form. Neither has tested the other, and nobody has tested either on eight-year-olds at a selective bar.
    - **Link to source:** [Eggen (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1177/01466219922031365) | [Thompson (2011)](https://doi.org/10.7275/wq8m-zk25) | [Eggen & Straetmans (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1177/00131640021970862) | [Weiss (1982)](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662168200600408) | [Kingsbury & Weiss (1979)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED209336.pdf) | [Kruyen, Emons & Sijtsma (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15305058.2011.643517)

- **Subcategory 4.2: A live, high-stakes test that already works this way**
  - **Source:** National Council of State Boards of Nursing, NCLEX computerized adaptive testing documentation
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The US nursing licensure exam is a variable-length adaptive test producing a **pass or fail decision, not a score**.
      - Its main stopping rule, which the board calls "the most common for NCLEX candidates," is the **95% confidence interval rule**: "the computer will stop administering items when it is 95% certain that the candidate's ability is clearly above or clearly below the passing standard."
      - The board defines the passing standard as "a cut point along an ability range that marks the minimum ability level requirement."
      - A result can be determined at the **minimum length of 85 questions**, and both minimum-length passes and fails "occur quite frequently." Only candidates too close to the standard continue to the maximum of **150**, at which point "the computer disregards the 95% confidence interval rule and considers only the final ability estimate."
      - The sister practical-nurse exam runs the same design at **60 operational items minimum and 120 maximum**, and its documentation says the exam "can stop with minimum number of questions if your ability is far above or below the passing standard."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the existence proof, and it beats any simulation because it has run at national scale for years where a wrong decision matters far more than a gifted referral. It does everything SPOV 4 describes: name the line first, buy only the information that says which side of it you are on, stop the moment the decision is safe, and fall back to a score only for genuinely ambiguous candidates. Worth noting what it does not do. It treats pass and fail symmetrically, because a licensure decision is symmetric. A screener that only recommends is not, and can afford to be lopsided.
    - **Link to source:** [NCLEX computerized adaptive testing](https://www.nclex.com/computerized-adaptive-testing.page)

- **Subcategory 4.3: Standard error is the wrong target, and the cost of a miss is the right one**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014) *Standards*; Livingston & Lewis (1995); Rudner (2001); Ippel & Magis (2020); Metz (1978); Youden (1950); Meehl & Rosen (1955); WHO (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - From the professional standards: test takers "**far above or far below the cut score**… can have considerable error in their observed scores without any effect on their classification decisions," and "**the standard error cannot be translated into the expected percentage of consistent or accurate decisions without strong assumptions**."
      - Decision accuracy has four inputs, only one of which is reliability, and "the same reliability coefficient yields different decision accuracy and decision consistency values at different cut-points. **The reliability coefficient alone does not determine either statistic.**"
      - Empirically: seven cut points on one test produced correct-classification rates from **.909 to .991 while the conditional standard error stayed roughly flat at 7.29 to 7.40**.
      - A specific trap for short adaptive tests: at 5 items the asymptotic standard error used in most stopping rules "**overestimates the SE for those test takers with abilities at the either end of the scale**," and the exact SE's advantage is "especially clear at the extreme ends" even at 10 items.
      - The standards permit lower precision for provisional decisions: "**If a decision can and will be corroborated by information from other sources or if an erroneous initial decision can be easily corrected, scores with more modest reliability/precision may suffice**," and "short screening tests may be used to reduce the pool."
      - On unequal error costs, the optimal threshold is the false-positive share of total error cost: "**if false negatives cost three times as much as false positives, the threshold is one quarter.** If costs are symmetric, it is one half."
      - Youden's J, the standard statistic for picking a diagnostic cutoff, carries an assumption its users skip. In Youden's words: "**This assumes false positives to be as undesirable as false negatives.**"
      - The WHO sets tuberculosis screening minimums at "**an overall sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 70%**," compares tools by fixing each at 90% sensitivity, and states that screening tests "are not intended to provide a definitive diagnosis" and "need to be followed by a diagnostic test."
      - The constraint on all of it: a screening device "must make possible a greater number of correct decisions than could be made in terms of the base rates alone," and the threshold rule is only optimal if the probability it is applied to is calibrated.
      - At GT's 95th-percentile cutoff, classifying every child as below the bar yields **95% overall accuracy while finding none of the qualifying children**. This is arithmetic on the denominator in 1.1, not a published study.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Standard error is not a decision metric and overall accuracy is the wrong objective for a one-sided screener. Two children with identical measurement error can face completely different odds of misclassification depending only on where they sit relative to the line, while a system that recommends nobody can look 95% accurate and still fail completely. Once decision accuracy is tied to the actual use, unequal error costs follow: a qualified child who receives no recommendation is a missed referral, whereas an unqualified child who receives one proceeds to confirmation. Medicine handles the same structure by fixing the sensitivity it requires and taking whatever specificity results. The discipline attached is the base rate, since at a low base rate most flags may be wrong, which is acceptable only when a flag invites review and a non-flag never denies access.
    - **Link to source:** [AERA, APA & NCME Standards (2014)](https://www.testingstandards.net/) | [Livingston & Lewis (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x) | [Rudner (2001)](https://doi.org/10.7275/an9m-2035) | [Ippel & Magis (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164419882072) | [Metz (1978)](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0001-2998%2878%2980014-2) | [Youden (1950)](https://doi.org/10.1002/1097-0142%281950%293:1%3C32::AID-CNCR2820030106%3E3.0.CO;2-3) | [Meehl & Rosen (1955)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048070) | [WHO (2021), Screening Module 2](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK569339/)

- **Subcategory 4.4: The strongest objection, which is that short tests buy brevity out of sensitivity**
  - **Source:** Kruyen, Emons & Sijtsma (2012), *International Journal of Testing* 12(4); Kingsbury & Weiss (1979)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - At a **10% selection ratio**, shortening a test from **40 items to 15** moved specificity only from **.97 to .96** but moved sensitivity from **.76 to .61**.
      - At 5 items sensitivity fell to **.46**, and "compared to 40-item tests, **5-item tests additionally rejected 28% of all suited applicants**."
      - The probability of a correct positive decision at a demanding standard collapsed with length: **.34 at 40 items, .10 at 15, .02 at 5**.
      - The authors recommend "at least **20 items**… at the group level, and at least **40 items** if correct decisions on the individual level are needed," and warn that "**even if short tests satisfy minimum-reliability requirements recommended in standard textbooks, individuals may run a high risk to be incorrectly rejected or accepted.**"
      - Limit on the objection's reach: this study nests shorter forms inside a fixed-length form and does not adapt item selection to the cutoff, which is the mechanism the classification literature relies on. The two are not directly comparable and have not been tested against each other.
      - A related finding cutting the same way: in adaptive mastery testing, the higher the standard, the cheaper it got to confirm a failure and the more expensive to confirm a pass. At the strictest criterion, everyone declared a master had taken every item in the bank.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This has to be answered rather than dodged, and it says something precise: at a selective bar a short test loses almost nothing on the reject side and a lot on the accept side, which is exactly the error a tool built to stop missing children cannot afford. Two things blunt it. The design tested is fixed-form rather than adaptive-at-the-cutoff, so it measures a different mechanism. And the loss is asymmetric in a way a screener can exploit rather than suffer, by demanding far more confidence to rule a child out than to put them forward. The underlying warning still stands. Shortness is not free, and anyone claiming a fifteen-minute screener has to show the sensitivity number, not the reliability number.

- **Subcategory 4.5: What the incumbent costs in time, and what a prototype built this way does**
  - **Source:** Riverside Insights CogAT brochure and administration directions; the GT screener prototype and its simulation harness
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The CogAT Screening Form is the shortest thing the incumbent publisher sells for this job, consisting of "the first subtest from each of the three batteries on the complete test." The brochure claims "an initial data point in only **30 minutes**"; the administration directions sum higher, to 39, 37 and 41 minutes across levels 5/6 to 8, plus sign-in, directions and a tutorial.
      - The full CogAT battery is nine subtests and takes approximately **two to three hours**. The publisher says the short form is for schools that "intend to use CogAT scores solely to screen students," and that it provides "a reliable anchor for other measures."
      - The prototype holds a Bayesian distribution over ability, updates it after each answer with a three-parameter item response model, and picks each next question to **maximise information at the decision threshold rather than at the running ability estimate**.
      - It stops when the probability of being above the threshold reaches **0.75** (recommend) or falls to **0.03** (no recommendation), or at the item cap. Minimum 8 questions, maximum 16, with a blueprint requiring quantitative, verbal, spatial and fluid coverage before an early stop can fire.
      - The two confidence levels are deliberately unequal, and the code says why: a symmetric threshold "fires 'below' on the prior rather than on anything the candidate did," because the prior probability of clearing a demanding bar is already small. A regression test confirms the asymmetric rule beats the symmetric one on sensitivity.
      - Output is a probability of being above threshold, a 90% credible interval, and either **recommend** or **no recommendation**. **No percentile, no IQ.** The interface turns the first into "Worth applying to GT" and tells families the second "does not tell us enough," is "not a verdict," and does not prevent them from applying.
      - The simulation nevertheless evaluates the pair as two classes: a candidate above the threshold who receives no recommendation is counted as a false negative. That is correct for measuring a **missed referral**, but it must not be read as a valid claim that the child is below the ability bar.
      - On **600 simulated general-population candidates**: sensitivity **72.7%**, specificity **97.1%**, precision **81.0%**, median **8** questions. On **600 simulated self-selected candidates**: **63.8%**, **90.2%**, **87.0%**, median **13**.
      - The bank holds **7,319 records across 53 question types**, of which **4,534 are automatically markable**.
      - The current prototype uses an ability threshold of **1.0 logits**, described in its configuration as roughly the top sixth of a standard normal population and deliberately looser than an admissions bar. Its simulated sensitivity and specificity therefore describe that prototype threshold, not performance at GT's 95th-percentile CogAT cutoff.
      - **Every performance figure above is simulated.** Responses come from the item response model with a 5% slip and 2% guess rate, item difficulties are synthetic and uncalibrated on children, and the repository says so plainly: "No real child has taken this." Effectiveness statistics in the app return "not available" rather than zero until a real outcome is attached.
      - Fifteen minutes is a **design target** in the product proposal, not a measured duration. No timing study on children has been run, and what is built today is a plain web app rather than a game.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The incumbent's short form takes thirty to forty minutes plus overhead and needs a proctor, so a fifteen-minute, no-proctor recommendation engine would be materially cheaper. The mechanism has been built and behaves the way the classification literature predicts, reaching an output in single-digit questions for most simulated candidates. Its semantics are already one-sided: recommendation is an invitation and no recommendation is explicitly not a verdict. Evaluation has to preserve that distinction. A qualified child with no recommendation is a miss for sensitivity, but no recommendation can never become a rejection. Two gaps remain before the product supports its intended claim: the prototype threshold is looser than GT's 95th-percentile cutoff, and every item parameter and performance figure is simulated rather than calibrated on children.
