# DOK 1 — What Actually Predicts Success in an Intensive Accelerated Program, Beyond Cognitive Ability

Fact extraction only. Every number below was read out of the source named beside it. Exact quotes are in
quotation marks. Anything I could not verify is flagged inline and listed again in the final section.

Every DOI in this document was checked against the Crossref registry and returned a matching record with the
author list, journal, volume, pages and year reproduced here. Every non-DOI full-text URL was checked and
returns HTTP 200, with two exceptions: `journals.sagepub.com` and `hep.gse.harvard.edu` return HTTP 403 to
command-line requests. That is bot-blocking, not a dead link — both pages were retrieved successfully by other
means. Where a publisher blocks access entirely (Taylor & Francis, ScienceDirect, APA PsycNet), the source is
flagged as abstract-only.

**The applicability question that recurs throughout.** The population of interest is students above the 95th
percentile on a cognitive test, selected into an accelerated program, with far-above-grade-level attainment as
the outcome. Almost every predictor below was validated on an average-ability, general-population sample with
grade-level GPA or a state achievement test as the outcome. Two things break at the top of the distribution:
range restriction attenuates any predictor correlated with ability, and the outcome ceiling changes what
"success" even means. Where a study speaks directly to the high-ability case, I say so. Where it does not, I
say that too.

## 1. Spatial ability as the neglected predictor

### 1.1 Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2009) — the primary source

**Source.** Wai, J., Lubinski, D., & Benbow, C. P. (2009). "Spatial ability for STEM domains: Aligning over 50
years of cumulative psychological knowledge solidifies its importance." *Journal of Educational Psychology*,
101(4), 817–835. DOI [10.1037/a0016127](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016127). Free full text:
[Vanderbilt PDF](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/826/2013/02/Wai2009SpatialAbility.pdf).

**Sample.** Project TALENT. A stratified random sample of the U.S. high school population, grades 9–12,
initial data collection 1960, "roughly 50,000 males and 50,000 females per grade level, for a total N of
approximately 400,000." Followed up 1, 5 and 11 years after high school graduation; the analyses here use the
11-year follow-up. **General population, not a selected sample.** Aligned against two other datasets:
Graduate Record Examination takers tested July 1, 2002–June 30, 2005 (1,245,872 for GRE-Mathematical;
1,245,878 for GRE-Verbal), and the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth.

**The headline "who gets missed" number.** Verbatim:

> "Within the three ability composites assembled for this study, **70% of the top 1% in spatial ability did not
> make the cut for the top 1% on either the math or the verbal composite**; yet, these individuals are highly
> talented in spatial ability."

**The second, larger number — and the one calibrated to an actual talent-search cutoff.** Verbatim:

> "in Project TALENT, **over half of participants in the top 1% on the Spatial Composite were below the top 3%
> cut on both the Mathematical and Verbal Composites**, and, thus, they would not be invited to participate in
> modern talent searches."

The context the authors give for why the 1% and 3% thresholds matter: "Some summer residential programs for
talented youths require scores in the top 1% on either verbal or mathematical ability measures to ensure
readiness to take advantage of the fast-paced learning demands in their programs, and some even require scores
in the top 0.5%."

**Incremental validity over math and verbal.** From footnote 2, describing multiple regressions in which
mathematical and verbal ability were entered first and spatial ability second, predicting a dummy-coded
STEM/non-STEM criterion across four panels (three terminal degrees plus occupations):

> "The incremental validity of spatial ability over mathematical ability and verbal ability for all four panels
> was statistically significant, as anticipated, and **the multiple-R² increment averaged .04 (accounting for an
> additional 4% of criterion variance)**."

**Spatial ability level and advanced STEM credentials.** Verbatim:

> "**45% of all those holding STEM PhDs were in Stanine 9 (or within the top 4%) on spatial ability 11 years
> earlier, and nearly 90% were in Stanine 7 or above. That is, less than 10% of those holding STEM PhDs were
> below the top quartile in spatial ability during adolescence.**"

By comparison, "about 30% of those holding STEM terminal master's degrees and 25% of those holding STEM
terminal bachelor's degrees were in Stanine 9."

**Authors' stated conclusions.** From the abstract: "including spatial ability in modern talent searches would
identify many adolescents with potential for STEM who are currently being missed." From the discussion: "con-
temporary talent searches miss many intellectually talented students by restricting selection criteria to
mathematical and verbal ability measures." And from the results: "This potential, currently being missed,
constitutes a rather sizable pool of untapped talent. Among those in the top 1% in spatial ability but not in
the top 1% in mathematical or verbal ability, a large proportion earned STEM and visual arts degrees and
entered STEM and visual arts occupations well beyond base rate expectations."

**The authors' own supply-side explanation, which is separate from the measurement claim:** "there is reason
to believe that the educational needs of spatially talented youths are more unmet than those of mathematically
or verbally talented youths, because the typical middle and high school curriculum has many more opportunities
for developing mathematical and verbal ability than spatial ability."

### 1.2 Webb, Lubinski & Benbow (2007) — incremental validity inside an already-selected sample, and a numeric discrepancy worth recording

**Source.** Webb, R. M., Lubinski, D., & Benbow, C. P. (2007). "Spatial ability: A neglected dimension in
talent searches for intellectually precocious youth." *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 99(2), 397–420. DOI
[10.1037/0022-0663.99.2.397](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.2.397). Free full text:
[Vanderbilt PDF](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/826/2013/02/WebbJEP2007.pdf).

**Sample, from the primary source.** Adolescents enrolled in "1-week summer enrichment classes or 3-week
accelerative educational opportunities designed for mathematically or verbally precocious youth." The ability
range is described precisely, and precisely matters here:

> "Although all participants were well above average in general intellectual ability (**all had scored in the
> top 3% on at least one subtest of a conventional achievement test** administered by their school), **the large
> majority did not meet the conventional selection criteria established by most talent search programs (i.e.,
> scoring in the top 0.5% of their age group on either mathematical or verbal reasoning ability)**. This
> provided us with a sufficiently large range of talent to test our hypotheses about an untapped talent pool."

Time 1 (≈ age 13): **1,060 participants** (617 boys, 443 girls); 88% White, 8% Asian, 1% Black, 2% other, 1%
unknown. Only 409 had taken the SAT. Time 2 (≈ age 18, 5 years later): ~93% located, **547 returned
questionnaires** (281 men, 266 women). Phase 3 — the analysis that controls for the SAT — ran on subsamples of
**159 to 211**.

**Phase 2 result (spatial ability over preferences only).** "In all 10 DFAs, spatial ability provided a
statistically significant improvement over preferences alone in predicting whether favorite and least favorite
high school course, leisure activities, undergraduate major, and anticipated occupation were in math–science,
humanities, or in other groups by **explaining an additional 1%–5% of the variance in group membership.**"

**Phase 3 result — the one that controls for the SAT, and the one that matters. Verbatim, with the authors'
own qualification attached:**

> "As hypothesized, spatial ability exhibited incremental validity over both the SAT + RIASEC and the SAT +
> SOV; **the magnitude of this increment averaged 2.4% over the 10 sets of analyses in Phase 3. However,
> because the number of variables controlled for before evaluating the incremental validity of spatial ability
> was atypically stringent and the sample sizes were relatively small (ranging from 159 to 211), only 4 of the
> 10 statistical tests achieved statistical significance.** Nevertheless, the uniformity found in the
> discriminant function structure matrices suggests that more stable and statistically significant
> contributions of spatial ability beyond the SAT + preferences **may be secured in subsequent studies using
> larger samples**."

The authors' own epistemic status statement, in their words: "In the early stages of testing psychological
theories, consistency in function form is more germane than statistical significance. **We are clearly in the
early stages of theory testing here.**"

**Numeric discrepancy to put on the record.** Wai et al. (2009) summarize this study as showing that "spatial
ability accounted for an additional 3% of the variance in predicting these criteria beyond both SAT measures
and two comprehensive educational–vocational preference questionnaires." **The primary source says 2.4%, not
3%, and says that only 4 of the 10 tests producing that average were statistically significant.** Cite Webb et
al.'s own number and its qualifier, not Wai et al.'s rounding of it.

**Why this is still the most transferable result in the section.** It is the only incremental-validity estimate
in this literature computed inside a sample already selected on ability, and Phase 3 restricts further to
participants who had taken the SAT — i.e., it asks exactly the right question. The honest reading is that the
answer is a small, mostly non-significant 2.4%, on educational–vocational choice criteria rather than
performance, in subsamples of about 200.

### 1.3 Lubinski (2010)

**Source.** Lubinski, D. (2010). "Spatial ability and STEM: A sleeping giant for talent identification and
development." *Personality and Individual Differences*, 49(4), 344–351. DOI
[10.1016/j.paid.2010.03.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2010.03.022). Free full text:
[IAP PDF](http://www.iapsych.com/articles/lubinski2010.pdf).

- Reproduces the Project TALENT incremental-validity result: "across all three analyses, spatial ability
  accounted for an average of 4% additional variance beyond mathematical and verbal ability."
- Abstract, the strongest normative claim in this literature: "**selecting students for advanced learning
  opportunities in STEM without considering spatial ability might be iatrogenic.**"
- The mechanism he proposes for that word: "**Verbal ability could be operating as a suppressor variable and
  systematically precluding through indirect selection students exceptionally talented in spatial ability but
  relatively unimpressive in verbal ability**; that is, many of these unselected students may be truly
  exceptional in reasoning with forms, patterns, and shapes."
- Base-rate context for how much discrimination is available *within* the top percentiles: "the base rate for
  earning a doctorate in the US is 1%... In 20-year follow-up studies of adolescents identified by age 13, for
  example, **30% of participants scoring SAT-M or SAT-V ≥ 500 secured doctorates, compared to 50% for those
  scoring ≥ 700**."

**Applicability note for §1.** This is the one predictor in this document whose evidence base is *about* the
top few percent rather than about the general population. Project TALENT itself is general-population, but the
Talent Search and SMPY samples used for the incremental-validity work are top-1%-to-top-3% samples. That makes
the transfer argument much stronger here than anywhere else below. The corresponding weakness is that the
criterion throughout is STEM degree/occupation attainment 5–20 years out, not performance in an accelerated
academic program.

## 2. Conscientiousness and self-discipline

### 2.1 Poropat (2009) — the five-factor meta-analysis

**Source.** Poropat, A. E. (2009). "A meta-analysis of the five-factor model of personality and academic
performance." *Psychological Bulletin*, 135(2), 322–338. DOI
[10.1037/a0014996](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014996). Free full text:
[gwern PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2009-poropat.pdf).

**Sample.** Aggregate samples from 58,522 (Agreeableness) to 70,926 (Conscientiousness). "Most analyzed
studies came from the tertiary level of education, but there were similar aggregate samples from secondary and
tertiary education. **There was a comparatively smaller sample derived from studies at the primary level.**"
Outcome is grades/GPA throughout. Compulsory-education systems; countries not enumerated in the text I read.

**Table 1 — the full result.** `r` = sample-weighted correlation; `ρ` = corrected for scale reliability;
`ρ_g` = the same correlation as a partial, controlled for intelligence.

| Predictor | k | N | r | ρ | Cohen's d | Grade diff. | ρ controlling for intelligence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agreeableness | 109 | 58,522 | .07 | .07 | 0.14 | 0.10 | .07 |
| **Conscientiousness** | 138 | 70,926 | **.19** | **.22** | **0.46** | **0.31** | **.24** |
| Emotional Stability | 114 | 59,554 | .01 | .02 | 0.03 | 0.02 | .01 |
| Extraversion | 113 | 59,986 | .01 | .01 | 0.02 | 0.01 | .01 |
| Openness | 113 | 60,442 | .10 | .12 | 0.24 | 0.16 | .09 |
| Intelligence | 47 | 31,955 | .23 | .25 | 0.52 | 0.35 | — |

All estimates significant at p < .001.

**Table 2 — the moderation by academic level, which is the most decision-relevant part of the paper.** These
are ρ (corrected for scale reliability).

| | k | N | ρ with Conscientiousness | | k | N | ρ with intelligence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary education | 8 | 3,196 | **.28** | | 4 | 1,791 | **.58** |
| Secondary education | 35 | 31,980 | .21 | | 17 | 12,606 | .24 |
| Tertiary education | 92 | 32,887 | .23 | | 26 | 17,588 | .23 |

Conscientiousness is the only Big Five dimension that does *not* decline from primary to later levels:
"there was a decline in strength of correlations of academic performance with each FFM dimension, apart from
Conscientiousness, from primary to secondary level and from primary to tertiary level."

**Authors' stated conclusions.**
- Abstract: "Where tested, correlations between Conscientiousness and academic performance were largely
  independent of intelligence. **When secondary academic performance was controlled for, Conscientiousness
  added as much to the prediction of tertiary academic performance as did intelligence.**"
- Conclusion: "Conscientiousness has the strongest association with academic performance of all the FFM
  dimensions; **its association with academic performance rivaled that of intelligence except in primary
  education**."

**Authors' stated limitations.**
- "There do not appear to be valid methods for estimating this form of range restriction, so no correction was
  made for range restriction with academic performance." Tertiary institutions "restrict access on the basis
  of prior academic performance and thereby create range restriction," but "none of the studies reported the
  selection ratios."
- "Practitioners should, nonetheless, be cautious in applying the results of this research because of the
  finding that **substantial proportions of variations between studies were systematic, rather than random**."
  Heterogeneity I² was 88.3%–97.1% across dimensions.
- "Other methodological limitations that have been mentioned, such as the lack of correction for range, may
  have led to substantial underestimates of the strength of correlations."
- Composition warning on the tertiary estimate: "roughly two fifths of the tertiary level sample was derived
  from studies with psychology students."

**Applicability flag.** No correction for range restriction; no gifted or high-ability subgroup analysis. The
primary-school conscientiousness estimate — the one closest to an elementary-age applicant pool — rests on
**k = 8 samples, N = 3,196**, and the primary-school intelligence estimate on **k = 4, N = 1,791**. These are
the thinnest cells in the whole meta-analysis and they are exactly the cells an early screener would need.

### 2.2 Duckworth & Seligman (2005) — self-discipline

**Source.** Duckworth, A. L., & Seligman, M. E. P. (2005). "Self-Discipline Outdoes IQ in Predicting Academic
Performance of Adolescents." *Psychological Science*, 16(12), 939–944. DOI
[10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01641.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01641.x). Free full text:
[gwern PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/dual-n-back/2005-duckworth.pdf).

**Sample — and this is the most important detail in the section.** Verbatim:

> "The participants were two consecutive cohorts of eighth-grade students recruited from a socioeconomically
> and ethnically diverse **magnet public school** in a city in the Northeast. **Fifth-grade students are
> admitted to this school on the basis of their grades and standardized test scores.** At the end of the eighth
> grade, roughly 70% of these students are selected for a more competitive high school program."

Study 1: N = 140 (71% of the school's 198 eighth graders), mean age 13.4 (SD 0.37); 55% Caucasian, 32.1%
Black, 8.6% Asian, 3.6% Latino, 0.7% American Indian; 56% female. Study 2: N = 164 (83%), mean age 13.8
(SD 0.51); 52% Caucasian, 31.1% Black, 12.2% Asian, 4.3% Latino, 0.6% American Indian; 54% female. IQ measured
with the Otis-Lennon School Ability Test, 7th ed. Self-discipline was a composite of student self-report (EJI
and Brief Self-Control Scale), parent report, teacher report, a hypothetical monetary-choice questionnaire and
(Study 2) a behavioral delay-of-gratification task; composite internal reliability .96 and .93.

**Table 2 — correlations with the composite self-discipline score and with IQ.**

| Outcome | Self-discipline, Study 1 (N = 140) | Self-discipline, Study 2 (N = 164) | IQ, Study 2 | p of difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First-marking-period GPA | .52 | .66 | .34 | < .001 |
| **Final GPA** | **.55** | **.67** | **.32** | **< .001** |
| Spring achievement test | .29 | .43 | .36 | n.s. |
| Selection to high school | .42 | .56 | .26 | < .001 |
| School absences | .17 | .26 | .07 | .06 |
| Homework hours | — | .35 | .09 | < .001 |
| Television hours | — | .33 | .06 | .01 |
| Time of day homework begun | — | .26 | .18 | < .001 |

**Table 3 — the regression, Study 2 (N = 154).** Predicting final GPA simultaneously from IQ,
first-marking-period GPA and self-discipline: IQ β = .01 (not significant); first-marking-period GPA β = .89
(p < .001); self-discipline β = .08 (p < .05). In Study 1 (N = 137) without IQ: first-marking-period GPA
β = .87 (p < .001), self-discipline β = .10 (p < .05).

Note what that regression says. Once prior GPA is in the model, self-discipline's standardized coefficient is
**.08**, and prior GPA's is **.89**.

**Authors' own stated limitations — all three are directly on point for a selective program.**

1. Range restriction on IQ but not on self-discipline: "in the studied population, there was restriction of
   range for IQ, but not for self-discipline. Indeed, whereas the standard deviation for the Otis-Lennon is 16
   for a normative population, in the current study of magnet-school students, **the standard deviation for the
   Otis-Lennon was just under 10**... According to classical test theory (Lord & Novick, 1968), **the
   unattenuated population correlation (r) between IQ and final GPA in the current study is estimated as .49**,
   still smaller than the observed correlation between self-discipline and GPA (r = .67)."
2. Self-discipline is probably range-restricted too: "the fact that students from this school were admitted on
   the basis of their past academic success and the finding that academic success and self-discipline were
   highly correlated suggest that **there was likely some restriction on range for self-discipline, as well as
   for IQ. To address this limitation, we plan to replicate these studies with more heterogeneous populations
   of adolescents.**"
3. The result depends on choosing GPA as the outcome: "the claim that discipline influences achievement more
   than talent does is weakened if one puts more stock in standardized achievement-test scores than in
   report-card grades: **In Study 2, the correlation between self-discipline and achievement-test scores
   (r = .43) was nonsignificantly higher than that between IQ and achievement-test scores (r = .36).**"

**Applicability note.** This is one of the very few studies in this document run *inside* an academically
selected population, which makes it unusually transferable — but note that "self-discipline outdoes IQ" is
partly an artifact of the selection: the school had already truncated the IQ distribution to SD ≈ 10 and left
the self-discipline distribution intact. The authors say this themselves. On the achievement-test outcome, the
gap between self-discipline and IQ was not significant.

## 3. Grit and its critiques

### 3.1 Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017)

**Source.** Credé, M., Tynan, M. C., & Harms, P. D. (2017). "Much ado about grit: A meta-analytic synthesis of
the grit literature." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 113(3), 492–511. DOI
[10.1037/pspp0000102](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000102). Free full text:
[PDF](https://mh19870410.files.wordpress.com/2024/04/2017-crede-much-ado-about-grit-a-meta-analytic-synthesis-of-the-grit-literature.pdf).

**Sample.** "584 effect sizes from 88 independent samples representing 66,807 individuals." Mixed adult,
college and school samples; no gifted or high-ability subgroup analysis reported.

**The correlation you asked for.** Grit with conscientiousness: **ρ = .84 (k = 22, N = 18,826, SD_ρ = .07)**.
Perseverance-of-effort facet with conscientiousness ρ = .83 (k = 8, N = 4,967); consistency-of-interest facet
ρ = .61 (k = 8, N = 4,967). Grit with self-control ρ = .72 (k = 4, N = 2,615). Grit with emotional stability
ρ = .41.

**Grit with cognitive ability: ρ = .05** (k = 21, N = 11,513, SD_ρ = .12). Perseverance ρ = .01; consistency
ρ = .00. Grit is essentially orthogonal to ability.

**Grit with criteria.**

| Criterion | k | N | ρ (overall grit) | ρ (perseverance) | ρ (consistency) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Overall academic performance | 39 | 13,141 | .18 | .26 | .10 |
| GPA (all levels) | 37 | 12,601 | .17 | .26 | .10 |
| Undergraduate GPA | 30 | 10,526 | .17 | .25 | .09 |
| High school GPA | 17 | 6,364 | .16 | — | — |
| Retention (marital-success study excluded) | 10 | 11,163 | .18 | — | — |
| Non-academic performance | 7 | 4,116 | .21 | — | — |

The perseverance-versus-consistency difference was significant at p < .001 in all four academic comparisons.

**Incremental validity over conscientiousness, verbatim from the results:**

> "Results for Model 1 indicate that **overall grit explains no variance in either overall academic performance
> or high school GPA after controlling for conscientiousness, and explains only a very small amount of
> incremental variance in college GPA (i.e., ΔR = .004)**. Importantly, conscientiousness explains incremental
> variance in these outcomes if first controlling for overall grit. Results for Model 2 show that
> **perseverance explained a substantial amount of incremental variance in overall academic performance
> (ΔR = .040), high school GPA (ΔR = .085), and a somewhat lower amount for college GPA (ΔR = .023)**.
> Consistency explained almost no unique variance in the three criteria."

**Flag on the metric.** In the copies I could access, the delta symbol and any superscript are lost in text
extraction, so the increments print as "ΔR" rather than "ΔR²". The Table 6 arithmetic is consistent with these
being increments in multiple *R* (conscientiousness alone R = .220, grit alone R = .180, both together
R = .220, i.e. grit adds .000). **I could not resolve from the accessible text whether the published table
labels these ΔR or ΔR². The authors' verbal statement — "overall grit explains no variance ... after
controlling for conscientiousness" — is unambiguous either way and is what I would cite.**

**Authors' stated conclusions.**
- Abstract: "our results ... indicate that **the higher order structure of grit is not confirmed, that grit is
  only moderately correlated with performance and retention, and that grit is very strongly correlated with
  conscientiousness**. We also find that the perseverance of effort facet has significantly stronger criterion
  validities than the consistency of interest facet... In aggregate our results suggest that interventions
  designed to enhance grit may only have weak effects on performance and success, **that the construct validity
  of grit is in question**, and that the primary utility of the grit construct may lie in the perseverance
  facet."
- Discussion: "the size of the correlation (ρ = .84) with overall conscientiousness is so strong as to not only
  limit the incremental value of grit scores for the prediction of performance over and above conscientiousness
  **but also suggest that grit may be redundant with conscientiousness**."
- The jangle-fallacy argument: "the correlation between overall grit and conscientiousness, and between
  persistence and conscientiousness (ρ = .89) is much stronger than what is typically found between scores on
  two different global measures of conscientiousness (ρ = .63; Pace & Brannick, 2010). This, in turn, suggests
  that **grit research may have fallen victim to the jangle fallacy and that grit as currently measured is
  simply a repackaging of conscientiousness** or one of the facets of conscientiousness."
- The direct comparison to Poropat: grit–GPA "in middle/high school (ρ = .16) and college (ρ = .17) are largely
  identical (if somewhat weaker) to those reported for conscientiousness in the recent meta-analytic review by
  Porapat (2009): ρ = .21 for middle/high school GPA and ρ = .23 for college GPA." *(The misspelling "Porapat"
  is in the original.)*

**On selection specifically**, the authors set up the question this way: "A finding that grit exhibits smaller
differences between groups is likely to make the construct more attractive in settings **where scores are used
for selection purposes or for making other high-stakes decisions** because the likelihood of adverse impact on
legally protected groups is reduced."

### 3.2 Duckworth's own published cautions

**Source A.** Duckworth, A. L., & Yeager, D. S. (2015). "Measurement Matters: Assessing Personal Qualities
Other Than Cognitive Ability for Educational Purposes." *Educational Researcher*, 44(4), 237–251. DOI
[10.3102/0013189X15584327](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X15584327). Full text:
[Sage tokenized link](https://journals.sagepub.com/stoken/rbtfl/hixxiPxVRpaxg/full).

From the abstract, verbatim: "In this article, we identify serious challenges to doing so... we compare
*self-report questionnaires*, *teacher-report questionnaires*, and *performance tasks*, using self-control as
an illustrative case study to make the general point that **each approach is imperfect in its own way**...
For example, **we do not believe any available measure is suitable for between-school accountability
judgments.** In addition to urging caution among policymakers and practitioners, we highlight medium-term
innovations that may make measures of these personal qualities more suitable for educational purposes."

The reference-bias mechanism, verbatim from the body: "Within-country analyses of the PISA show the expected
positive association between self-reported conscientiousness and academic performance, but **between-country
analyses suggest that countries with higher conscientiousness ratings actually perform worse on math and
reading tests**... **Students attending middle schools with higher admissions standards and test scores rate
themselves lower in self-control**... Likewise, KIPP charter school students report spending more time on
homework each night than students at matched control schools, and they earn higher standardized achievement
test scores—but score no higher on self-report questionnaire items."

On faking and on gaming: "Even when faking does not occur, accountability pressures for qualities such as
growth mind-set can lead to **superficial parroting of growth mind-set ideas, so as to increase self-reports
rather than reflect true, deep, mind-set changes in students**."

Closing: "our hope is that the broader educational community proceeds forward with both alacrity and caution,
and with equal parts optimism and humility."

**Source B.** Duckworth, A. L. (2016, March 26). "Don't Grade Schools on Grit." *The New York Times*, Sunday
Review. [Article page](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/03/27/opinion/sunday/dont-grade-schools-on-grit.html).

Quotes, all three of which I found reproduced identically across four independent secondary sources:

> "These days, however, I worry I've contributed, inadvertently, to an idea I vigorously oppose: high-stakes
> character assessment."

> "But we're nowhere near ready — and perhaps never will be — to use feedback on character as a metric for
> judging the effectiveness of teachers and schools. We shouldn't be rewarding or punishing schools for how
> students perform on these measures."

> "Should we turn measures of character intended for research and self-discovery into high-stakes metrics for
> accountability? In my view, no."

**Flag, and it matters.** The *New York Times* is paywalled to me; I did not read the original. The quotes are
corroborated by [Philadelphia Magazine](https://www.phillymag.com/news/2016/03/28/penn-prof-grit-oped/), a
[Dallas Morning News syndication](https://www.dallasnews.com/opinion/commentary/2016/03/29/angela-duckworth-dont-judge-a-teacher-by-the-grit-of-the-student/),
the [Marshall Memo](https://marshallmemo.com/issues/62a55bfdc5aa7dd8763f3576d169042f/MarshMemo631.pdf), and
[UCLA's SMHP](https://smhp.psych.ucla.edu/Hottopic/hottopic(grit).html).

**Second and more important flag.** Every Duckworth caution I could verify is about **using character measures
for school and teacher accountability**, not about using the Grit Scale to **select individual students**.
Those are different uses with different failure modes. **I searched for and did not find a published Duckworth
statement explicitly cautioning against using the Grit Scale for admissions or student selection.** The
closest published statement is the Duckworth & Yeager (2015) sentence that no available measure is suitable
for between-school accountability, plus their general treatment of self-report faking. Anyone citing "Duckworth
says don't use grit for selection" is extrapolating; the honest version is "Duckworth says don't use character
measures for high-stakes accountability, and documents the self-report faking and reference-bias problems that
would also apply to selection."

## 4. Growth mindset — and where the two headline studies disagree

### 4.1 Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara (2018)

**Source.** Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). "To What Extent
and Under Which Circumstances Are Growth Mind-Sets Important to Academic Achievement? Two Meta-Analyses."
*Psychological Science*, 29(4), 549–571. DOI
[10.1177/0956797617739704](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704). Free full text:
[Case Western PDF](https://artscimedia.case.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2018/10/03145228/Sisk-et-al.-2018.pdf).
Data at [osf.io/453ds](https://osf.io/453ds).

**Meta-analysis 1 — correlation between mindset and achievement.** k = 273 effect sizes, N = 365,915. Mean
adjusted N per effect size 1,429. Studies from over 20 countries.

- **r = .10, 95% CI [.08, .13], p < .001.** Assuming reliability .80, r = .12, 95% CI [.09, .14].
- Distribution of effects: "**157 of the 273 effect sizes (58%) are not significantly different from zero.
  Another 16 effect sizes (6%) are significantly different from zero but negative**... The remaining 100 effect
  sizes (37%) are significantly different from zero and positive."
- "Academic risk status and SES did not affect the relationship."

**Meta-analysis 2 — intervention effects.** k = 43 effect sizes, N = 57,155. Mean adjusted N 1,664.

- **d = 0.08, 95% CI [0.02, 0.14], p = .010.** I² = 43.15 (τ² = .010).
- "**37 of the 43 effect sizes (86%) are not significantly different from zero. One effect size is
  significantly different from zero but negative.** The remaining 5 effect sizes (12%) are significantly
  different from zero and positive."

**Moderator results relevant to who benefits.**

| Subgroup | d | 95% CI | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adolescents | 0.08 | [−0.01, 0.17] | .090 |
| Adults | 0.08 | [−0.02, 0.17] | .123 |
| Low academic risk | 0.06 | [−0.01, 0.12] | .109 |
| Moderate academic risk | 0.08 | [−0.04, 0.19] | .177 |
| High academic risk (full samples) | 0.17 | [−0.11, 0.45] | .231 |
| High academic risk (using reported high-risk subsamples) | **0.19** | [0.02, 0.36] | **.031** |
| Middle/upper-class SES | 0.03 | [−0.06, 0.11] | .538 |
| **Low SES** | **0.34** | **[0.07, 0.62]** | **.013** |
| Passive control group | 0.02 | [−0.05, 0.10] | .522 |
| Active (placebo) control group | 0.08 | [0.01, 0.16] | .034 |
| GPA as the outcome | 0.07 | [0.002, 0.14] | .045 |

SES was a significant moderator, Q(1) = 4.76, p = .029. Academic-risk status was not, Q(2) = 0.67, p = .715.

**Authors' stated conclusions.**

> "Part of the reshaping effort has been to make funding mind-set research a 'national education priority'
> (Rattan et al., 2015, p. 723) because mind-sets have 'profound effects' on school achievement (Dweck, 2008,
> para. 2). **Our meta-analyses do not support this claim. Effect sizes were inconsistent across studies, but
> most analyses yielded small (or null) effects.**"

> "However, from a practical perspective, **resources might be better allocated elsewhere than mind-set
> interventions.** Across a range of treatment types, Hattie, Biggs, and Purdie (1996) found that the
> meta-analytic average effect size for a typical educational intervention on academic performance is 0.57.
> **All meta-analytic effects of mind-set interventions on academic performance were < 0.35, and most were
> null.** The evidence suggests that the 'mindset revolution' might not be the best avenue to reshape our
> education system."

A finding relevant to any measurement plan: mindset manipulations often did not move mindset. "If mind-set
manipulations are not demonstrating an influence on students' mind-sets (**as was found in nearly half the
studies including manipulation checks**), then the mechanism affecting any observed change in achievement is
either due to chance or due to mediating variables."

### 4.2 Yeager et al. (2019) — the National Study of Learning Mindsets

**Source.** Yeager, D. S., Hanselman, P., Walton, G. M., Murray, J. S., Crosnoe, R., Muller, C., Tipton, E.,
Schneider, B., Hulleman, C. S., Hinojosa, C. P., Paunesku, D., Romero, C., Flint, K., Roberts, A., Trott, J.,
Iachan, R., Buontempo, J., Yang, S. M., Carvalho, C. M., Hahn, P. R., Gopalan, M., Mhatre, P., Ferguson, R.,
Duckworth, A. L., & Dweck, C. S. (2019). "A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves
achievement." *Nature*, 573(7774), 364–369. DOI
[10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y). Free full text:
[PMC6786290](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6786290/).

**Sample.** "a stratified random sample of 65 regular public schools in the United States that included 12,490
ninth-grade adolescents who were individually randomized to condition." 139 schools invited; 65 agreed,
participated and provided student records. Demographics: "11% self-reported being black/African-American, 4%
Asian-American, 24% Latino/Latina, 43% white and 18% another race or ethnicity; 29% reported that their mother
had a bachelor's degree or higher." Generalizability: "The National Study of Learning Mindsets showed a
**Tipton generalizability index of 0.98**, which is very high" (0.90 corresponds to essentially a random
sample). Intervention: two online sessions, under one hour total, no teacher training.

**Main pre-registered result, lower-achieving students (below the within-school median):**

> "lower-achieving adolescents earned higher GPAs in core classes at the end of the ninth grade when assigned
> to the growth mindset intervention, **B = 0.10 grade points (95% confidence interval = 0.04, 0.16), s.e. =
> 0.03, n = 6,320, k = 65, t = 3.51, P = 0.001, standardized mean difference effect size of 0.11**."

**The result that matters most for a program that admits only high performers:**

> "Confirming the predictions in the pre-analysis plan, **higher-achieving students demonstrated no significant
> treatment effects on core course GPAs, B = 0.01 grade points (95% confidence interval = −0.03–0.06), s.e. =
> 0.02, n = 6,170, k = 65, t = 0.480, P = 0.634, standardized mean difference effect size = 0.01**, resulting in
> a significant intervention × lower-achiever interaction B = 0.09 grade points (95% confidence interval =
> 0.01–0.17), s.e. = 0.04, n = 12,490, k = 65, t = 2.179, P = 0.034)."

Their own explanation: "higher-achieving students **may have already had habits (for example, turning in work
on time) and environments (for example, supportive family, teachers or peer groups) that fostered high GPAs
even in the control condition**."

**Other verified numbers.**
- Belief change among lower achievers: B = −0.38 on fixed-mindset reports (95% CI −0.31, −0.46), s.e. = 0.04,
  n = 5,650, k = 65, t = −10.14, P < 0.001, standardized 0.33. The manipulation worked.
- School-level moderation: "Treatment effects on ninth-grade GPAs among lower-achieving students were **smaller
  in schools with higher achievement levels**, intervention × school achievement level (continuous)
  interaction, unstandardized B = −0.07, s.e. = 0.03, z = −2.76, n = 6,320, k = 65, P = 0.006, standardized
  β = −0.25."
- Advanced math enrolment (full cohort, both achievement groups): "**increased the likelihood of students
  taking advanced mathematics (algebra II or higher) in tenth grade by 3 percentage points** (95% CI = 0.01,
  0.04), s.e. = 0.01, n = 6,690, k = 41, t = 3.18, P = 0.001, from a rate of 33% in the control condition to a
  rate of 36%... corresponding to a 9% relative increase." Here the school-achievement interaction reversed
  sign (B = 0.04, z = 2.26, P = 0.024): "**Within the highest-achieving 25% of schools, the intervention
  increased the rate at which students took advanced mathematics in tenth grade by 4 percentage points**
  (t = 2.37, P = 0.018)."
- The benchmark they use for calibrating 0.11 SD: "0.20 s.d. and 0.23 s.d. were the two largest effects
  observed in a recent cohort analysis of the results of all of the pre-registered, randomized trials that
  evaluated promising interventions for secondary schools funded as part of the US federal government's i3
  initiative (**the median effect for these promising interventions was 0.03 s.d.**)."
- Bayesian causal forest robustness check: "The BCF analysis assigned a near-certain posterior probability that
  the population-average treatment effect (PATE) among lower-achieving students was positive and greater than
  zero, P_PATE > 0 ≥ 0.999."

### 4.3 Where the two disagree, stated precisely

They agree on magnitude and disagree on framing and target.

- **Magnitude is nearly the same.** Sisk's pooled intervention effect is d = 0.08 [0.02, 0.14]. Yeager's
  standardized effect among the targeted lower-achieving subgroup is 0.11. Yeager's effect among
  higher-achieving students is 0.01 and not significant. Sisk's adolescent subgroup is d = 0.08 and not
  significant.
- **They agree that the benefit, if any, is concentrated in disadvantaged or low-performing students.** Sisk's
  only two significant positive subgroups are low-SES (d = 0.34) and academically high-risk (d = 0.19). Yeager
  pre-registered lower achievers and found the effect only there.
- **They disagree on the practical verdict.** Sisk: "resources might be better allocated elsewhere than
  mind-set interventions." Yeager: the same magnitude represents "a substantial proportion of the effects on
  grades of the most effective rigorously evaluated adolescent interventions of any cost or duration," because
  the intervention costs almost nothing and the comparison set has a median effect of 0.03 SD.
- **They disagree on sampling.** Sisk's k = 43 intervention effects come mostly from convenience samples with a
  median published-study sample size of 66; Yeager's single study is a nationally generalizable probability
  sample of 65 schools and 12,490 students. Yeager (2019) postdates Sisk (2018) and is not included in it.

**Applicability note for §4.** Nothing here supports mindset as a *selection* variable. Both papers study
mindset as an *intervention target*, and the intervention evidence is null for exactly the population an
accelerated program admits — Yeager's higher achievers, and Sisk's low-risk and middle/upper-class subgroups.
I found no study estimating the predictive validity of a mindset measure for admission decisions.

## 5. Behavioral measures of challenge seeking, as distinct from self-report

### 5.1 What the instrument is

**Source.** Yeager, D. S., Romero, C., Paunesku, D., Hulleman, C. S., Schneider, B., Hinojosa, C., Lee, H. Y.,
O'Brien, J., Flint, K., Roberts, A., Trott, J., Greene, D., Walton, G. M., & Dweck, C. S. (2016).
"Make-a-Math-Worksheet Task." *PsycTESTS Dataset*. DOI
[10.1037/t49812-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t49812-000).

**What was measured, from the PsycTESTS record.** Students build their own worksheet from Khan Academy
problems. "There were three topic areas (Introduction to Algebra, Advanced Algebra, and Geometry), and within
each topic area there were four 'chapters'... and within each chapter there were six problems, each labeled
'Not very challenging,' 'Somewhat challenging,' or 'Very challenging' (two per type). Each page showed the six
problems for a given chapter and students were instructed to select 'at least 2 and up to 6 problems' on each
page." Total possible 12–36 items. **Development sample: 7,501 ninth-grade students in the United States and
Canada.** The scored variable is the difference between the number of hard and easy problems chosen.

Initial validation, per the same record: "the choice of a greater number of easy problems as compared to hard
problems at Time 2 was **modestly correlated with grit, self-control, prior performance, interest in math, math
anxiety** measured at Time 1, 1 to 4 weeks earlier, and all in the expected direction. Also, the worksheet task
behavior correlated with the single dichotomous hypothetical choice."

**Flag: this is a test-database record, not the underlying journal article.** I read the PsycTESTS description
and verified authorship and year via Crossref. I did not read Yeager et al. (2016) itself.

### 5.2 What it predicts — the validation study

**Source.** Rege, M., Hanselman, P., Solli, I. F., Dweck, C. S., Ludvigsen, S., Bettinger, E., Crosnoe, R.,
Muller, C., Walton, G., Duckworth, A., & Yeager, D. S. (2021). "How can we inspire nations of learners? An
investigation of growth mindset and challenge-seeking in two countries." *American Psychologist*, 76(5),
755–767. DOI [10.1037/amp0000647](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000647).

*(Note: this paper is often cited as "Yeager et al."; the verified Crossref author order puts Mari Rege first
and Yeager last.)*

**Samples.** New analyses of the U.S. National Study of Learning Mindsets, N = 14,472 ninth graders; plus the
U-say experiment, "nearly all schools in 2 counties in Norway," N = 6,541.

**Convergent validity.** "among students creating worksheets with more hard than easy problems, **57% said they
would have chosen the harder extra credit math assignment on the hypothetical scenario, compared to 33% among
those who created worksheets with more easy than hard problems**, a significant difference, χ²(1, N = 11778) =
677.32, p < .001." And: "a fixed mindset measured at Time 2 predicted less challenge-seeking behavior on the
make-a-math-worksheet task, **r(14084) = −.14, p < .001**."

**Predictive validity for a real behavioral outcome — the fact most relevant to selection.** Verbatim:

> "Even among students who were **not** taking advanced math in 9th grade, according to administrative records,
> those who created worksheets with more hard than easy problems had a **15% chance of being in Algebra II or
> above in 10th grade, relative to 9%** among those who created worksheets with more easy than hard problems, a
> significant difference, χ²(1, N = 3381) = 23.96, p < .001. Among students who took advanced math in 9th
> grade, these numbers were **92% and 89%**, respectively, also a significant difference, χ²(1, N = 1554) =
> 4.13, p = .042."

Norway: "The continuous make-a-math worksheet measure significantly predicted a student's likelihood of being
enrolled in advanced math, **b = .016, SE = .002, t = 8.00, p < .001**."

**The finding that distinguishes this measure from the GPA-based mindset results.** Verbatim:

> "The effects emerged on a behavioral marker of challenge-seeking—the make-a-math-worksheet task—**across
> student demographic groups and achievement levels**, and in generalizable samples. **High- and low-achieving
> students alike sought out challenges when treated**, somewhat in contrast to past studies that have used
> grade point averages as the primary outcome and shown benefits among students who did not already have high
> grades."

**Applicability notes.**
1. Read the predictive-validity number honestly. The largest gap the task produces is **15% versus 9%** — six
   percentage points — on tenth-grade advanced-math enrolment among students not already in advanced math.
   Among students already in advanced math (the population closest to a selective program's admits) the gap is
   **92% versus 89%**, three percentage points, at p = .042.
2. This task has never been used or validated as an **admissions** instrument. I found no test–retest
   reliability estimate for it, no evidence about whether it is fakeable under high stakes, and no study of its
   behavior when students know their selection depends on the answer. That last gap is severe: the whole point
   of a behavioral measure is that it is harder to fake than self-report, and nobody has tested that claim
   under the incentive conditions an admissions setting creates.
3. In Yeager et al. (2019) the task was used as a **school-level norm variable**, not as an individual
   predictor: "The school norm was estimated by taking the average number of challenging mathematical problems
   that adolescents in the control condition attending a given school chose to work on." The school-norm
   moderation result was B = 0.11 (95% CI 0.01, 0.21), s.e. = 0.05, z = 2.18, n = 6,320, k = 65, P = 0.029,
   β = 0.23. Notably, the parallel analysis using **self-reported** mindset norms "did not yield significant
   moderation" — behavior moderated, self-report did not.

## 6. Delay of gratification

### 6.1 The original claim

**Source.** Shoda, Y., Mischel, W., & Peake, P. K. (1990). "Predicting adolescent cognitive and self-regulatory
competencies from preschool delay of gratification: Identifying diagnostic conditions." *Developmental
Psychology*, 26(6), 978–986. DOI
[10.1037/0012-1649.26.6.978](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.26.6.978).

**Flag: I did not read Shoda et al. (1990) directly. Every number in this subsection is Watts, Duncan & Quan's
(2018) report of it.** The citation, journal, volume, pages and authors are verified against Crossref.

**Sample, as characterized by Watts et al.** "Mischel and Shoda's reported longitudinal associations were based
on **very small and highly selective samples of children from the Stanford University community (ns = 35–89)**...
Although Mischel's original work included over 600 preschool-age children (Shoda et al., 1990), follow-up
investigations focused on much smaller samples (e.g., for their investigation of SAT and behavioral outcomes,
**Shoda and colleagues were able to contact only 185 of the original 653 children**)."

**The claimed correlations.** Verbatim from Watts et al.:

> "For the 35 to 48 children who were tested in the diagnostic condition, and for whom adolescent follow-up
> data were available, Shoda and colleagues (1990) observed large correlations between delay time and SAT
> scores, **r(35) = .57 for math, r(35) = .42 for verbal**, and between delay time and parent-reported
> behaviors, for example, '[my child] is attentive and able to concentrate,' **r(48) = .39**. **These bivariate
> correlations were not adjusted for potential confounding factors** that could affect both early delay ability
> and later outcomes."

Also note that the original correlations held only in one experimental condition: "They found positive
associations between gratification delay and later outcomes only for c[hildren in the diagnostic condition]" —
the condition in which no coping strategies were supplied and the treat was visible.

### 6.2 Watts, Duncan & Quan (2018) — the replication

**Source.** Watts, T. W., Duncan, G. J., & Quan, H. (2018). "Revisiting the Marshmallow Test: A Conceptual
Replication Investigating Links Between Early Delay of Gratification and Later Outcomes." *Psychological
Science*, 29(7), 1159–1177. DOI
[10.1177/0956797618761661](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618761661). Free full text:
[PMC6050075](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6050075/). Also at
[eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9k92888p).

**Sample.** NICHD Study of Early Child Care and Youth Development. "Participants were recruited at birth from
10 U.S. sites across the country, providing a **geographically diverse, although not nationally
representative**, sample." Delay task at 54 months; outcomes at Grade 1 and age 15. Analytic n = **918**; the
focal subsample is children whose mothers had not completed college, **n = 552** ("a sample that is 10 times
larger than the sample size in the Shoda et al., 1990, study"); children of college-completing mothers
n = 366.

Method differences worth recording: "the 7-min cap was much shorter than Mischel's maximum assessment length;
the children in Mischel's sample were asked to wait between 15 and 20 min." **55% of all children hit the 7-min
ceiling; 68% of the children of degreed mothers vs 45% of non-degreed.** In the non-degreed sample, mean wait
3.99 min (SD 3.08), 45% waited the full 7 min, **23% waited less than 20 s**; in the degreed sample mean wait
5.38 min and only 10% waited less than 20 s. Children received only the diagnostic-condition version of the
task.

**The revised effect sizes, children of non-degreed mothers, standardized.**

| Outcome | Bivariate | + controls measured before 54 months | + concurrent 54-month controls |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grade-1 achievement | **0.28** (SE 0.04, p < .001) | 0.10 (SE 0.03, p = .002) | **0.05 (SE 0.03, p = .114)** |
| Age-15 achievement | **β = 0.24** (SE 0.04, p < .001) | 0.08 (SE 0.03, p = .016) | **0.05 (SE 0.03, p = .140)** |

Compare against Shoda et al.'s .57 and .42. The abstract's own summary: "we found that an additional minute
waited at age 4 predicted a gain of approximately one tenth of a standard deviation in achievement at age 15.
But **this bivariate correlation was only half the size of those reported in the original studies and was
reduced by two thirds in the presence of controls** for family background, early cognitive ability, and the
home environment."

**The threshold finding.** "**Most of the variation in adolescent achievement came from being able to wait at
least 20 s.**" In the fully specified models, "At age 15, the threshold nature of the relationship was most
apparent; the coefficients produced by the three groups that waited longer than 20 s all fell between 0.23 and
0.30, and were not close to being statistically significantly different from one another (p = .752)."

**Behavioral outcomes replicated not at all.** "we **failed to find even bivariate associations** between delay
of gratification at age 54 months and a host of behavioral outcomes at age 15, which was remarkable given the
stability in self-control measures found in other studies."

**Authors' stated conclusions.**

> "It surprised us that for the children of nondegreed mothers, **most of the achievement boost for early delay
> ability was gained by waiting a mere 20 s.** Shoda et al. (1990) argued that the relationship ... might be
> driven by the ability to generate useful metacognitive strategies... **Such strategies are unlikely to have
> played much of a role in a child's ability to wait for only 20 s.**"

> "We observed that delay of gratification was strongly correlated with concurrent measures of cognitive
> ability, and **controlling for a composite measure of self-control explained only about 25% of our reported
> effects on achievement. These results suggest that the marshmallow test may capture something rather distinct
> from self-control.**"

> "models with controls for concurrent measures of cognitive skills and behavior **reduced the association
> between delay of gratification and age-15 achievement to nearly zero. This implies that an intervention that
> altered a child's ability to delay but failed to change more general cognitive and behavioral capacities
> would likely have limited effects on later outcomes.**"

**Authors' stated weaknesses.** The 7-min ceiling; "variation in our delay-of-gratification measure at age 54
months was not exogenous"; and "**data not drawn to be nationally representative provide a shaky foundation for
generalization.**" On the higher-SES group specifically — the group closest to a selective applicant pool —
"the 7-min ceiling prevented a direct replication of Mischel and Shoda's original work... **The lack of
precision in our higher-SES results was unfortunate, though it should be noted that point estimates in fully
controlled models were often very small.** At the very least, these results further suggest that bivariate
associations between delay of gratification and later outcomes probably contain substantial bias, even for
more privileged children."

**Applicability flag.** The replication's focal sample is deliberately *lower*-SES than the original. The
higher-SES arm, which is the closer analogue to a self-selected applicant pool for a private accelerated
program, is the arm the ceiling effect destroyed — 68% of those children maxed out the task. **A delay task
with a 7-minute cap has essentially no discriminating power in an advantaged, high-ability population.**

## 7. Working memory and attention control

### 7.1 What Engle's lab actually established

**Source A.** Draheim, C., Tsukahara, J. S., Martin, J. D., Mashburn, C. A., & Engle, R. W. (2021). "A toolbox
approach to improving the measurement of attention control." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*,
150(2), 242–275. DOI [10.1037/xge0000783](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000783). Free full text:
[Engle Lab PDF](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2021/Draheim%20et%20al.%20(2021)%20toolbox.pdf).

**Sample.** "over 400 participants (final N = 396)" — **students and community members in Atlanta, Georgia.
Adults.** Testing across four 2-hour sessions.

**The mediation result.** In the model using the best-performing attention tasks (antisaccade, sustained
attention-to-cue, adaptive response-deadline flanker): "**The attention factor had a strong relationship to
working memory capacity (.75) and fluid intelligence (.65). Importantly, attention control did fully mediate
the working memory capacity/fluid intelligence relationship in this model (the path from working memory
capacity to fluid intelligence is .21 and nonsignificant).**"

**The authors' own immediate qualification, which is routinely dropped when this result is cited:**

> "However, setting this path to .33 (the value from the first model) and using the model comparisons approach
> **this .21 value was not statistically different from the mediation value in the first model. As such,
> attention control in this model did not statistically significantly mediate the working memory capacity/fluid
> intelligence relationship more than when the antisaccade, RT Stroop, and RT flanker were used.**"

In the first model (traditional tasks) "the attention control factor **did not** fully mediate the relationship
between working memory capacity and fluid intelligence, as the path between the two (.33) was statistically
significant even after accounting for attention control."

**Source B.** Burgoyne, A. P., & Engle, R. W. (2020). "Attention Control: A Cornerstone of Higher-Order
Cognition." *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 29(6), 624–630. DOI
[10.1177/0963721420969371](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721420969371).

> "performance on the antisaccade task and other simple but difficult attention-control tests predicts working
> memory capacity and, furthermore, explains the relationship between working memory capacity and fluid
> intelligence. That is, **after attention control is accounted for, the once-strong relationship between
> working memory capacity and fluid intelligence all but disappears** (Draheim, Tsukahara, Martin, Mashburn, &
> Engle, 2020)."

**Source C.** Draheim, C., Pak, R., Draheim, A. A., & Engle, R. W. (2022). "The role of attention control in
complex real-world tasks." *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 29(4), 1143–1197. DOI
[10.3758/s13423-021-02052-2](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-021-02052-2).

The lab's own statement of the theoretical claim: "**It should therefore be the case that, in most situations,
attention control is a better indicator of one's overall cognitive capability than working memory capacity
and/or fluid intelligence alone.** In other words, knowledge of an individual's ability to control their
attention should explain more variation in higher-order cognitive behaviors and performance than either working
memory capacity or fluid intelligence."

Their own methodological standard, from the Cambridge chapter version: "**What is needed is incremental
validity: Does working memory capacity predict the criterion measure above and beyond attention control and
fluid intelligence?**"

**The honest headline for this section.** Engle's incremental-validity work is about the relations among
**laboratory cognitive constructs — working memory capacity, attention control, fluid intelligence — in adult
samples.** It is not about predicting academic achievement in children. **I found no Engle-lab study reporting
incremental validity of attention control over general ability for school achievement, for admission to an
accelerated program, or in any child sample.** Anyone importing "attention control beats working memory and
fluid intelligence" into an admissions context is transferring a finding across construct, criterion and age
group simultaneously.

### 7.2 Working memory and academic achievement — the meta-analytic base rates

- **Peng, P., Namkung, J., Barnes, M., & Sun, C. (2016).** "A meta-analysis of mathematics and working memory:
  Moderating effects of working memory domain, type of mathematics skill, and sample characteristics."
  *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 108(4), 455–473. DOI
  [10.1037/edu0000079](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000079). "**A meta-analysis of 110 studies with 829 effect
  sizes found a significant medium correlation of mathematics and WM, r = .35, 95% CI [.32, .37].**" By skill:
  "word problem-solving and whole-number calculations had the strongest relations with WM (rs = .37 and .35),
  whereas WM and geometry were significantly more weakly related (r = .23)." Verbal, numerical and
  visuospatial WM showed "comparable association."
- **Peng, P., Barnes, M., Wang, C., Wang, W., Li, S., Swanson, H. L., Dardick, W., & Tao, S. (2018).** "A
  meta-analysis on the relation between reading and working memory." *Psychological Bulletin*, 144(1), 48–76.
  DOI [10.1037/bul0000124](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000124). **WM–reading r = .29. Flag: I read this figure
  in Miller-Cotto & Byrnes (2019), not in Peng et al. (2018) itself.** The same secondary source reports that
  "the linkage between WM and reading drops out when one controls for both decoding and vocabulary" (attributed
  to Quinn et al., 2018), which I also did not verify at source.
- **Alloway, T. P., & Alloway, R. G. (2010).** "Investigating the predictive roles of working memory and IQ in
  academic attainment." *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*, 106(1), 20–29. DOI
  [10.1016/j.jecp.2009.11.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2009.11.003). UK sample. From the abstract:
  "**The findings indicate that children's working memory skills at 5 years of age were the best predictor of
  literacy and numeracy 6 years later. IQ, in contrast, accounted for a smaller portion of unique variance to
  these learning outcomes**... Critically, we find that working memory at the start of formal education is a
  more powerful predictor of subsequent academic success than IQ." **Flag: abstract only. I did not verify N,
  the ability range, or the effect sizes.** This is a single study, and its claim is in direct tension with the
  Fullerton Longitudinal Study result in §8.2 and with the stability evidence immediately below.

### 7.3 The countervailing evidence: working memory is the *least* stable of the abilities

From Breit, Scherrer, Tucker-Drob & Preckel (2024), the meta-analysis detailed in §10:

- Working memory (Gwm) had a **significantly lower** rank-order stability than general intelligence
  (ρ = −.13, p = .002), as did fluid reasoning (Gf, ρ = −.11, p < .001) and visual processing (Gv, ρ = −.05,
  p = .007). Knowledge-based abilities (Gc) did not differ from g.
- Asymptotic stability by age 20 with a 5-year interval: **g = .84, Gc = .85, Gs = .82, Gv = .79, Gf = .78,
  Gwm = .76.**
- The authors' explicit warning: "**scores relating to effortful-processing abilities may sometimes be
  insufficiently stable to support diagnostic decisions with long-term consequences, especially in young
  children, where stability is generally lower. We observed particularly low stabilities in Learning Efficiency
  and Working Memory, the latter even when adjusting for test [reliability].**"

### 7.4 The one study asking whether specific abilities add *more* at high ability

**Source.** Breit, M., & Preckel, F. (2020). "Incremental validity of specific cognitive abilities beyond
general intelligence for the explanation of students' school achievement." *Gifted and Talented International*,
35(2), 73–85. DOI
[10.1080/15332276.2020.1799454](https://doi.org/10.1080/15332276.2020.1799454).

**Sample.** "the standardization sample (N = 1,371, ages 12.5–16.5) of the Berlin structure-of-intelligence
test (BIS-HB)." Germany.

**Abstract, verbatim:** "Results of HMR analyses revealed that **the specific cognitive abilities had little
incremental validity in the low- and mid-IQ group. However, they provided significant benefit for the
explanation of school grades in the high-IQ group.** The results suggest that the interpretation of intelligence
tests may benefit from a differential weighting of different test information, depending on general ability of
the tested individual."

**Flag: abstract only. Taylor & Francis blocked every access route I tried. I have no ΔR² values, no definition
of where the "high-IQ group" was cut, and no subgroup sample sizes.** This is the single most directly relevant
finding in the document for the transfer question, and it is the one I could verify least.

**And the evidence pointing the other way.** Hajovsky, D. B., Niileksela, C. R., Olsen, S. C., & Sekula, M. K.
(2023). "Do Cognitive–Achievement Relations Vary by General Ability Level?" *Journal of Intelligence*, 11(9),
177. DOI [10.3390/jintelligence11090177](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence11090177). Free:
[PMC10532248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10532248/). Samples: the Woodcock–Johnson III normative
sample (8,782 total, 4,470 in K–12) and the WJ IV normative sample (7,416 total, 3,891 in K–12), both
nationally representative U.S. Groups defined as Low (<25th percentile), Average (25th–75th), High (>75th).
Their result: "For the WJ III Elementary sample, the primary difference across groups was **the path from the
GIA to BRS, where the value for the Low group was larger than the Average and High groups**... In addition, the
R² for RC was larger for the Low group compared to the Average and High groups." They also report that
McLarnon et al. (2018), studying MCAT-derived measures, found the opposite of Breit & Preckel for narrow
abilities: "**it was found that the low-g group showed stronger relations between narrow cognitive measures and
GPA.**" Their own framing: "**These mixed findings suggest some evidence of differentiation of
cognitive–achievement relations by g, but this phenomenon has not been examined within U.S. nationally
representative school-age samples utilizing standardized measures.**"

**Note the ceiling on all of this: the "High" group in Hajovsky et al. is above the 75th percentile. Nobody in
this literature has estimated cognitive–achievement relations above the 95th percentile.**

## 8. Prior achievement as the benchmark any new predictor must beat

### 8.1 Lavelle-Hill et al. (2024)

**Source.** Lavelle-Hill, R., Frenzel, A. C., Goetz, T., Lichtenfeld, S., Marsh, H. W., Pekrun, R., Sakaki, M.,
Smith, G., & Murayama, K. (2024). "How the predictors of math achievement change over time: A longitudinal
machine learning approach." *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 116(8), 1383–1403. DOI
[10.1037/edu0000863](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000863).

**Sample.** The PALMA study: **3,425 German secondary school students**, annual assessments Years 5–9. "Samples
were representative of the student population in the state of Bavaria (Germany) and included students from all
three school types within the German public school system: vocational-track schools (Hauptschule),
intermediate-track schools (Realschule), and academic-track schools (Gymnasium)." **105 predictors** covering
cognitive ability, motivation and emotion, cognitive strategies, family context, classroom context, and
demographics, reported by students, parents and teachers. Models: Elastic Net and Random Forest, cross-validated
prediction R².

**Next-year prediction — how much variance prior achievement explains, and how much everything else adds.**

| Interval | Best ML model (all 105 predictors) | Prior math achievement alone | Added by self-reports, grades and cognitive tests over prior achievement + school track |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Year 5 → 6 | .70 | **.58** | **+.07** |
| Year 6 → 7 | .73 | **.65** | **+.06** |
| Year 7 → 8 | .78 | **.65** | **+.08** |
| Year 8 → 9 | (reported in Fig. 3A) | **.75** | **+.02** |

Verbatim: "**by comparing the prior performance and school track baseline model to the best-performing machine
learning model, self-report data, grades, and cognitive tests add an additional prediction R-squared of .07
(Year 5 → Year 6), .06 (Year 6 → Year 7), .08 (Year 7 → Year 8), and .02 (Year 8 → Year 9), respectively.**"

**Varying-lag prediction, all predictors measured in Year 5:** Year 5 → 6 = .70 (MAE 34.99), 5 → 7 = .68
(34.52), 5 → 8 = .67 (39.48), 5 → 9 = .63 (43.16).

**Authors' stated conclusions.** "**prior math achievement appears to be one of the strongest predictors of math
achievement across all intervals**"; "**the influence of prior math achievement indeed becomes stronger over
time, indicating that the interindividual stability of achievement scores increases as students progress through
the school years**"; "while other factors (motivation and emotion, cognitive strategies, and family context)
have **relatively small contributions, the effects are relatively temporally stable**." Aside from prior
achievement and school track, "classroom context rated by teachers is the strongest predictor among the other
predictor variables and takes on a 'U'-shaped trend."

A methodological note the authors flag: "we found that the linear model (the Elastic Net model) was the
better-performing machine learning model. This, alongside analyzing the patterns of SHAP importance... suggests
that the relationships between the predictors in the data set and math achievement are **linear and
independent—the impact of nonlinear or interaction effects appeared limited**."

### 8.2 The Fullerton Longitudinal Study — intelligence stops adding once prior achievement is in the model

**Source.** McCoach, D. B., Yu, H., Gottfried, A. W., & Gottfried, A. E. (2017). "Developing talents: A
longitudinal examination of intellectual ability and academic achievement." *High Ability Studies*, 28(1),
7–28. DOI [10.1080/13598139.2017.1298996](https://doi.org/10.1080/13598139.2017.1298996). Free full text:
[gwern PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/fullerton/2017-mccoach.pdf).

**Sample.** The Fullerton Longitudinal Study, launched 1979: **130 children followed from age 1 to age 17**.
"Infants were selected from notifications of all births from hospitals surrounding the university. Infants free
of neurological and visual problems, of normal birth weight, and whose parents spoke English were eligible."
Retention "with at least 80% of the original sample returning at any assessment." Southern California. **General
population, small N.**

**Findings, verbatim.**

> "Childhood intelligence explains over **56% (r² = .75² = .56) of the variance in reading achievement and
> almost 70% (r² = .83² = .69) of the variance in math achievement at age seven**."

> "**after controlling for prior achievement, childhood and adolescent intelligence do not explain additional
> variance in either reading or math achievement from ages 8- to 17-years. Thus, the effects of intelligence on
> subsequent achievement are completely mediated by prior achievement. Put simply, intelligence predicts
> achievement, prior achievement predicts subsequent achievement, and after accounting for prior achievement,
> intelligence does not aid in the prediction of subsequent achievement.**"

> "**Students who enter school with strong academic skills tend to maintain their academic advantage throughout
> their elementary and secondary education.**"

Model fit: χ²(542) = 756.0; RMSEA = .055; CFI = .96; TLI = .96.

### 8.3 The third corroboration

Duckworth & Seligman's own Study 2 regression (§2.2) is a third instance of the same pattern in a third
population: predicting final GPA from IQ, prior GPA and self-discipline simultaneously gives **prior GPA
β = .89, self-discipline β = .08, IQ β = .01 (n.s.)**.

And Poropat's Table 2 shows the ability side of it: the intelligence–achievement correlation falls from
**ρ = .58 at primary level to .24 at secondary and .23 at tertiary**, while conscientiousness holds roughly flat
(.28 → .21 → .23).

**The consistent finding across all four sources.** Prior achievement, once it exists, dominates. Everything
else — the whole 105-variable battery in Lavelle-Hill, IQ in the Fullerton study, self-discipline in Duckworth &
Seligman — adds single-digit increments on top of it. **The binding constraint for an early screener is that a
five-to-eight-year-old applicant has almost no prior achievement record to use, which is precisely why the
question of what else predicts is being asked, and precisely why the answer is thin.**

### 9.3 Hamilton et al. (2018) — poverty, after controlling for achievement

### 9.4 Excellence gaps — where the term comes from, and what NAEP shows

**The term.** Coined in Plucker, J. A., Burroughs, N., & Song, R. (2010). *Mind the (Other) Gap! The Growing

improvement in academic performance, are achievement gaps also shrinking at the highest levels of student
achievement?**... in our discussions with both policymakers and policy researchers, it is obvious that excellence

**The book.** Plucker, J. A., & Peters, S. J. (2016). *Excellence Gaps in Education: Expanding Opportunities for
Talented Students.* Harvard Education Press. ISBN 9781612509945.
[Publisher page](https://hep.gse.harvard.edu/9781612509945/excellence-gaps-in-education/). **Flag: I verified the
book exists and read the publisher's description. I did not read the book and extract no numbers from it.** The
publisher's own summary of the thesis: "**these significant gaps reflect the existence of a persistent talent

**Current NAEP magnitudes — flagged as a think-tank report, not peer-reviewed.** Coffey, M., & Tyner, A. (2023).
*Excellence Gaps by Race and Socioeconomic Status.* Thomas B. Fordham Institute. Free:
[ERIC ED637715](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED637715.pdf). Data: NAEP Data Explorer, eighth grade,

- "the proportion of Advanced students who are White dropped significantly, from **82 to 61 percent**... Asian
  students (who went from **10 percent to 22 percent**) and, to a lesser degree, Hispanic students (from **3
  percent to 8 percent**). Unfortunately, the proportion of Advanced students who are Black decreased over that
  time, **from a tragically low 3 to 2 percent**."
- "On the 2022 National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP)... **the number of Black and Hispanic eighth

- Race is not reducible to SES: "**3.0 percent of the highest-SES Black students achieve at the Advanced level,

- Their overall finding: "**Controlling for SES with the data available in NDE mitigates—but does not
  eliminate—excellence gaps by student race/ethnicity**... because Advanced achievement rates are generally low
  for lower-SES students, **racial/ethnic excellence gaps are even larger within the highest-SES group than they
  are for students overall.**"

## 10. Age and the developmental instability of early measurement

### 10.1 Breit, Scherrer, Tucker-Drob & Preckel (2024) — the meta-analysis

**Source.** Breit, M., Scherrer, V., Tucker-Drob, E. M., & Preckel, F. (2024). "The stability of cognitive
abilities: A meta-analytic review of longitudinal studies." *Psychological Bulletin*, 150(4), 399–439. DOI
[10.1037/bul0000425](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000425). Free full text:
[PMC11626988](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11626988/).

**Sample.** "**205 longitudinal studies that involved a total of 87,408 participants, resulting in 1,288
test–retest correlation coefficients among manifest variables.**" Mean participants per effect size 440.53
(SD 1,391.10), range n = 9 to 15,496. 650 effect sizes for g across 151 samples; 195 for Gc (76 samples), 172
for Gf (60 samples). Test–retest intervals from **1 day to 79 years** (M = 6.52, SD = 10.81). Baseline ages
**1.00 to 88.50 years** (M = 18.07, SD = 21.37). **Clinical samples excluded.**

**The reference point.** "For an age of 20 years and a test–retest interval of 5 years, we found a mean
rank-order stability of **ρ = .76**." (The summary section reports ρ = .77 observed and **ρ = .86 disattenuated**
at the same age and interval — a small discrepancy between the abstract and the summary that I am reporting
rather than resolving.)

**The answer to what an early screener can promise, verbatim:**

> "Stability is much lower in young children. **Before age 4, stability never exceeds .70, whereas in late
> adolescence, the stability no longer drops below this value for any test-retest interval.**"

**The age × interval interaction — the closest thing to age-5-to-8 coefficients the paper reports:**

> "This interaction effect implies that the impact of test-retest interval is larger in young children than in
> adolescents or adults. **In two-year-olds, an increase of test-retest interval from one to five years results
> in a decrease in stability by .08. In five-year-olds, the same increase in interval results in a decrease in
> stability by .06. From the age of eight onward there is little additional change, with an increase of
> test-retest interval from one to five years resulting in a decrease in stability by .05.**... the interaction
> further implies that **until the age of 8, the interval effect is not asymptotic like in adolescents and
> adults but instead continuously decreasing.**"

**Flag: the paper reports the functional form and these interval-decay increments rather than a table of
stability coefficients at ages 5, 6, 7 and 8. I did not find point estimates for those specific ages in the text
I could access; they would have to be computed from the published model parameters in Table 3 or read off
Figures 5 and 6.**

**The asymptote by ability, age 20, 5-year interval.** Complete dataset .79; **g = .84**; Gc = .85; Gs = .82;
Gv = .79; **Gf = .78**; **Gwm = .76**. Table 4 estimates "ranged from ρ = .65 in Ga to ρ = .80 in g. The mean
effect across all abilities was ρ = .76."

**Low reliability is not the explanation.** "**The low stability in young children does not appear to be
primarily due to lower test reliability in this age group.** This may seem surprising, as one might expect lower
test-reliability in young children, given that cognitive testing is more challenging in this age group, but this
problem is usually compensated for by individual testing with extensive, age-appropriate test batteries."
Disattenuating raises the asymptote from .80 to .90 but leaves the age curve essentially unchanged.

**A moderator result that speaks directly to the high-ability transfer question.** "**The general cognitive
ability level of the sample was not significantly related to stability in any dataset (p > .05).**" There is no
meta-analytic evidence that scores from high-ability samples are more (or less) stable than average.

**Instrument matters.** "CFT (ρ = −.09, p = .019), **Raven's matrices (ρ = −.17, p < .001)**, Woodcock Johnson
(ρ = −.08, p = .004), and mixed instruments (ρ = −.04, p = .044) demonstrated significantly lower stability than
the reference category WISC." Also: "incomplete testing was associated with lower stability than the reference
category complete testing (complete dataset: ρ = −.04, p = .009)."

**The authors' own recommendation, verbatim:**

> "our results and their implications may, in many respects, be restricted to the nonclinical population. **We do
> not, for example, recommend the use of cognitive testing for long-term predictions in young children.**"

And their framing of the whole paper: "These results provide important standards with respect to the
'**shelf-life**' of cognitive test scores across development and time."

**Authors' stated limitations.** "our age moderator analyses were based on the convergence assumption (Bell,
1953), assuming that differences in age between samples are informative about changes within the population. **No
one study investigated the stability of cognitive abilities from early childhood to late adulthood.**... **This
approach potentially confounds age differences, cohort differences, and methodological differences between
studies.**"

### 10.2 Yu, McCoach, Gottfried & Gottfried (2018) — latent stability across four developmental periods

**Source.** Yu, H., McCoach, D. B., Gottfried, A. W., & Gottfried, A. E. (2018). "Stability of intelligence from
infancy through adolescence: An autoregressive latent variable model." *Intelligence*, 69, 8–15. DOI
[10.1016/j.intell.2018.03.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.03.011).

**Sample.** The Fullerton Longitudinal Study again (see §8.2 for full sample details; N = 130). "twelve
assessments of intellectual performance over a sixteen-year interval." Latent intelligence modelled at four
periods, three assessments each: infancy (1, 1.5, 2 years), preschool (2.5, 3, 3.5), **childhood (6, 7, 8)**,
adolescence (12, 15, 17).

**Verbatim from the abstract:**

> "Intelligence exhibited a high degree of stability across the four developmental periods. For instance,
> **infant intelligence revealed a strong cross-time correlation with preschool intelligence (r = 0.91) and
> moderate correlations with childhood and adolescent intelligence (r = 0.69 and 0.57, respectively).**
> Intelligence followed a stage-autoregressive pattern whereby **correlations between IQ scores decreased as the
> timespan between assessment waves increased.** Further, from infancy to adolescence, **the effect of
> intelligence during earlier periods was completely mediated by intelligence during the adjacent developmental
> period.**"

**Two things to hold onto when reading those numbers.** First, these are **latent** correlations — corrected for
measurement error and each pooling three separate assessments — so they are systematically higher than the
manifest test–retest correlations that a single real-world screening administration would produce. Second, the
"childhood" construct here **is** the age-6-to-8 window. The relevant figure for an early screener is that
childhood intelligence correlates 0.69 with the *preceding* period and — per the same model — the effect of
earlier periods is fully mediated by the adjacent one, meaning nothing measured before age 6 adds information
about adolescence once the age-6-to-8 measurement exists.

**Flag: abstract and secondary summary only; ScienceDirect blocked the full text. I have the four latent
cross-time correlations quoted above and no others.**

### 10.3 Two long-interval benchmarks, reported at second hand

Both of these appear inside Breit et al. (2024) and I did not read the primary sources:

- **Scottish Mental Surveys.** "Findings from the Scottish Mental Surveys, in which children's IQ was first
  measured at age 11, showed **test–retest correlations of .67 for a time span of 59 years and .54 for a time
  span of 79 years** (Deary, 2014; Deary et al., 2013)."
- **Tucker-Drob & Briley (2014).** "found an increasing stability (test–retest correlation) over the course of
  childhood in their meta-analysis of twin and adoption studies, **from just above .2 in infancy to above .7 by
  the age of 12 for a test–retest interval of six years.**"
- **Bayley (1949).** "The rapid increase in stability during childhood was already described by Bayley (1949),
  who **found hardly any stability in infancy but stabilities over .90 at age 13.**"

## What I searched for and could NOT verify — explicit list

1. **Nothing on the spatial-ability side is now unverified** — Webb, Lubinski & Benbow (2007) was obtained in
   full from Vanderbilt and read directly. **This produced a correction: the widely repeated "additional 3% of
   variance" figure is Wai et al.'s (2009) rounding. The primary reports 2.4%, with only 4 of 10 tests
   statistically significant and subsample Ns of 159–211.** See §1.2. I am keeping this item on the list because
   anyone working from the secondary citation will have the wrong number.
2. **The exact metric in Credé et al.'s Table 6.** The extracted text renders the increments as "ΔR" with the
   delta and any superscript lost. The table arithmetic is consistent with increments in multiple *R*, not *R*².
   I could not resolve this from the copies I could access. The authors' verbal statement is unambiguous
   regardless.
3. **A published Duckworth statement cautioning against using the Grit Scale for student selection or
   admissions.** **Not found.** Everything I could verify concerns high-stakes *accountability* for schools and
   teachers, not selection of individual students. §3.2.
4. **The New York Times op-ed itself.** Paywalled. The three quotes in §3.2 are corroborated verbatim across
   four independent secondary sources, but I did not read the original.
5. **Breit & Preckel (2020) full text.** Taylor & Francis blocked every route. **This is the most directly
   relevant study in the document for whether predictors transfer above the 95th percentile, and I have only its
   abstract.** No ΔR² values, no high-IQ-group cut definition, no subgroup Ns. §7.4.
6. **Peters, Gentry, Whiting & McBee (2019) — the typeset published version.** Sage is paywalled. However, the
   authors' **final draft** is public on OSF and I read all 45 pages of it; every RI in §9.1 comes from that
   draft, and the national figures match what two other peer-reviewed papers report the published version says.
   The residual risk is only that the published article revised a number between draft and print.
7. **Age-specific stability coefficients at ages 5, 6, 7 and 8.** Breit et al. (2024) report the functional form,
   the pre-age-4 ceiling (never above .70), and the interval-decay increments at ages 2, 5 and 8. They do not
   report — or I could not locate — a table of point stability estimates at each of those ages. Those would need
   to be computed from the published Table 3 model parameters. §10.1.
8. **Yu et al. (2018) full text.** ScienceDirect blocked. Abstract only; I have the four latent cross-time
   correlations and nothing else.
9. **Shoda, Mischel & Peake (1990) full text.** Not read. The r = .57 / .42 / .39 figures and the sample
   description in §6.1 are Watts et al.'s (2018) report of them.
10. **Peng et al. (2018) full text.** The WM–reading r = .29 figure comes from Miller-Cotto & Byrnes (2019),
    which cites it. Peng et al.'s DOI, journal, volume and pages are verified.
11. **Alloway & Alloway (2010) full text.** Abstract only. N, ability range and effect sizes not verified. Its
    headline claim — working memory at age 5 beats IQ for predicting attainment 6 years later — is in tension
    with the Fullerton result and with the low measured stability of working-memory scores, and I would not cite
    it without reading it.
12. **Yeager et al. (2016), the article underlying the Make-a-Math-Worksheet Task.** I read the PsycTESTS record
    (DOI 10.1037/t49812-000), not the journal article. The task's construction, the 7,501-student development
    sample and the initial validation correlations all come from that record.
13. **Test–retest reliability of the make-a-math-worksheet task, and its fakeability under high stakes.** No
    study found. Both are prerequisites for using it in selection. §5.
14. **Any study estimating the predictive validity of a mindset, grit, self-discipline, delay-of-gratification,
    or challenge-seeking measure for admission to, or performance in, an accelerated academic program serving
    students above the 95th percentile.** **Not found for any of the five.** Every predictor in this document was
    validated on average-ability or moderately selected populations. The closest approaches are Duckworth &
    Seligman (a selective magnet school, and the authors say the range restriction distorts the comparison) and
    the Wai/Lubinski spatial-ability line (top 1–3% talent-search samples, but with STEM career outcomes rather
    than program performance).
15. **Cognitive–achievement relations above the 95th percentile.** The highest-ability group studied in this
    literature is Hajovsky et al.'s ">75th percentile." Breit & Preckel's "high-IQ group" cut is unknown to me.
    Nobody has published on the 95th-percentile-and-above band. §7.4.
16. **Plucker & Peters (2016), *Excellence Gaps in Education*.** Book verified to exist; not read; no numbers
    extracted from it.
17. **Coffey & Tyner (2023) peer-review status.** It is a Thomas B. Fordham Institute report, not peer reviewed.
    The NAEP figures it reports are drawn from the public NAEP Data Explorer, which I did not query directly.
