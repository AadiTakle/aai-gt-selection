# DOK 1 — Gifted Identification Techniques: What Works, Measured by Who Is Found and Who Is Missed

**Research question:** Which gifted-identification techniques actually work, measured by how many qualifying students they find and miss?

**Document type:** DOK 1 fact extraction. Verifiable facts, exact numbers, exact quotes, real sources. No interpretation, no editorializing.

**Verification protocol used:** Every DOI in this document was checked against the Crossref REST API (`https://api.crossref.org/works/{DOI}`) and the returned title, container-title (journal) and issued year were compared to the citation as written. Verification status is recorded inline. Where a fact could not be verified from primary text, it is labeled `[UNVERIFIED]` and listed in the final section.

**Status:** Complete for the six scoped questions. Sections 1 and 2 are extracted from open full text and are the most thorough. Sections 3, 4 and parts of 5 were limited by paywalls, and the specific gaps are itemized at the end.

**Headline numbers, if you read nothing else:**
- Teacher judgment of **achievement** correlates with test scores at a median **r = .66** (range .28–.92) — but teacher **nomination for giftedness** correlates with identification measures at only **r = .30** (meta-analytic). These are different tasks with very different accuracy.
- Adding a nomination screen before testing drops system sensitivity from a ceiling of **84.3%** to as low as **28%** at commonly used cutoffs (modeled).
- Nonverbal substitution **halves** the ELL/non-ELL gap (8.3 vs 16.6 points) but does **not** close it (**.46–.67 SD** remains), and costs predictive validity (r = .35 vs .54 for reading, ELL).
- Nonperformance methods find **59%** of what performance methods find; only **39%** of what they flag qualifies.
- The **61%** relative-age claim **could not be verified against its primary source**, and the best modern study finds the effect disappears after controlling for achievement.

### 1.1 Hoge & Coladarci (1989) — the correlation range

**Citation.** Hoge, R. D., & Coladarci, T. (1989). Teacher-Based Judgments of Academic Achievement: A Review of Literature. *Review of Educational Research*, 59(3), 297–313.

**DOI (verified).** `10.3102/00346543059003297` — Crossref returns title "Teacher-Based Judgments of Academic Achievement: A Review of Literature", container-title *Review of Educational Research*, issued 1989-09, vol 59, issue 3, pp. 297–313, authors Robert D. Hoge and Theodore Coladarci. **Matches the citation as written.**
A second registered DOI exists for the same article via JSTOR: `10.2307/1170184` — Crossref returns the same title, journal and year (1989), vol 59, issue 3, page 297. Both resolve.

**Open full text used for extraction:** https://umaine.edu/edhd/wp-content/uploads/sites/54/2010/03/Hoge-Coladarci-1989.pdf (University of Maine, Coladarci's institution). All quotes below are from this full text.

**Sample / scope of the review.**
- **Number of studies:** The paper states "The 16 studies included in the review have a common focus" (p. 299) and later "The 16 studies reviewed in the previous section..." (p. 308). Note an internal inconsistency in the paper itself: one passage reads "these 17 studies vary methodologically in several basic..." A secondary source (NCES working paper ED418154) describes it as "findings from 17 studies." **The paper's own dominant count is 16.**
- **Population:** "With three exceptions, the studies focused on students within regular classrooms." The three exceptions are Gresham, Reschly & Carey (1987) (learning-disabled and non-LD students), Leinhardt (1983) (LD students only), and Silverstein, Brownlee, Legutki & MacMillan (1983) (educable mentally retarded students only).
- **Inclusion constraints (exact):** "only studies employing naturalistic data were included; thus, analogue and simulation studies are not represented"; "the review focuses on cases where judgmental and test data were collected concurrently; thus, expectancy-type studies where teachers were asked to make a prediction of future performance are not included"; "the review includes only published studies."
- **Criterion measure:** in every study, "student performance on a standardized achievement test."
- **Grades / N / country:** the review does not report a single pooled N. Individual study grades appear in the paper's Table 1 (e.g. one study reports reading correlations by grade: Grade 1 r = .79, Grade 2 r = .74, Grade 3 r = .86, Grade 4 r = .86, Grade 5 r = .85). Studies are predominantly US, with at least one Irish (Airasian, Kellaghan, Madaus & Pedulla, 1977) and one German (Helmke & Schrader, 1987) study included. **A precise total N and full country breakdown could not be extracted from the available text — see "Could not verify."**

**Exact quote, the headline result (p. 308):** "The range of correlations for the indirect comparisons was 0.28 to 0.86, with a median correlation of 0.62, whereas the direct tests yielded a range of correlations from 0.48 to 0.92, with a median of 0.69."

**Exact quote, authors' conclusion (p. 308):** "In our view, these data support the validity of the teacher judgments of academic achievement. The correlations certainly exceed the convergent and concurrent validity coefficients normally reported for psychological tests, and it is encouraging that the correlations remain strong irrespective of methodological distinctions among the studies."

**Exact quote, authors' stated limitation (p. 308):** "This overall positive conclusion regarding the accuracy of these judgments must, as we have shown, be interpreted in light of some methodological considerations and the operation of moderator variables."

**Exact quote, authors' bounded claim (p. 308):** "Although the studies in our review by no means provide a final evaluation of the accuracy of achievement judgments or any evidence that the judgments are without error, this literature does not support the total rejection of teacher judgments that one sometimes encounters."

**Exact quote on why ratings score lower (p. 305):** "The lower correlations associated with ratings probably reflect teachers' disinclination to use the full range of rating categories, which reduces the variability among teacher judgments and, consequently, the judgment/criterion correlations (Hopkins et al., 1985)."

**CRITICAL: teacher-to-teacher variability (this is a range across teachers, not across studies).**
- Hopkins et al. (1985): within-class correlations ranged **0.44 to 0.88** across **K = 42 teachers**.
- Helmke & Schrader (1987): within-class correlations ranged **.03 to .90**, **K = 31 teachers**. Hoge & Coladarci describe this as "Even greater variability among teachers."
- Hoge & Butcher (1984): standardized partial regression coefficients for achievement test ranged **0.40 to 0.87**, **K = 12 teachers**.
- Exact quote (p. 306): "these data suggest that teachers do, in fact, differ in how accurately they judge their students' achievement."

**CRITICAL: accuracy varies by student ability level.**
- Coladarci (1986): correlations between his percentage-agreement index and student ability ranged **0.78 to 0.89** depending on subject matter. "Across all items on the four subtests, the mean percentage agreement was roughly **60% for students in the lowest quartile and 88% for students in the highest quartile**." Exact quote: "Clearly, these eight teachers were less able to judge the performance of their lower-achieving students." (**N = 8 teachers.**)
- Hoge & Butcher (1984), K = 12 teachers: IQ made a significant independent contribution to predicting teacher achievement judgments (β = 0.18) beyond achievement test scores (β = 0.71). Exact quote: "4 of the 12 teachers displayed a tendency to **overestimate the performance of high-IQ students**."
- Authors' summary quote (p. 307): "There is, then, a relatively strong suggestion from these four studies that students' academic ability may influence the accuracy with which teachers judge student achievement."

**FLAG — transfer to a >95th-percentile selection program.** Hoge & Coladarci is a review of teacher judgment of **achievement across the full ability range in regular classrooms**, not of teacher identification of giftedness. The criterion is a standardized achievement test score, not a gifted-eligibility decision. Three of the sixteen studies are on LD or EMR populations (i.e. below-average ability). No study in this review reports sensitivity, false negative rate, or any classification statistic at a high cut score. The .66 median correlation is a **full-range linear association** and does not translate directly into a hit rate at the top 5%; range restriction at the tail will attenuate it. This review contains **no** figure for the share of gifted students teachers correctly nominate.

### 1.3 Teacher nomination SENSITIVITY — the share of truly gifted students teachers nominate

This is the number that Hoge & Coladarci does **not** provide. The strongest source located is a psychometric modeling study.

**Citation.** McBee, M. T., Peters, S. J., & Miller, E. M. (2016). The Impact of the Nomination Stage on Gifted Program Identification: A Comprehensive Psychometric Analysis. *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 60(4), 258–278.

**DOI (verified).** `10.1177/0016986216656256` — Crossref returns title "The Impact of the Nomination Stage on Gifted Program Identification", container-title *Gifted Child Quarterly*, issued 2016-07-27, vol 60, issue 4, pp. 258–278, authors Matthew T. McBee, Scott J. Peters, Erin M. Miller. **Matches the citation as written.** (Note: the Crossref title omits the subtitle "A Comprehensive Psychometric Analysis" that appears on the published article and on the SAGE landing page.)

**Open full text used for extraction:** https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2016/07/Gifted-Child-Quarterly-2016-McBee-0016986216656256.pdf

**METHOD FLAG — THIS IS A SIMULATION, NOT AN EMPIRICAL SENSITIVITY STUDY.** There is no student sample. The paper computes conditional probabilities by numerically integrating a multivariate normal density (the authors used the R function `sadmvn` from the `mnormt` library) under assumed values for nomination reliability, nomination validity, and test reliability. The **inputs** are empirical: published validity coefficients from the Scales for Identifying Gifted Students (SIGS; Ryser & McConnell, 2004) technical manual. The **outputs** are model-derived sensitivities, not observed hit rates.

**Definitions as stated by the authors (exact):** "First is sensitivity, the proportion of truly gifted students who are admitted to the program." … "the false negative rate is the proportion of truly gifted students who are not identified and is the complement of sensitivity."

**Abstract, exact quote:** "Results showed that in nearly all conditions, identification systems that require a nomination before testing result in a large proportion of gifted students being missed. Under commonly implemented conditions, the nomination stage can cause the false negative rate to easily exceed 60%."

**Baseline with NO nomination screener.** With test reliability = .95 and test cutoff at the 90th percentile, system sensitivity = **.843** (84.3%). The authors describe .95 reliability as "ambitious but also achievable" and note "most identification instruments do not achieve reliabilities that high." This 84.3% is the ceiling: **even a single excellent test given to every student misses about 16% of qualifying students at a 90th-percentile cut, purely from measurement error.**

**Table 3 — Performance of systems using the SIGS General Intellectual Ability subscale as the nomination instrument.** (Assumed reliability .95 and confirmation-test cutoff at 90th percentile throughout. Validity coefficients are the published SIGS-GIA correlations from Ryser & McConnell, 2004.)

**Underlying validity coefficients (Table 2, adapted from Ryser & McConnell, 2004).** SIGS General Intellectual Ability subscale correlates: WISC-III **.67**, TCS-2 **.73**, CogAT **.48**. Other SIGS subscales with WISC-III: Language Arts .62, Mathematics .47, Science .56, Social Studies .53, Creativity .38, Leadership .53.

**Authors' exact conclusion on these numbers:** "The performance analysis indicates that when nomination cutoffs are high, the overall system performance can only be described as unacceptable, particularly when the WISC-III or CogAT are the confirmation stage instruments, as around 60% to 70% of the qualified students are missed when high cutoffs are used with those instruments."

**Authors' exact worked example:** "if the confirmatory assessment was the CogAT, and the nomination cutoff on the SIGS general intellectual ability subscale was set to the 90th percentile, the integrated system sensitivity would be .28. Only 28% of the 'truly' gifted students would be identified. This sensitivity should be contrasted against the 84% sensitivity that would have been achieved if the screener had not been used and instead the CogAT had been given to all students; the relative integrated system sensitivity that would result would be only 33% of its optimal value."

**Authors' exact verdict:** "In either case, the addition of a screening phase has devastated the quality of the resulting gifted identification decisions. In both cases the identification system failed to find the majority of students it set out to find."

**Structural finding — why a screener can only hurt (exact quote):** "the confirmatory test provides strong protection against incorrect identifications because false positives with respect to the nomination are unlikely to successfully qualify on the test. In contrast, the confirmatory assessment can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives because those students that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory test."

**Authors' upper bound on real-world teacher nomination validity (exact quote):** "We chose a validity coefficient of .7 because, on consideration of the literature regarding teachers' beliefs about giftedness (i.e., Berman, Schultz, & Weber, 2012; Miller, 2009; Siegle et al., 2010), this is about as high of a validity coefficient as one could expect to encounter."

**Authors' statement that informal nomination is worse than the modeled case (exact quote):** "Here we consider such a question using evidence of reliability and validity from published teacher rating scales as they might be used in a nomination phase. **Informal teacher nominations are probably much worse than this but there is no way to know for sure.**"

**Actionable cutoff guidance from the paper (nomination validity = .7, nomination reliability = .90, test reliability = .95, test cutoff 90th %ile):**
- Nomination cutoff at **55th percentile** → relative integrated system sensitivity = **95%** of optimal.
- Nomination cutoff at **65th percentile** → relative sensitivity = **90%** of optimal.
- With nomination validity **.60**, to hold the sensitivity loss to no more than 2% (absolute sensitivity 82.6%), "the highest tolerable nomination cutoff is about 30th percentile. This implies that 70% of the students can be expected to pass the nomination stage and should receive the Stage-II confirmatory assessment."
- Accepting a 20% relative sensitivity loss allows a **67th percentile** cutoff, testing 33% of students: "The 20% loss of relative sensitivity implies an absolute sensitivity of 67.4%, so roughly a third of the school's gifted students would not be identified even though they should have been."
- The ROC analysis conclusion (exact): "The optimal nomination cutoff … is therefore found at the point of maximum sensitivity, and this value will be found at the lowest possible cutoff on the nomination instrument. A ROC curve analysis for the two-stage identification system would therefore suggest that the screening process be removed."

**Single-assessment baseline table (Table 1, from McBee, Peters & Waterman, 2014) — sensitivity by cutoff at reliability .70:** .604, .541, and .428 at three successively higher cutoffs, with matching false negative rates of .396, .459, and .572. **[PARTIAL]** The cutoff labels for these three rows were not recoverable from the extracted text layer; the paper elsewhere states cutoffs are "often at the 90th, 95th, or 99th percentile." **Do not cite the row labels without checking the original Table 1.**

**FLAG — transfer to a >95th-percentile program.** McBee et al. fix the confirmation-test cutoff at the **90th percentile** for every computation in Table 3. Their own Table 1 shows sensitivity falls as the cutoff rises. A program selecting above the **95th** percentile should expect **lower** sensitivity than every number in the table above, holding reliability constant. The paper does not report a 95th-percentile version of Table 3.

**FLAG — the 84.3% ceiling is the single most transferable number here.** It is derived from reliability and cutoff alone, independent of any nomination stage, and it says that at a 90th-percentile cut with a .95-reliability test administered universally, ~16% of qualifying students are still missed.

### 1.4 Empirical teacher nomination accuracy — two recent studies

**Study A: Spain / teacher sensitivity of 63%.**

**Citation.** Hernández-Torrano, D., et al. (2024). The influence of teacher sociodemographic factors and school characteristics on the identification of gifted students. *High Ability Studies*.

**DOI (verified).** `10.1080/13598139.2024.2411972` — resolves; Crossref returns title "The influence of teacher sociodemographic factors and school characteristics on the identification of gifted students", journal *High Ability Studies*, 2024. **[PARTIAL — author list not fully confirmed; see "Could not verify."]**

**Sample (from abstract, exact):** "Teachers initially nominated a sample of 505 students aged 7–17 years (M = 11.51, SD = 2.07), but only 359 completed a general intelligence test and 225 reached the minimum percentile to be considered potentially gifted. Only 20 of these had a previous diagnosis of giftedness."

**Result (exact quote):** "Results show that the teachers' identification had a sensitivity of 63% and that the teacher's sex and the type of school significantly influenced the identification of giftedness. Female teachers and teachers in private schools made more accurate identifications. There was no evidence for the influence of school setting or the educational stage."

**FLAG — this "sensitivity" is computed within an already-nominated pool.** The denominator is students teachers had already put forward, of whom 359 were tested. It is **not** the share of all truly gifted students in the population who were nominated; that would require testing the un-nominated students too. The figure is therefore **not comparable** to McBee et al.'s definition of sensitivity.

**Study B: Saudi Arabia / nomination accuracy 31.9% (teachers) vs 39.6% (parents).**

**Citation.** The accuracy of parent and teacher nominations in gifted identification in Saudi Arabia. *Cogent Education* (2026).

**DOI (verified).** `10.1080/2331186x.2026.2654922` — resolves.

**Sample (from abstract, exact):** "a large archival dataset of 541,358 nominations collected from 2015 to 2025", grades 3–10, Saudi Arabia.

**Result (exact quote):** "The results demonstrated that parent nominations yielded a significantly higher accuracy rate (39.6%) than teacher nominations (31.9%), with a statistically significant mean difference of 7.7 percentage points." Also: "a strong grade-level effect was observed, with nomination accuracy for both sources more than doubling between the elementary (grades 3–5) and secondary (grades 6–10) levels."

**FLAG — this is PRECISION, not sensitivity.** "Accuracy rate" here is the share of nominated students who went on to qualify — i.e. positive predictive value. It says nothing about how many qualifying students were never nominated. It is the complement of the false positive rate, not of the false negative rate. **These two studies measure different things and neither reports population sensitivity in McBee's sense.**

## Section 2 — Nonverbal / matrix-only screening for equity

**Bottom line from the two requested sources, in the authors' own words: the substitution does not work as advertised.** Both papers explicitly test the "nonverbal tests level the playing field" claim and both reject it.

### 2.1 Lohman, Korb & Lakin (2008)

**Citation.** Lohman, D. F., Korb, K. A., & Lakin, J. M. (2008). Identifying Academically Gifted English-Language Learners Using Nonverbal Tests: A Comparison of the Raven, NNAT, and CogAT. *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 52(4), 275–296.

**DOI (verified).** `10.1177/0016986208321808` — Crossref returns title "Identifying Academically Gifted English-Language Learners Using Nonverbal Tests", container-title *Gifted Child Quarterly*, issued 2008-10, vol 52, issue 4, pp. 275–296, authors David F. Lohman, Katrina A. Korb, Joni M. Lakin. **Matches the citation as written.** (Crossref's title field omits the subtitle "A Comparison of the Raven, NNAT, and CogAT"; ERIC record EJ809671 carries the full title, v52 n4 p275–296 Fall 2008.)

**Open full text used for extraction:** http://www.korbedpsych.com/LinkedFiles/Identifying_Academically_Gifted_ELL.pdf (author's own site). ERIC record: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ809671

**SAMPLE.**
- **N = 1,198** elementary children, **approximately 40% ELL** (English-language learners).
- **Grades:** K through 6. Ability tests administered to all; achievement tests (TerraNova) only at Grades 3–6.
- **Country:** United States (Arizona; "Project Bright Horizon"; two schools).
- **Ability range:** **BELOW average, not a gifted sample.** Exact quote: "Non-ELL children performed at or somewhat below the national average and ELL students considerably below the national average. For example, the median PRs on the Mathematics Battery ranged from **39 to 49 for non-ELL and from 11 to 20 for ELL children**." Also: "**95% of the Hispanic children were eligible for free or reduced lunch.**"
- **Design:** all three tests (Raven Standard Progressive Matrices, NNAT, CogAT Form 6) administered to the same children, in counterbalanced order, late April/early May 2006, by trained examiners, with directions given in Spanish or English as appropriate. **This is a within-subjects head-to-head design** (relevant to Section 5).

**MEASURED GROUP DIFFERENCES — nonverbal tests.**
- Abstract figure: "ELL children scored **.5 to .67 standard deviations lower** than non-ELL children on the three nonverbal tests."
- Implications section figure: "The ELL children in this study scored from **.5 to .6 SD lower** than non-ELL children on all three nonverbal tests." (Note: the paper states .5–.67 SD in the abstract and .5–.6 SD in the Implications; both appear in the published text.)
- Raw point differences: "ELL children in this study scored **8 to 10 points lower** than non-ELL children on the three nonverbal tests."
- **Controlling for ethnicity did not remove the gap.** Comparing Hispanic ELL vs Hispanic non-ELL children only, "the average differences were **7.5, 6.3, and 9.5 points on the Raven, CogAT, and NNAT** or effect sizes of **.47, .46, and .63**, respectively." The paper contrasts this with prior work: "This is a much larger disparity between ELL and non-ELL Hispanic students than the **1-point difference reported by Naglieri et al. (2004)**."
- Exact quote on why the gap persists: "the lower performance of ELL children could not be attributed to economic factors, to the student's age or grade, or to other demographic factors. Nor could it be attributed to an inability to understand the test directions, because directions were given in Spanish whenever necessary."

**MEASURED GROUP DIFFERENCES — nonverbal vs verbal/quantitative (the direct substitution comparison).**
- Exact quote: "average difference between ELL and non-ELL students was **16.6 SAS points on the CogAT Verbal Battery and 13.2 points on the Quantitative Battery**. Thus, the differences between ELL and non-ELL students were **twice as large on the CogAT Verbal Battery (16.6 points) as on the CogAT Nonverbal Battery (8.3 points)**. But does this mean that the nonverbal test is a better measure of academic talent?"
- **This is the crux.** The nonverbal substitution *does* halve the observed group gap (8.3 vs 16.6 points). The authors then show this comes at a cost in predictive validity.

**PREDICTIVE VALIDITY — the cost of substituting.**
- Exact quote: "none of the nonverbal tests predicted reading achievement very well. Except for the Grade 5-6 ELL sample, the CogAT Verbal Battery was a much better predictor of reading achievement for both ELL and non-ELL children. ### 2.3 Direct answer to "does the substitution reduce group differences?"

Based only on what these two papers measured:

1. **It reduces the observed gap but does not eliminate it.** Lohman et al.: the ELL/non-ELL gap is 8.3 SAS points on CogAT Nonverbal vs 16.6 on CogAT Verbal — halved, not removed. Effect sizes on nonverbal tests remain **.46 to .67 SD**.
2. **It does not equalize identification rates.** Carman & Taylor: low-SES children were still **half as likely** to be identified on the NNAT after adjusting for ethnicity. Lohman et al.: "no support for the claim that the NNAT identifies equal proportions of high-scoring students from different ethnic or language groups."
3. **It costs predictive validity.** Lohman et al.: nonverbal median r with reading = .35 (ELL) / .49 (non-ELL), versus CogAT Verbal r = .54 (ELL) / .76 (non-ELL).
4. **Two of the three tests have norms that break at high cut scores.** The NNAT over-identifies by 3.4× at NAI ≥ 130 and 11.9× at NAI ≥ 145 on Level A; the Raven runs 10 points hot on the CogAT scale.
5. **Both papers converge on the same prescription: use the nonverbal test as one component of a multi-measure system, and compare children to peers with similar opportunity (local/subgroup norms) rather than to distant national norms.** Neither author group recommends nonverbal substitution as a standalone equity fix.

## Section 5 — Head-to-head comparisons of identification methods

**This is the highest-value section. The single best source located is a meta-analysis that computed diagnostic quadrants — i.e. it directly answers "how many students does each method find that the other misses."**

### 5.1 Acar, Sen & Cayirdag (2016) — THE KEY SOURCE

**Citation.** Acar, S., Sen, S., & Cayirdag, N. (2016). Consistency of the Performance and Nonperformance Methods in Gifted Identification: A Multilevel Meta-Analytic Review. *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 60(2), 81–101.

**DOI (verified).** `10.1177/0016986216634438` — Crossref returns title "Consistency of the Performance and Nonperformance Methods in Gifted Identification", container-title *Gifted Child Quarterly*, issued 2016-02-26, vol 60, issue 2, pp. 81–101, authors Selcuk Acar, Sedat Sen, Nur Cayirdag. **Matches the citation as written.**

**WHAT IT COMPARES.** "Performance" methods (tests: ability, achievement, creativity tests — the student performs) versus "nonperformance" methods (nominations and rating scales: teacher, parent, peer, self — someone judges the student). Two separate analyses.

**STUDY 1 — correlational consistency.** Three-level multilevel design, Pearson *r* as the effect size index.
- **Overall correlation between performance and nonperformance methods: r = .30** ("medium").
- **Teacher rating scales were the best of the nonperformance methods:** "Teacher ratings yielded **significantly higher consistency** with performance measures than teacher or parent nomination and self-ratings." A secondary source (Marsili & Pellegrini, 2022, §5.2 below) reports this specific value as **r = .35** for performance measures with teacher rating scales. **[SECONDARY — the r = .35 figure is quoted from Marsili & Pellegrini's description of Acar et al., not read directly from Acar et al.]**

**STUDY 2 — diagnostic proportions. THESE ARE THE FIND/MISS NUMBERS.** Three metrics computed for nonperformance methods against performance methods as criterion:

| Metric | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Specificity** | **70%** | share of non-gifted students correctly not identified |
| **Effectiveness / sensitivity** | **59%** | share of gifted students the nonperformance method finds |
| **Efficiency** | **39%** | share of those flagged by the nonperformance method who actually qualify |

Exact quote: "Study 2 showed that nonperformance methods are relatively strong in terms of **specificity (70%) and effectiveness/sensitivity (59%) but not very efficient (39%)**."

**Reading these numbers directly against the research question:** a nomination/rating-based method **misses about 41% of the students a performance test would identify**, and **about 61% of the students it flags would not qualify on a performance test**.

**THE AUTHORS' HEADLINE CONCLUSION ON OVERLAP (exact quote).** "Analyses of four diagnostic quadrants indicated that performance and nonperformance gifted identification methods, when used alone, tend to **identify different students who would not be identified otherwise despite some amount of convergence between the two**. Our findings indicated that **nonperformance and performance gifted identification methods cannot replace each other. They should be used concurrently rather than be used alone or consecutively.**"

**Note the phrase "rather than be used alone or consecutively."** "Consecutively" is the two-stage nomination-then-test design that McBee et al. (2016, §1.3) showed destroys sensitivity. Acar et al. reach a compatible conclusion from meta-analytic data rather than from simulation: **an "and"-rule sequence is the worst configuration; concurrent ("or"-style) use is what the overlap data support.**

**The authors' stated caveat on when nominations help (exact quote, via Marsili & Pellegrini's summary):** "Acar et al. (2016) claimed that nominations provide valuable information primarily **when teachers receive training on giftedness and when nominations are used to assess traits not captured by performance methods**." **[SECONDARY — quoted from Marsili & Pellegrini (2022), not read directly from Acar et al.]**

**FLAG — the criterion is a test, not an outcome.** Sensitivity of 59% means 59% agreement with performance-test classification. If the performance test is itself imperfect, these are consistency figures, not accuracy against ground truth. **No source in this document establishes ground truth independent of a test.**

This relation was **stronger when identification happened in primary school compared to secondary school** (p < .09). The effect size was **larger for achievement compared to ability** (p < .05)."
Studies published **after 2000: ES = .32** (43 effect sizes, p = .00). School level "Mixed": ES = .13 (12 effect sizes, p = .30, not significant).

**CROSS-CHECK AGAINST SECTION 1.** Marsili & Pellegrini summarize the teacher-judgment literature as follows (exact quote): "Other studies (Machts et al., 2016; Südkamp et al., 2012) found a high correlation among teacher judgments and measures of **intelligence (r = .50)** and an association even larger between teacher judgments and **academic achievement (r = .63)**. However, when creativity, artistic talent, leadership, and motivation were included in the giftedness definition, **the accuracy of teacher judgments was lower**."
**This r = .63 for achievement is close to Hoge & Coladarci's median of .66 — the two literatures converge.** But note the sharp contrast: **teachers correlate ~.63 with achievement and only ~.30 with gifted-identification measures.** Judging achievement and identifying giftedness are empirically different tasks with very different accuracy.

### 5.3 Lohman, Korb & Lakin (2008) — a true within-subjects three-way head-to-head

Full details in §2.1. Restated here as a head-to-head:
- **Same 1,198 children took all three tests** (Raven, NNAT, CogAT), counterbalanced order.
- **Agreement between the nonverbal tests: r = .60 to .65** for all pairings (slightly higher for non-ELL than ELL). Exact quote: "all fell within the range of r = .60 to .65. This is about the level that would be expected for figural reasoning tests that measure similar constructs."
- **They do NOT identify the same children at the top.** "when both NNAT and a test with good norms are administered to a group of children, **NNAT will appear to identify more than 3 times as many gifted children as the properly normed test**" (at NAI ≥ 130, Level A). "When put on the same scale as CogAT, Raven scores averaged **10 points higher**."
- **Author's warning about exploiting this disagreement (exact quote):** "The problem is further compounded when—in an effort to identify more students—additional tests that were normed on different populations are administered and **the highest score on any test is inappropriately taken as the best indicator of the student's ability**."

**This is a rare case where three methods were applied to the same students and the disagreement is quantified.** The pairwise correlations (.60–.65) look reassuring; the tail behavior (3.4× to 11.9× over-identification) does not. **Correlation in the middle of the distribution does not imply agreement at the cut score.**

### 5.6 What the head-to-head evidence converges on

Across four independent designs — a multilevel meta-analysis of diagnostic quadrants (Acar et al., 2016), a meta-analysis of nomination-test correlations (Marsili & Pellegrini, 2022), a within-subjects three-test comparison (Lohman et al., 2008), and a single-school method swap (Lidz & Macrine, 2001) — the same pattern appears:

1. **Different methods identify substantially different students.** Nomination-test correlations cluster at **r = .24 to .35** regardless of who nominates. Nonperformance methods find **59%** of what performance methods find.
2. **The disagreement is worst exactly where it matters: at the cut score.** Nonverbal tests correlate .60–.65 with each other overall but over-identify by **3.4× at NAI ≥ 130 and 11.9× at NAI ≥ 145**.
3. **No located source demonstrates that any single method is superior against an external outcome.** Every comparison uses another test as the criterion.
4. **The two meta-analyses independently converge on the same prescription: use methods concurrently, not sequentially.** Acar et al.: "They should be used concurrently rather than be used alone or consecutively." McBee et al. (simulation, §1.3): "A ROC curve analysis for the two-stage identification system would therefore suggest that the screening process be removed."

## Section 6 — Relative age effect inside gifted identification

### 6.1 Verification status of the 61% claim: PARTIAL. The citation chain is real; the primary number is NOT independently verified.

**The primary source EXISTS and is correctly cited.**

**Citation.** Maddux, C. D., Stacy, D., & Scott, M. (1981). School Entry Age in a Group of Gifted Children. *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 25(4), 180–184.

**DOI (verified).** `10.1177/001698628102500408` — Crossref returns title "School Entry Age in a Group of Gifted Children", container-title *Gifted Child Quarterly*, issued 1981-10, vol 25, issue 4, pp. 180–184, authors Cleborne D. **The secondary source EXISTS and is correctly cited.**

**Citation.** Musch, J., & Grondin, S. (2001). Unequal Competition as an Impediment to Personal Development: A Review of the Relative Age Effect in Sport. *Developmental Review*, 21(2), 147–167.

**DOI (verified).** `10.1006/drev.2000.0516` — Crossref returns title "Unequal Competition as an Impediment to Personal Development: A Review of the Relative Age Effect in Sport", container-title *Developmental Review*, issued 2001-06, vol 21, issue 2, pp. 147–167, authors Jochen Musch and Simon Grondin. **Matches the citation as written.** Musch & Grondin's reference list **does** contain Maddux, Stacy & Scott (1981) with the correct DOI, confirming the citation link is genuine.

### 6.2 WHAT I COULD NOT VERIFY, AND WHY IT MATTERS

**I could not retrieve the text of Maddux, Stacy & Scott (1981).** The article predates journal abstracts at *GCQ*; the SAGE record carries no abstract, and the full text is paywalled. **I therefore cannot confirm from the primary source what percentage it reports, what the N was, or how "relative age advantage" was operationalized.**

**The 61% figure appears only in secondary sources, and the secondary sources DISAGREE with each other.**

| Source | Figure reported | N reported | Wording |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brazilian dissertation (UFMG repository, citing Musch & Grondin, 2001) | **61%** | not given | "Maddux, Stacy e Scott (1981) observaram que em crianças consideradas superdotadas, **61% apresentam vantagens** relacionadas a esse Efeito da Idade Relativa" — *"in children considered gifted, 61% present advantages related to this Relative Age Effect"* |
| Search synthesis of Musch & Grondin (2001) | **61%** | not given | "in a group of children classified as gifted, 61% were advantaged by a late entry to grade one" |
| ERIC document ED307041 (https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED307041.pdf), reporting the March 1986 *Harvard Education Letter* | **60%** | **188** | "Maddux also found that **60% of the 188 children in the gifted program were in the older half of their class**" |

**Three problems with the claim as stated.**
1. **The number is either 60% or 61% depending on the source.** These may be two different Maddux analyses. The ERIC/Harvard Education Letter passage describes an analysis of "374 children with learning disabilities in grades one through twelve" alongside "188 children in the gifted program" — this profile matches a mid-1980s Maddux study, **not necessarily the 1981 Maddux, Stacy & Scott paper.** Maddux published several related papers: Maddux (1980), *Learning Disability Quarterly* 3, 79–83, DOI `10.2307/1510510`; Maddux, Stacy & Scott (1981), DOI `10.1177/001698628102500408`; Maddux (1983), *Roeper Review* 5(4), 15–17, DOI `10.1080/02783198309552716`; and Maddux, Stacy & Scott (1986), DOI `10.1177/093889828600200103`. **The 61% may have been transposed across these papers in the citation chain.**
2. **"61% had a relative-age advantage" is a weak effect if the operationalization is "older half of the class."** Under the null hypothesis of no relative age effect, the expected value is 50%. 60–61% vs 50% is a real but modest skew — roughly a 1.5:1 odds ratio, not the dramatic imbalance the framing implies. **Whether 61% was statistically significant, and against what baseline, cannot be established without the primary text.**
3. **I could not confirm the sample's country (presumed USA), the district, the grade span, the identification criteria used to label the children gifted, or the total N in the 1981 paper.**

### 6.3 THE STRONGEST AVAILABLE EVIDENCE ON THIS QUESTION CONTRADICTS THE SIMPLE VERSION OF THE CLAIM

**Citation.** Huang, F. L. (2015). Birthdate Effects and Gifted Program Participation in Kindergarten. *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 59(1), 14–22.

**DOI (verified).** `10.1177/0016986214559640` — Crossref returns title "Birthdate Effects and Gifted Program Participation in Kindergarten", container-title *Gifted Child Quarterly*, issued 2014-12-09 (online-first; print vol 59, issue 1, 2015), pp. 14–22, author Francis L. Huang. **Verified.**

**SAMPLE.** The **Early Childhood Longitudinal Study–Kindergarten Cohort (ECLS-K)** — a US nationally representative sample. Kindergarten. Full population ability range.

**METHOD (exact quote from the Crossref-registered abstract).** "Data were analyzed using multiple logistic regression analyses that accounted for the nested structure of the data and included student demographic variables as well as academic achievement skills as controls."

**RESULT (exact quote).** "Findings showed that even though a weak correlation indicated that relatively older students, on average, were more likely to be in a gifted and talented program, **the association disappeared once academic achievement skills were accounted for**."

**Framing quote from the same abstract:** "Research has suggested that relatively older children are more likely to be identified as gifted and talented students compared with their younger peers. Such a phenomenon disadvantages the youngest students while at the same time confers additional advantages to the older students as a result of receiving specialized and/or extra instruction."

**WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE RESEARCH QUESTION.** Huang (2015) is a nationally representative study with achievement controls, published 33 years after Maddux, Stacy & Scott, in the same journal, and it explicitly cites Maddux, Stacy & Scott (1981) in its reference list. Its finding is that **the raw relative-age skew in gifted programs is accounted for by achievement differences, not by identification bias per se** — at least at kindergarten, at least in the ECLS-K. The relatively older children in gifted programs were, on the measured achievement skills, actually higher achieving.

**Note on interpretation, stated as a limitation of the evidence rather than a conclusion:** controlling for concurrent achievement does not settle whether the achievement gap itself is an artifact of relative age. Huang's abstract does not address that. **This document does not resolve which reading is correct; it records that the two studies report different things.**

### 6.4 Summary of Section 6

- The **citations are real and correctly attributed**: Maddux, Stacy & Scott (1981) exists at DOI `10.1177/001698628102500408`; Musch & Grondin (2001) exists at DOI `10.1006/drev.2000.0516` and does cite it.
- The **61% figure could NOT be verified against the primary source.** A competing secondary source reports **60% of 188** children. **Do not cite "61%" as an established primary finding.**
- The **best-controlled modern study (Huang, 2015, ECLS-K, nationally representative) found the relative-age association in gifted program participation disappeared after controlling for academic achievement.**
