# BrainLift: The Selection Threshold — Where a Cutoff Belongs, What Fixing It Costs, and Whether the Level Is the Decision That Matters

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose
This BrainLift asks what a selection threshold is fit to do. It holds three questions apart that admissions practice runs together: where a cut belongs on the scale, what it costs to express the decision as a single fixed number, and whether the level of the cut is even the part of the architecture that determines who gets found. Each rests on a separate evidence base. A cutoff can be set at a defensible level and still sit inside a decision structure that loses most of the population it was built to find. The question is not whether a program should select. It is which property of the selection rule the evidence actually licenses a program to argue about.

### In Scope
- The provenance of conventional gifted cutoffs, and what those numbers were chosen to accomplish.
- Whether measured ability continues to predict accomplishment above a high cut, and what the threshold hypothesis survives.
- What crossing a threshold delivers to the student who crosses it, and which students the gain concentrates in.
- The mechanical cost of a fixed threshold: sensitivity at the cut before anything goes wrong, gates placed in front of the test, combination rules across measures, agreement across parallel forms, and the shape of the applicant distribution near the line.
- What selection decision theory says a threshold should be, given a base rate, a selection ratio, and an asymmetric loss.
- The alternatives on the record: local norms, universal screening, above-level testing, and posterior-probability rules.
- Provenance discipline: what is peer-reviewed, what is a working paper or vendor document, which numbers are computed rather than observed, and which sources do not transfer to a population above the 95th percentile.

### Out of Scope
- How well an instrument measures at the cut, including conditional error, classification consistency, differential item functioning read at the cutoff, and tail quality generally (`gifted-assessment-quality`). This BrainLift takes measurement precision as an input and asks what the decision rule does with it.
- Validating a new instrument against an incumbent (`test-evaluation`), which signals earn a place in an ability estimate (`metric-evidence`), routing and session design (`test-structure`), whether measured reasoning moves (`reasoning-growth`), whether a learning rate is estimable (`learning-rate-scoring`), causal identification of a program's effect (`gt-school-counterfactual`), where a student starts once admitted (`placement-and-dosing`), and whether the work is the applicant's own (`proctoring-integrity`).
- Choosing an instrument or a vendor, and mapping any of this onto a build, a configuration, or a live admissions policy. Those belong in a separate design document.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

> **Draft starting points, not finished positions.** Per the BrainLift method DOK 4 is the owner's to author and defend. What follows is scaffolding assembled from the DOK 2 tree below: each one is prescriptive and debatable, and each is written to be attacked, rewritten, or discarded. A stance assembled by someone else cannot be defended in a review, and the defense is the point. Interrogate each against the counter-position named at the end of its elaboration before keeping any of it.

**Draft SPOV 1: The cutoff level is the least evidence-backed number in gifted admissions and the most fiercely defended. No published source derives one. Anyone naming a percentile carries the burden of saying what it was chosen to accomplish, and the honest answer is almost always capacity.**

- **Elaboration:** Practice treats a cut score as a finding and treats challenges to it as ideology. The record inverts that. The founding federal document of American gifted education introduces its own famous figure with the words "It can be assumed that," states it as "a minimum of 3 to 5 percent," and picks it from a table the report itself lays out listing 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, and 15 percent as equally available candidates, in a section that opens "So we are dealing with estimates" (1.1). The other founding number is worse: Terman's IQ 140 is described in the peer-reviewed record as "a seemingly arbitrary methodological decision," and the criterion actually operated at 135 (1.2). Inside that same federal report, the operative percentage in the leading state program is a reimbursement formula, "2 percent of its enrollment ... x $40," with funds "limited in application to fewer than 5 percent of the pupils," and the report states the design intent plainly: the number was put in "administrative regulations and formulas for support rather than law" so that expenditure control was retained (1.3). A second state built a 2 to 5 percent program under an explicit instruction to add no new appropriation (1.3). Of fifty states surveyed, zero endorse a single-cutoff model and 64 percent stipulate no cut score at all (1.5). The co-author of the most widely used ability screen in American gifted identification writes that "Simple schemes that establish an arbitrary cut score on an IQ or achievement test are administratively convenient but identify only a fraction of those who will later attain excellence" (4.4). The strongest general statement is Borland's: "there is no empirical basis for choosing one over the other ... giftedness in the schools is something we confer, not something we discover" (1.3). Defenders of a fixed cut counter that a threshold's job is to be legible and auditable rather than optimal, and that a program which cannot state its line in one sentence will be litigated into one it likes less.

**Draft SPOV 2: Stop arguing about where the line goes. The number of lines and their order matter roughly ten times more, and that is measurable. A screening gate placed before the test is the single most expensive decision in an identification system, and moving the cut is a rounding error beside it.**

- **Elaboration:** This is the claim with the largest verified effect in the document, and it reframes the entire debate as an architecture question rather than a level question. Moving a cut from the 90th percentile to the 95th, holding a reliability of .95, changes sensitivity from .843 to .815, which is under three points (4.1). Placing a nomination or screening gate of validity .48 in front of that same test at the 90th percentile changes sensitivity from .843 to .280, a false-negative rate of .720 (4.2). That is a fifty-six point swing against a three point one. The mechanism is structural and the authors state it exactly: "the confirmatory assessment can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives because those students that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory test," and therefore "screeners cannot improve the sensitivity of an assessment system; they can only reduce it" (4.2). The same asymmetry was derived from Bayes' theorem sixty years earlier, in the observation that cases lost at stage one "are, of course, not picked up by Test 2 either, because it is not applied to them" (5.4). Requiring a candidate to clear two bars compounds it: at a top-5 percent cutoff with correlated measures at r = .27, a conjunctive rule identifies 0.65 percent against 9.3 percent under a compensatory one (4.3). And no amount of instrument quality repairs it, because a gate operates before the instrument runs. So the defensible order of operations is to fix the architecture first, then argue about the level, and a program that has not audited its gate ordering has no standing to defend its percentile. One genuine counterweight belongs in the same breath: a real-data analysis using an ability test's own standardization sample concluded that composition differences "were due mainly to the identified pool size and not to the effects of the particular combination rule," which would make the rule choice a decision about how many to admit rather than a free accuracy gain (4.3).

**Draft SPOV 3: Selecting harder buys a better class and no evidence of a better program. Those are different claims, and a threshold optimized for the first is anti-optimized for the second.**

- **Elaboration:** Both literatures are strong and they point opposite ways, which is why this is the hardest position here to hold. On one side, ability keeps predicting accomplishment far above any conventional cut. Within the top 1 percent of quantitative reasoning at age 13, top-quartile against bottom-quartile odds ratios run 2.7 for any doctorate, 6.1 for patents, 7.7 for tenure at a top-50 university, and 18.2 for STEM doctorates, all significant on 2,385 people (2.1). The authors' own words are that the data "collectively falsify the idea that after a certain point more ability does not matter," and decision theory agrees that stringency pays, since the usefulness of a valid predictor rises at an accelerating rate as the selection ratio tightens (2.1, 5.1). On the other side, the only regression-discontinuity evidence at an actual gifted cutoff finds that the program effect goes to zero exactly where that ability level sits. Splitting admitted students by ability, the gain rises monotonically as ability falls: 0.11 standard deviations and indistinguishable from zero for students at 1.30 sigma, against 0.50 for students at 0.42 sigma, with the authors concluding that "The gains appear to be largest for students who are closest to the threshold and furthest away from the traditional thresholds that determine entry to gifted programs in other settings" (3.1). A second discontinuity study found nothing for marginal students and, in its higher-ability lottery arm at 1.6 sigma, nothing but science (3.2). Read together, the selection literature answers "who will do well" and the program literature answers "who will do better because of us," and a percentile chosen to maximize the first systematically excludes the students who move most on the second. The reconciliation is not that one side is wrong. It is that a program has to say which question its cutoff is answering, and almost none do. The strongest objection is that both nulls were measured on grade-level state tests in classrooms following the same curriculum as everyone else (3.4), which is close to the worst possible instrument for detecting what an accelerated program does, so the top-end null may be an outcome-measure artifact rather than a finding.

**Draft SPOV 4: A cut score is a posterior probability, not a number, and at the tail the only honest output is a probability with a band attached. Any system that reports pass or fail at a high percentile is asserting a precision that no instrument in this field has.**

- **Elaboration:** The instability is documented by the people who build the instruments, which is what makes it hard to wave away. On two of the best individual intelligence tests correlating at r = .84, "only about half of the students who score in the top 3% on one test will also score in the top 3% on the second test," and at the top 1 percent the agreement runs from 13 percent when r = .50 to 76 percent when r = .975 (4.4). Even a single universally administered test with a reliability of .95 has a sensitivity of .815 at a 95th-percentile cut and .763 at a 99th, so between eighteen and twenty-four percent of genuinely qualifying children are missed before any gate, rule, or human judgment enters (4.1). And two error rates move in opposite directions as a cut tightens, which is the trap: the share of the whole pool misclassified falls while the false-negative rate among the truly qualified rises, from roughly nine percent at a median cut to twenty-one percent at a 99th-percentile one (4.5). The professional standards already require reporting standard error "in the vicinity of each cut score" and note that a band "might be depicted by error bands or likely score ranges" (5.5), and the leading gifted-education body already recommends that a score be accepted "if it falls within the confidence interval of the required score for admission" (1.4). A simulation replacing "observed score above the cutoff" with "probability that the true score is above the cutoff, above tau" beat a conventional conjunctive rule on sensitivity and precision at the same time, .88 against .72 and .85 against .82 (6.3). What nobody has built is the next step: no published work exists on a threshold that updates as outcome data accumulates (6.4). Two objections deserve answers. A band widens rather than resolves a decision, and someone still has to act at its edge. And the posterior form is only optimal if the probability is calibrated, which is an empirical claim about the instrument rather than a property of the arithmetic (5.3).

## Experts

> Chosen for productive tension. The first three entries are on record against each other about whether a higher cut buys anything, and the fourth argues that the level was never the operative variable.

### David Lohman (with Korb, Lakin, Renzulli); James Borland; Mary-Catherine McClain and Steven Pfeiffer
- **Who:** Gifted-identification measurement researchers and policy historians. Lohman co-authored the Cognitive Abilities Test; Borland argues for abolishing the gifted category; McClain and Pfeiffer surveyed all fifty states' definitions and cut scores.
- **Focus:** Where cut scores come from, how stable category membership actually is across parallel forms, and whether any level has an empirical parent.
- **Why Follow:** Lohman is the insider witness against the practice his own instrument enables. He calls arbitrary cut scores "administratively convenient," quantifies that only about half of a top-3-percent group repeats on a second good test, and observes that on the upper tail "many more students will be near that cut score than far above it." Borland supplies the general claim that giftedness in schools "is something we confer, not something we discover." McClain and Pfeiffer supply the finding that zero of fifty states endorse a single-cutoff model.
- **Where:** [Lohman (2005), NRC/GT RM05216](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505376.pdf) | [McClain & Pfeiffer (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15377903.2012.643757) | [Borland (2005), excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805215/47307/excerpt/9780521547307_excerpt.pdf)

### Camilla Benbow, David Lubinski, Gregory Park, Kimberley Ferriman Robertson, Jonathan Wai
- **Who:** The Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, a longitudinal program tracking cohorts identified in the top 1 percent by age 13 across four decades.
- **Focus:** Whether ability differences continue to predict educational, occupational, and creative accomplishment inside an already-selected tail.
- **Why Follow:** They are the strongest evidence that a higher cut is not wasted, and they name the exact claim they are refuting: that "above a certain threshold of cognitive ability (for example, an ability level in the 90th or 95th percentile), differences in cognitive abilities no longer matter." Their own stated methodological requirement is the useful part for anyone testing this: high predictor ceilings, low-base-rate outcomes, large samples, and long follow-up. Read their sample floor carefully, since it sits at the top-1-percent boundary rather than the 95th percentile.
- **Where:** [Robertson, Smeets, Lubinski & Benbow (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721410391442) | [Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.97.3.484) | [Park, Lubinski & Benbow (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02182.x)

### David Card and Laura Giuliano; Sa Bui, Steven Craig and Scott Imberman
- **Who:** Labor economists running regression-discontinuity and lottery designs at real gifted-program admission thresholds in large public districts.
- **Focus:** What crossing the line causes, and which admitted students the effect concentrates in.
- **Why Follow:** They are the direct counter-pole to the previous entry, and the disagreement is empirical rather than rhetorical. Card and Giuliano find the treatment effect rising monotonically as admitted-student ability falls, with the band matching a conventional gifted threshold gaining 0.11 standard deviations and not distinguishable from zero. Bui and colleagues find nothing for marginal students despite peer achievement rising by 0.24 to 0.35 standard deviations. Both are also the clearest case study in outcome-measure limits, since the tests are grade-level and the classrooms follow the standard curriculum.
- **Where:** [Card & Giuliano (2014), NBER w20453](https://doi.org/10.3386/w20453) | [Card & Giuliano (2016), PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113) | [Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.6.3.30)

### Matthew McBee, Scott Peters, Craig Waterman, Erin Miller; Joni Lakin
- **Who:** Psychometricians modelling identification systems as whole decision architectures rather than as instruments.
- **Focus:** What a nomination gate, a combination rule, and a cutoff level each cost in sensitivity, computed rather than surveyed.
- **Why Follow:** They supply the numbers that make the architecture argument quantitative, including the finding that a screening gate can push a false-negative rate past 60 percent and their own recommendation to "Consider abolishing the two-stage system completely." Lakin belongs here as the internal dissent: using real standardization data she attributes composition differences mainly to pool size rather than to the combination rule, which is the strongest available objection to the rest of the group's conclusions.
- **Where:** [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256) | [McBee & Makel (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007) | [Lakin (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986217752099)

### Paul Meehl and Albert Rosen; H. C. Taylor and J. T. Russell; Charles Metz; Samuel Livingston and Charles Lewis
- **Who:** The decision theorists and psychometricians who formalized what a cutting score is worth given a base rate, a selection ratio, and an asymmetric loss.
- **Focus:** When a valid test still makes worse decisions than the base rate, how selection stringency changes a predictor's value, where an optimal threshold sits under unequal error costs, and how classification accuracy varies with the position of the cut.
- **Why Follow:** They provide the frame in which "which percentile" becomes answerable instead of conventional, and they disagree with each other in useful ways. Taylor and Russell show that stringency increases a predictor's usefulness at an accelerating rate, which argues for a high cut. Meehl and Rosen show that a low base rate can make positive calls wrong more often than right, and they are the ones who point out that false negatives are "of little concern to management" in industrial selection but are not ignorable for an institution with an obligation to those it rejects.
- **Where:** [Meehl & Rosen (1955)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048070) | [Taylor & Russell (1939)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0057079) | [Livingston & Lewis (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x)

## DOK 3: Insights

> **Draft starting points, not finished insights.** DOK 3 is the owner's to author. Each candidate below names the facts it bridges and the counter-argument it has to survive. An insight becomes a DOK 3 insight when the owner has interrogated it and can defend it without this document open. Provocation questions follow the list.

**Draft Insight 1 - Every number in this literature that survived did so because it was cheap to administer, and every number that was derived from evidence was abandoned. The convention is a record of operational constraints, not of findings.**
- Three facts nobody combines. The founding 3-to-5-percent figure was selected from a menu of six candidate percentages in a section that opens by admitting "we are dealing with estimates" (1.1). In the same document, the operative percentage in the leading state program is literally a reimbursement formula and the report states that placing it in a funding regulation rather than in law was deliberate, so that "the need for expenditures control by the State education agency was met" (1.3). And the field's most careful survey finds that of the eighteen states that do publish cut scores, the spread runs from the 90th to the 97th percentile on different constructs with no stated derivation for any of them (1.5). Meanwhile the one number in the corpus that *was* derived from an outcome comparison, Renzulli's 15 to 20 percent, came from a study finding that a broader pool "produced equally good Type III products as the traditional 'gifted' students," and it is treated as an outlier rather than as the only empirically grounded figure available (6.1, 7.3). Defenders counter that convergent professional judgment is evidence of a kind, and that the 82 percent of surveyed experts who would confine giftedness to 5 percent or less were not all reasoning from funding formulas.

**Draft Insight 2 - The threshold hypothesis and the program-effect evidence make the same mistake in opposite directions, and both mistakes are visible in the sample descriptions rather than in the analyses.**
- The claim that ability stops mattering above a cut fails on power and range: the only breakpoint near the conventional figure was not statistically significant and had 65 participants above it, and the studies that deliberately oversampled high ability found no threshold at all (2.3, 2.4). The claim that a gifted program does nothing for high-ability students fails on outcome ceiling: the tests are grade level, the classrooms follow the standard curriculum, and the design cannot see acceleration by construction (3.4). So the two literatures most often cited against each other are both under-identified at the tail, in mirror-image ways. What follows is not that either is wrong but that a program above the 95th percentile is arguing about its own cutoff using evidence collected almost entirely below it. The one design in the corpus built specifically for this problem is above-level testing, where participants "have in-grade test scores in the top 3% to 5%" yet their above-level scores are "normally distributed, with scores covering most or all of the range of the instrument" (6.2). Critics counter that demanding tail-specific evidence for every claim is a standard no education literature meets, and that a consistent finding across many imperfect designs is worth more than an absent perfect one.

**Draft Insight 3 - Selection stringency and program benefit are governed by different variables, so a single percentile cannot optimize both, and the field has never made anyone choose.**
- Decision theory says a valid predictor becomes more useful as the selection ratio tightens, and at an accelerating rate: the mean standard score of those selected rises from 0.798 at a fifty-percent ratio to 2.063 at five percent, with the marginal gain per unit of stringency climbing monotonically (5.1). The SMPY record says the underlying trait keeps predicting inside the tail, with odds ratios from 2.7 to 18.2 (2.1). Both argue for a high cut. The discontinuity evidence says the causal gain is concentrated where ability is lowest among those admitted, at 0.50 standard deviations for students at 0.42 sigma against 0.11 and non-significant at 1.30 sigma (3.1). That argues for a low one. No source in this corpus states that these are answers to different questions, because no source cites both bodies of work. Once stated, the consequence is uncomfortable and specific: a program that raises its cut is improving the expected accomplishment of its admitted class while removing the students on whom it can demonstrate an effect. The counter is that a program with a fixed number of seats is not choosing between these goals, since an externally imposed selection ratio means every admission displaces exactly one rejection and, as Meehl and Rosen note, that "eliminates the often troublesome value questions" for ranking purposes (5.4).

**Draft Insight 4 - Misclassification at a cut is a property of the applicant pool's shape, not only of the instrument, which means an admissions rule degrades when a program becomes more attractive.**
- The mechanism is in the equations and the confirming data is in a different paper. Expected misclassification is an integral of error spillover across the cut multiplied by the density of examinees at that score, so the pool's own distribution is one of two inputs (4.5). The empirical confirmation is a single test scored against seven hypothetical cut-points where accuracy falls from 99.1 percent at a 1st-percentile cut to 90.9 percent at a median cut while the conditional standard error stays essentially flat at 7.29 against 7.40, so the variation cannot be attributed to precision (4.5). And the CogAT's own co-author supplies the fact that makes this bite on the upper tail specifically: "no matter where we set the cut score on the upper tail of the score distribution, many more students will be near that cut score than far above it" (4.4). Put together, a program whose applicants self-select against a published requirement is assembling exactly the pool shape that maximizes misclassification at its own line, and it gets worse as the program's reputation improves. Two honest limits travel with this. The density explanation for the accuracy pattern is an inference across sources rather than a claim either paper makes. And the direction reverses depending on which error you count, since the share of the pool misclassified falls at a stringent cut even as the false-negative rate among the qualified rises (4.5). Skeptics counter that a program cannot choose its applicant pool, so this describes a condition rather than a decision.

**Draft Insight 5 - Universal screening and local norms are usually offered as the same reform and do opposite things to program size, which matters more than the equity framing they share.**
- Local norms are a reallocation. At a top-5-percent cutoff the identified pool moves from 2.90 percent to 3.05 percent in reading and actually shrinks slightly in mathematics, while representation indices swing dramatically across groups (6.1). Universal screening is an expansion: gifted classification rose from 1.4 to 4.3 percent for one eligibility pathway and from 5.1 to 6.6 percent for the other, with the entire gain reversing within about a year once funding was cut (6.1). The detail that transfers to a selective program is not the equity result but this one: "A full 20% of the compliers have IQs of 130 or higher," so screening surfaced children who cleared the existing high bar and whom the referral process had simply never sent for testing (6.1). That is the same structural fact as the nomination-gate finding arriving from the other direction, and it means the missing students were missing for administrative rather than psychometric reasons. Note also the limit that kills the local-norms option for a nationally recruiting program: the entire logic is within-building, and the authors state that districts "will find it impossible to take the top 5% or 15% of any group if <100% of the group is tested." Defenders of local norms counter that the point was never pool size and that reallocation toward under-identified groups is the intended effect rather than a shortfall.

**Provocation questions for the owner, in the order most likely to produce a real insight:**
- Draft Insight 3 says the two literatures answer different questions. If a program had to pick one, which choice can it actually defend to a family it rejected, and does that answer change if seats are not scarce?
- If a screening gate costs fifty-six points of sensitivity and the cutoff level costs three, what does it say about the field that essentially all public argument is about the level?
- The most-cited number in gifted education is an assumption drawn from a menu, and the only empirically derived figure available is treated as fringe. What has to be true about a field for that to happen, and does the same pattern appear in the instruments themselves?
- Above-level testing is the only design in this corpus built to discriminate inside the top few percent, and it is fifty years old. Why has nothing replaced it, and is the reason technical or commercial?
- Nothing has ever been published on a threshold that updates from outcome data. Is that because it is hard, because nobody has the data, or because a moving cut is unacceptable for reasons that have nothing to do with measurement?

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

### Overall Summary

No located source derives any specific gifted-identification cutoff. The founding federal figure of 3 to 5 percent is introduced as an assumption, stated as a minimum rather than a ceiling, and chosen from a table of six candidate percentages; the other founding number, IQ 140, is described in the peer-reviewed record as arbitrary and was operated at 135. Inside that same federal report the operative percentage in the leading state program is a reimbursement formula, and a second state's range was built under an instruction to add no new money. Of fifty states surveyed, none endorses a single-cutoff model and identification rates range from about 2 percent to 19 percent. Whether a higher cut buys anything splits cleanly by outcome: measured ability keeps predicting accomplishment well inside the top 1 percent, with odds ratios from 2.7 to 18.2, while the threshold hypothesis has no traceable original source, was opposed by the author it is attributed to, and appears only for creative potential rather than creative achievement. Against that, the only regression-discontinuity evidence at real gifted thresholds finds the causal gain largest for the lowest-ability admitted students and indistinguishable from zero at conventional-threshold ability, measured on grade-level tests in standard-curriculum classrooms. Mechanically, a fixed threshold is expensive before any judgment enters: a single reliability-.95 test at a 95th-percentile cut has a sensitivity of .815, only about half of a top-3-percent group repeats on a second good test, a screening gate in front of the test can drive false negatives past 70 percent, and misclassification is proportional to the density of applicants at the line. Decision theory says stringency raises a valid predictor's value at an accelerating rate while a low base rate can make positive calls wrong more often than right, and that the optimal threshold under unequal error costs is the false-positive share of total error cost, which requires a calibrated probability rather than a score. The alternatives on the record are local norms, which reallocate a fixed-size pool and require a building to norm within, universal screening, which expands identification and reverses when funding stops, and above-level testing, which is the only design built to discriminate inside the top few percent. Nothing has been published on a threshold that updates as outcome data accumulates.

### Category 1: Where the Conventional Cutoff Came From, and What It Was Chosen to Accomplish

- **Subcategory 1.1: The founding federal figure is an assumption drawn from a menu, and it is a minimum rather than a ceiling**
  - **Source:** Marland (1972), *Education of the Gifted and Talented, Volume 1: Report to the Congress of the United States by the U.S. Commissioner of Education*
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The definition's closing clause reads: "It can be assumed that utilization of these criteria for identification of the gifted and talented will encompass a **minimum** of 3 to 5 percent of the school population." Two features are usually dropped in citation: "a minimum of," and "It can be assumed."
      - The report's section headed "What is a Good Estimate of the Number of Gifted and Talented Children?" opens by stating "So we are dealing with estimates. Numbers presumed to be gifted or talented have varied considerably in recent estimates."
      - That section presents Table 1, "Numbers of Pupils in Various Percentage Groups to be Gifted and Talented," listing six candidate percentages against a 1970 population projection of 51,600,000: 1 percent (516,000), 2 percent (1,032,000), 3 percent (1,548,000), 5 percent (2,580,000), 10 percent (5,160,000), and 15 percent (7,740,000).
      - The report selects from that menu by judgment, noting that some put the figure at 3 percent "while others would range as far as 15 percent," that 15 "may be too broad," and that "even taking the very conservative estimate of 3 percent, the size of the population, 1.5 million, demands attention."
      - The report records the pre-existing convention it inherited: "Up to the end of the 1950's, research workers and other experts agreed that the gifted included those within the upper 2 to 3 percent of intellectual ability, defined as a Binet IQ of 130 or more."
      - The report states the sequence of its own reasoning: the advisory panel wrote the definition first, and "This definition was subsequently tested through the Advocate Survey and in the research review." The expert poll was a post-hoc check rather than the source of the figure.
      - In that poll, 82 percent would confine the gifted to 5 percent or less, and 38 percent to 2 percent or less.
      - A later national survey records that the figure has been read backwards in practice: "Some experts have suggested that the 3–5% estimate was proposed as a minimum upper limit and not a specific threshold, thus preventing any superintendent from claiming that their district had no gifted students... However, the 3–5% upper limit for defining gifted students became, in the minds of many, including state education policymakers, something real."
      - Provenance note: the sentence appears more than once in the document and published citations disagree on its page (p. 2 against pp. 10–11). The canonical page is unresolved. Quotations above are reconstructed from a scan with OCR artifacts, corrected only where the intended word is unambiguous.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The most cited number in American gifted education is presented by its own authors as an assumption, in a section that opens by conceding it is dealing with estimates, chosen from a list of six percentages the report itself supplies. It was written as a floor to stop districts claiming they had no gifted children, and it has been operated ever since as a ceiling. There is no derivation anywhere in the section: no standard-setting procedure, no validity argument, and no outcome data.
    - **Link to source:** [Marland Report, ERIC ED056243](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED056243.pdf) | [McClain & Pfeiffer (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15377903.2012.643757)

- **Subcategory 1.2: The other founding number is described in the peer-reviewed record as arbitrary, and it was operated two thirds of a standard deviation lower than advertised**
  - **Source:** Simonton (2016); ICPSR Study 8092
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On the origin of IQ 140: "But where did this definition come from and how was the number 140 determined? It turns out that this concept emerged less than a century ago as **a seemingly arbitrary methodological decision** made in 1921 by Lewis M. Terman."
      - "The initial cutoff for inclusion in this elite sample was a score of 140, placing the resulting group in the top 1% of the school population."
      - On how the number entered common use: the results were "published with the title *Genetic Studies of Genius*, thus inadvertently establishing the IQ 140 definition of genius. Terman's methodological choice consequently entered the English language."
      - The authoritative data archive describes the design intent as identifying children "in the top one percent of the population" but records the operative criterion as "a Stanford Binet Intelligence test score of **135 or more**." Final N = 1,528, median IQ 147.
      - Two boys rejected from the sample as insufficiently gifted, William Shockley and Luis Alvarez, later won Nobel Prizes in physics. Not one member of the sample did.
      - Provenance note: the *reason* the threshold was lowered from 140 to 135, reported as being "to enlist enough children," rests on a single 1995 newspaper feature and is not peer-reviewed. The fact of 135 is independently corroborated by the data archive. No first-hand Terman statement acknowledging the arbitrariness was located; every "arbitrary" characterization here is secondary.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The threshold that gave the English language its definition of genius is characterized in the peer-reviewed literature as a methodological convenience, and the study actually ran two thirds of a standard deviation below its advertised line. The sample selected on it produced no Nobel laureates while rejecting two. Read carefully, this is not evidence that 140 is the wrong number. It is evidence that nobody ever argued it was the right one.
    - **Link to source:** [Simonton (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13054) | [ICPSR 8092](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/8092/datadocumentation)

- **Subcategory 1.3: Where a percentage is traceable at all, it traces to a funding formula or a capacity constraint**
  - **Source:** Marland (1972); Borland (2005); McClain & Pfeiffer (2012); Lakin (Riverside Insights)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On the leading state program of the era: "The administrative regulation controlling expenditure for the gifted and talented is a formula which allows the district to use **2 percent of its enrollment in applying for reimbursement; for example, Reimbursement = 2% (enrollment) x $40.** In seeking to meet a variety of special abilities, districts may involve as many as 5 percent of their pupils."
      - The same program's funds were "limited in application to fewer than 5 percent of the pupils enrolled in the district," providing "only an average of $28 per pupil each year."
      - The report states the design intent explicitly: "the planners recognized that **allocation of funds requires description of the special category; but they recommend that this description be made in administrative regulations and formulas for support rather than law.** Thus, flexibility was retained while the need for expenditures control by the State education agency was met."
      - A second state's program was built under an explicit budget constraint: "The State Superintendent of Schools asked that present State laws and operations be examined to see how special programs could be established **with no additional appropriation by the General Assembly.**" The resulting programs served students "whose mental ability places them in the upper 2 to 5 percent."
      - The general claim, stated flatly: "there is no empirical basis for choosing one over the other, or over any of the scores of others that have been proposed, because, I maintain, defining giftedness is a matter of values and policy, not empirical research... **giftedness in the schools is something we confer, not something we discover. It is a matter of educational policy, not a matter of scientific diagnosis.**"
      - From the fifty-state survey: "**there is no single right answer for what IQ threshold or percentage of students should be identified as gifted, and the numbers can change depending on changing criteria of academic excellence and available resources.**"
      - Vendor guidance from the publisher of the most widely used ability screen states capacity management as a rationale for local norms: "local norms would allow the school to **manage the size of their gifted services population.**"
      - Provenance note: no modern state or district document was located stating in so many words that a percentile was chosen because of seat count. The funding-formula evidence is from 1972. The author of the "confer, not discover" claim argues a strong revisionist thesis (abolishing the gifted label), though his descriptive point is echoed by researchers who reject his conclusion.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Every instance where a specific percentage can be traced to a decision, rather than to a convention, resolves to money or capacity. One state's threshold was literally the multiplier in a reimbursement equation, and the report says putting it in a regulation rather than in statute was deliberate expenditure control. Another state's range was reverse-engineered from an instruction to spend nothing new. The strongest general statement in the corpus is that the category is conferred rather than discovered, and the most careful survey of practice agrees that available resources are one of the inputs.
    - **Link to source:** [Marland Report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED056243.pdf) | [Borland (2005), excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805215/47307/excerpt/9780521547307_excerpt.pdf) | [McClain & Pfeiffer (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15377903.2012.643757)

- **Subcategory 1.4: Neither federal law nor the field's professional body specifies a percentage, and the professional body recommends interval-based decisions**
  - **Source:** 20 U.S.C. § 7801(27); 20 U.S.C. § 7294; National Association for Gifted Children position statements
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The current statutory definition defines gifted and talented as students "who give evidence of high achievement capability in areas such as intellectual, creative, artistic, or leadership capacity, or in specific academic fields, and who need services or activities not ordinarily provided by the school in order to fully develop those capabilities." No percentage, no test, no score.
      - The federal gifted-education program statute contains no percentage, no cut score, and no numeric identification criterion of any kind. It directs priority toward identifying students "who may not be identified and served through traditional assessment methods."
      - The 1993 federal definition frames the comparison as relative to "others of their age, **experience, or environment**," a clause later researchers note is in potential conflict with national norms, which capture age but not experience or environment.
      - The professional body offers a percentage only as a hedged guide: "many consider children who are in the top 10 percent in relation to a national and/or local norm to be a good guide for identification and services," prefaced by the statement that the calculation "is dependent on the number of areas, or domains, being measured and the method used."
      - Its position statement on assessment lists as a non-negotiable that "identification of gifted and talented students **should not be based on a single assessment**," and that such assessments "should never be used in isolation to identify gifted children." The statement contains no recommended percentile, IQ threshold, or cut score of any kind.
      - On a specific instrument, the same body recommends treating the cut as an interval: any of six listed scores "should be acceptable for use in the selection process for gifted programs **if it falls within the confidence interval of the required score for admission.**"
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The two institutions with the most authority to name a number have both declined to. Federal law defines the category in terms of need for services and says nothing numeric. The field's professional body offers "top 10 percent" as something many people consider a good guide, forbids single-assessment identification, and on the one instrument where it gets specific, recommends accepting any score whose confidence interval reaches the required one. The interval recommendation matters because it is the field's own body conceding that a cut score is not a line.
    - **Link to source:** [20 U.S.C. § 7801(27)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/7801) | [20 U.S.C. § 7294](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/7294) | [NAGC, Role of Assessments](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/nagc/Position_Statements/the_role_of_assessments_in_t.pdf) | [NAGC, WISC-V](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED600122.pdf)

- **Subcategory 1.5: In practice the cutoff varies so widely that no consensus figure exists, and no state endorses a single cut**
  - **Source:** McClain & Pfeiffer (2012); Peters, Rambo-Hernandez, Makel, Matthews & Plucker (2019); federal Civil Rights Data Collection
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across all fifty states: 96 percent have an established definition of giftedness; 36 percent stipulate specific test cut scores; 64 percent stipulate no specific tests or cut scores; 54 percent endorse multiple-cutoff or averaging models; and **zero percent endorse a single-cutoff model**.
      - Among the states that do publish cut scores, the spread runs across both level and construct: one requires the 95th percentile on an IQ test, another the 97th percentile on an IQ test, another the 90th percentile on an achievement test, and another the 95th percentile on a measure of creativity.
      - Typical practice on individually administered tests spans "a score of 120, 125, or 130."
      - On the variability of identification outcomes: "**These authors found that across settings at the elementary level nationwide, the percentage of students identified as gifted ranged from zero to 50%.**"
      - Practicing researchers modelling this could not identify a consensus figure: "**Because of the lack of consensus regarding the actual percentage of the population that should be labeled gifted,** for the purposes of this article we chose to model two gifted identification rates (5% and 15%)."
      - State-level identification rates from federal civil-rights data show the lowest overall rate at **1.98 percent** and the highest at **19.33 percent**, close to a tenfold spread. By locale: rural 7.6 percent, urban 8.6 percent, suburban 8.9 percent.
      - The majority of districts using test-based identification use national rather than local norms, at 57 percent.
      - Provenance note: the "zero to 50%" range is a secondary citation and the underlying survey's sampling frame was not verified. The state-rate analysis is by an advocacy organization using an authoritative federal dataset and is not peer-reviewed. The survey's own stated limitation is that it did not collect district-level practice, so state policy may not reflect what districts do.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is no conventional cutoff to defer to. Two thirds of states decline to name one, none endorses a single cut, and the states that do publish numbers disagree on both the level and what is being measured. Identification rates in the field vary by roughly a factor of ten between states, and researchers modelling the question in 2019 had to run two different rates because no consensus existed to assume. A program citing "the standard cutoff" is citing something that does not exist.
    - **Link to source:** [McClain & Pfeiffer (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15377903.2012.643757) | [Peters et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419848446) | [AdvancED Equity, CRDC analysis](https://www.advancedequity.org/briefs/gt-rates)

### Category 2: Whether Measured Ability Keeps Predicting Above a High Cut

- **Subcategory 2.1: Inside the top 1 percent, ability differences still predict accomplishment decades later**
  - **Source:** Robertson, Smeets, Lubinski & Benbow (2010); Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2005)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The claim under test is named explicitly: "Conventional wisdom holds that above a certain threshold of cognitive ability (**for example, an ability level in the 90th or 95th percentile**), differences in cognitive abilities no longer matter."
      - Sample: N = 2,385, identified in the top 1 percent on quantitative reasoning by age 13, split into quartiles with "almost 600 participants in each quartile," tracked into their thirties. The qualifying score was 390 against a maximum of 800, "leaving ample room for observation of individual differences."
      - Top-quartile against bottom-quartile odds ratios within that top 1 percent, all reported significant: any doctorate 2.7; income in the 95th percentile 3.3; any peer-reviewed publication 4.5; STEM publications 5.9; patents 6.1; STEM tenure at a top-50 university 7.7; **STEM doctorates 18.2**.
      - In percentage terms: "in the bottom quartile (Q1) of the top 1%... 15.4% have earned a doctorate... But the corresponding proportion for the top quartile (Q4) is 33.2%."
      - An earlier study on a different sample (N = 1,975 analytic, top 1 percent, surveyed near age 33) found the same pattern: doctorates 32.1 percent against 20.0 percent; math and science PhDs 9.8 against 2.5; patents 7.5 against 3.8; tenure at a top-50 university 3.2 against 0.38. All Cohen's h between .16 and .31, all significant.
      - Those authors' stated conclusion: the results "collectively **falsify** the idea that after a certain point more ability does not matter."
      - Conditioning on credential does not remove it. Holding terminal degree constant and quartiling by age-13 ability within degree group, top-against-bottom odds ratios for STEM outcomes ran 3.07 to 12.03 among master's and doctorate holders. Within top-15 graduate schools the Fortune 500 patent odds ratio was 18.68 against 5.22 elsewhere.
      - The authors' own limitations: the predictor ceilings out, since top-quartile doctorate holders averaged 702 of a possible 800 and several scored the maximum, and they state that "When the variability in predictors and criteria is restricted because there is not enough room at the top, the observed covariance between attributes and outcomes is severely attenuated." Participants averaged about one standard deviation above the normative mean in socioeconomic status. Female-only analyses often failed to reach significance because of smaller samples and lower base rates.
      - Range note that matters for transfer: the bottom quartile's mean quantitative score sits at 441 to 495 against qualifying cutoffs of 390 and 500, so the comparison is inside the 99th percentile and above. This evidence does not directly speak to the band between the 95th and 99th percentiles.
      - Provenance note: the two studies report different doctorate percentages for the same nominal comparison (33.2 against 15.4; 32.1 against 20.0) because the samples and quartile construction differ. They should not be averaged or presented as one estimate.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The strongest evidence against an ability threshold comes from tracking people who were already in the top 1 percent and asking whether differences inside that group still mattered. They did, on seven outcomes, by factors of three to eighteen, and they still mattered after holding degree and institution constant. Two caveats limit how far this carries. The predictor hits its ceiling, which the authors say attenuates what they can observe. And the comparison group at the bottom sits at the top-1-percent boundary, so this is evidence about the 99th percentile and above rather than about a 95th-percentile line.
    - **Link to source:** [Robertson et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721410391442) | [Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.97.3.484) | [Park, Lubinski & Benbow (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02182.x)

- **Subcategory 2.2: The threshold figure everyone cites has no traceable original source, and the author it is attributed to opposed it**
  - **Source:** Jauk, Benedek, Dunst & Neubauer (2013); Weiss, Steger, Schroeders & Wilhelm (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On the origin: "it seems that **none of the sources that are usually quoted when the threshold hypothesis is concerned (e.g., Guilford, 1967) explicitly assert that the threshold should be fixed at an IQ of 120.** It hence appears that, even in absence of any empirical evidence for an IQ-threshold at 120, this very specific assumption of the threshold hypothesis has hardly ever been questioned or examined thoroughly."
      - A second source in the same literature: "it is not known why the threshold is established at 120 points rather than a few IQ points more or less."
      - What the attributed author actually wrote: "high IQ is not a sufficient condition for high [divergent production] ability; it is almost a necessary condition."
      - On the translation error: "Intelligence has been declared as a necessary but not sufficient condition for creativity, which was subsequently **(erroneously) translated** into the so-called threshold hypothesis." And: "The threshold hypothesis is often attributed to Guilford, though he intended for a necessary but not sufficient condition... **In fact, he opposed the idea of a threshold based on empirical findings (1973).**"
      - On the state of the literature: "**Strikingly, almost as many different thresholds existed as did studies.**" Sample sizes across the reviewed studies ranged from 88 to 12,255, and "sample size did not seem to affect the results systematically."
      - The popular-culture version, quoted verbatim in the peer-reviewed literature: "The relationship between success and IQ works only up to a point. Once someone has an IQ of somewhere around 120, having additional IQ points doesn't seem to translate into any measurable real-world advantage."
      - Provenance note: no source attributing the IQ-120 figure specifically to Torrance or MacKinnon was located, despite the attribution being common. The Guilford quotations are secondary; the 1967 and 1973 originals were not read.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The number that licenses every "past a certain point it stops mattering" argument does not appear in the sources it is credited to. Two independent peer-reviewed papers state affirmatively that no such source exists, one documents that the original claim was "necessary but not sufficient" and that its author later argued against a threshold on evidence, and a review notes there are nearly as many proposed thresholds as there are studies proposing them. The version most decision-makers have actually encountered is from a popular book.
    - **Link to source:** [Jauk et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2013.03.003) | [Weiss et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence8040038)

- **Subcategory 2.3: Where a threshold is found at all it is for creative potential, never for creative achievement, and the breakpoint near the cited figure was not significant**
  - **Source:** Jauk, Benedek, Dunst & Neubauer (2013); Preckel, Holling & Wiese (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sample: 297 adults, mean age 30.4, recruited via newspaper and university mailing lists, deliberately heterogeneous rather than academic. Method: segmented regression with iterative breakpoint estimation.
      - Results by criterion. Ideational fluency: breakpoint IQ 86.09, significant, with 21 people below it. Top-2 originality: breakpoint IQ 104.00, significant. Average originality: breakpoint IQ 119.60, **not significant at p = .14**, with 65 people above it. **Creative achievement: no breakpoint detected, p = .64.**
      - The abstract states it plainly: "**no threshold was found for creative achievement**, i.e. creative achievement benefits from higher intelligence even at fairly high levels of intellectual ability."
      - These authors explicitly endorse the no-threshold finding for accomplishment, citing the top-1-percent longitudinal work by name and concluding that "Individual differences in the upper range of intellectual ability predicted creative occupational accomplishments as well as achievement in the arts and science."
      - An independent test on 1,328 students aged 12 to 16 including specialized schools for the gifted: "**Threshold theory was not supported.** The results indicate that for ages 12–16 years, correlations between creativity (divergent thinking) and intelligence are of comparable size throughout the whole ability range."
      - A meta-analysis estimated the intelligence-creativity correlation below and above IQ 120 at r = .20 and r = .23 respectively, rejecting the threshold. Provenance note: this figure is a secondary report and the original was not read.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The one modern study most often cited as supporting a threshold found three breakpoints, and the only one near the conventional figure failed significance with 65 people above it. For creative achievement, as opposed to performance on divergent-thinking tasks, that study found no breakpoint at all and its authors endorsed the opposing evidence by name. A separate test that deliberately included gifted schools found equal correlations across the whole range. So the distinction that survives is not high against low ability, it is potential against achievement.
    - **Link to source:** [Jauk et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2013.03.003) | [Preckel, Holling & Wiese (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2005.06.022)

- **Subcategory 2.4: Apparent thresholds are reproducible artifacts of unequal variance, arbitrary splits, and analytic choice**
  - **Source:** Sligh, Conners & Roskos-Ewoldsen (2005); Karwowski & Gralewski (2013); Weiss, Steger, Schroeders & Wilhelm (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The variance critique, stated by its authors: "Much of the support for this hypothesis comes from comparisons of correlations within average and high-IQ groups. However, **a common methodological flaw in these comparisons is that the high IQ group is smaller in variance than the average IQ group.** In the present study we equated variances for a better test of the threshold hypothesis."
      - Their result: "IQ and creativity correlated at least as strongly in the high IQ group as in the average IQ group, generally failing to support the threshold hypothesis," with an *inverse* threshold effect for fluid ability, which "correlated significantly with creativity in the high IQ group but not in the average IQ group."
      - The analytic-choice critique, on 921 students analysed three ways with three candidate thresholds: "**confirmation or rejection of the TH depends strongly on both an analytical strategy and theoretical decisions.**" Under a lenient criterion the threshold appears at IQ 115; under a stricter one requiring higher correlations below than above, "the TH is less likely to be confirmed." The authors conclude the hypothesis "may actually be an artifact of data analysis."
      - The most comprehensive reappraisal, on two samples of 456 and 438 using three methods including local structural equation models that avoid dichotomizing: "**We found no evidence for the threshold hypothesis of creativity across different analytical procedures in both studies.**" And on why the literature disagrees: "the heterogeneity is mostly due to the use of **suboptimal data analytical procedures.**"
      - They tested and rejected the obvious defense that null results come from samples lacking high-ability cases: "**on the contrary, the studies reported in Table 1 show the opposite effect. Studies that oversampled highly gifted participants... did not find evidence for the threshold hypothesis.**"
      - On the absence of theory: "to discuss where a threshold should exactly be placed in the ability distribution is irrelevant if the 'why' is not clear." Their stated decision rule: "**Applying Occam's razor, no threshold should be assumed or postulated unless convincingly demonstrated otherwise.**"
      - The statistical-power version of the same argument, from the opposing camp: "When a study lacks sufficient statistical power to detect a real relationship between cognitive ability and outcome variables above this threshold, some researchers mistakenly interpret their inability to find a significant relationship... as evidence supporting the threshold hypothesis."
      - Four stated requirements for a valid threshold test: predictor measures that do not ceiling out above the threshold, outcomes with low base rates, a large sample, and a long interval between assessment and outcome.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A threshold can be manufactured three separate ways: by comparing a high-ability group whose variance is smaller, by choosing where to split a continuous variable, and by lacking the power to detect a real slope in a thin upper segment. When variances are equated the effect disappears and partly reverses. When the continuous variable is left continuous, nothing is found across two samples and three methods. And the defense that the nulls come from low-ability samples was tested directly and found backwards, since the studies that oversampled gifted participants are the ones finding no threshold. The burden therefore sits on the threshold claim, which is where the most thorough review puts it.
    - **Link to source:** [Sligh, Conners & Roskos-Ewoldsen (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2162-6057.2005.tb01254.x) | [Karwowski & Gralewski (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tsc.2012.05.003) | [Weiss et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence8040038)

- **Subcategory 2.5: The magnitude of the ability-performance relationship was revised sharply downward, and the revision is about over-correcting for exactly the restriction a selective program creates**
  - **Source:** Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022); Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The headline revision: general mental ability's operational validity for predicting job performance was revised from the long-standing estimate of **.51 down to .31**.
      - On the cause: "we conclude that each has significant issues that often result in substantial overcorrection and that therefore the validity of many selection procedures for predicting job performance has been **substantially overestimated**... mean validity estimates reduced by .10–.20 points."
      - A consequence for ranking: "the validity for GMA tests is reduced from .52 to .31 and that GMA tests are **no longer the strongest predictor** of job performance, but instead lag behind structured interviews and biodata."
      - On contemporary data: "GCA measures produce useful correlations with measures of job performance in the modern era, with a mean observed correlation of **.16**, and a mean corrected correlation of **.22**."
      - The direction of the argument matters: this work finds the field systematically *over*-corrected for range restriction, which cuts against the common move of dismissing a small observed correlation inside a selected group by asserting it would be much larger unrestricted.
      - An older reconfirmation of linearity, on 174 studies with a mean sample of 210 each: "nonlinear relationships were not found at levels substantially greater than would be expected by chance."
      - Provenance and scope note: this literature concerns the **magnitude** of the relationship and the methodology for correcting it. No analysis in either paper tests whether the ability-performance slope differs above against below a high cutoff. The linearity study ran on job-incumbent samples that are themselves range restricted, so flattening confined to the extreme upper tail would likely be undetectable in that design. Do not read "validity is lower than we thought" as "validity flattens above a cutoff." They are different claims and only the first is supported here.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The single number most often used to justify ability-based selection was cut by roughly forty percent, and in contemporary data it lands lower still. That weakens any argument resting on how strongly ability predicts, and it weakens it symmetrically for every cutoff level. What it does not do is establish a threshold, because none of this work examines the shape of the relationship at the top. The relevant asymmetry runs the other way: the correction literature's finding is that the field over-corrected for restriction, so a small correlation observed inside an admitted cohort should not be inflated on the assumption that restriction is hiding a large one.
    - **Link to source:** [Sackett et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994) | [Sackett et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001159) | [Coward & Sackett (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.75.3.297)

### Category 3: What Crossing the Line Delivers, and to Whom

- **Subcategory 3.1: At a real gifted threshold the causal gain rises as admitted-student ability falls, and is indistinguishable from zero at conventional-threshold ability**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Setting: a large public district with three admission pathways into the same classroom. Pathway A required an IQ of 130 or above for non-disadvantaged students (N = 2,679); Pathway B required 116 for students eligible for free lunch or English-learner services (N = 4,472); a third pathway admitted high achievers purely on within-school achievement rank with no IQ bar (N ≈ 4,144).
      - At the IQ-130 threshold, despite a strong first stage of +26.7 percentage points in a gifted classroom and +49.4 points classified gifted, the effects were: reading −0.052 (SE 0.050), math −0.084 (0.064), writing −0.083 (0.081). Donut-hole and differenced specifications agreed.
      - Splitting the achievement-rank pathway by ability, treatment-on-treated effects rise monotonically as ability falls: rank 1–5 at 1.30 sigma baseline gained 0.11 (SE 0.15); rank 6–10 at 0.83 sigma gained 0.16 (0.11); rank 11–15 at 0.59 sigma gained 0.39 (0.15); rank 16–20 at 0.42 sigma gained 0.50 (0.22).
      - The authors identify the top band as the analogue of the IQ-threshold group: students ranked 1 to 5 "have much higher achievement (1.3σ's) — about the same as the Plan A analysis group."
      - Their conclusion, verbatim: "**The gains appear to be largest for students who are closest to the threshold and furthest away from the traditional thresholds that determine entry to gifted programs in other settings.**"
      - Their proposed mechanism: "we suspect that the variation reflects systematically larger treatment effects for students with lower initial levels of achievement, who have the most to gain," plus "it is very hard to raise the scores of students who are already performing very well — a **'flat of the curve' effect**."
      - They rule out a ceiling artifact, noting only 2 percent of the high-IQ students near the threshold hit the maximum score, and they rule out composition effects.
      - In the peer-reviewed half of the project the heterogeneity sharpens: minority high achievers gain 0.5 sigma persisting to sixth grade, while for white students including low-income white students "the resulting estimates are all small in magnitude and uniformly insignificant."
      - Provenance note: the nulls at the IQ threshold appear **only in the unpublished working paper**, which lists no published version. The high-achiever results were published in a peer-reviewed economics journal.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the only study that puts students on both sides of an actual gifted cutoff and follows what happens. The result runs backwards to the assumption the cutoff encodes. Students admitted at the ability level a conventional threshold selects for gained nothing measurable, while students well below that level gained about half a standard deviation, and the effect grew steadily as ability fell. The authors name the mechanism themselves as a flat-of-the-curve effect. The caveat is substantial and belongs attached to every citation of this: the top-end null is in a working paper that was never published, while the results for lower-ability students were.
    - **Link to source:** [Card & Giuliano (2014), NBER w20453](https://doi.org/10.3386/w20453) | [Card & Giuliano (2016), AER version](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20150484)

- **Subcategory 3.2: A second discontinuity design found nothing for marginal students and nothing for high-ability lottery winners except in one subject**
  - **Source:** Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Setting: a Southwestern district of more than 200,000 students with universal fifth-grade gifted evaluation. Regression-discontinuity samples of roughly 2,600 and 5,500; lottery sample 542.
      - Published conclusion: "We find that achievement for marginal students **neither improves nor worsens** from GT services in the short run. We also find that lottery winners only perform better in science. Using a bounding analysis we cannot rule out zero, though we do not find any significant negative effects."
      - Precision bounds: they can rule out gains above 0.06 SD in math, 0.09 in reading, 0.15 in language, 0.13 in social studies, and 0.23 in science, despite peer achievement rising 0.24 to 0.35 SD.
      - The lottery arm was not a marginal group. Participants "score 1.6 standard deviations above the mean" against 1.0 sigma for the average gifted student in the district. That higher-ability arm also found nothing but science, at +0.28 SD.
      - Provenance note: the precision bounds come from the earlier working paper rather than the published article.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** An independent design in a different district reached the same place from a different direction. Marginal students gained nothing detectable, and the higher-ability students who won a lottery into the program gained nothing either, except in science. Notably, peer achievement rose substantially, so the students did get the better classmates the program promises, and it did not convert into measured achievement. The bounds are tight enough that a large effect can be excluded, though not a small one.
    - **Link to source:** [Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.6.3.30)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Universal screening surfaces high-ability children the referral process missed, and the entire gain reverses when it stops**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Design: all second graders took a nonverbal ability test, with referral at 130 for non-disadvantaged students and 115 for disadvantaged ones, and **the IQ eligibility standards themselves unchanged**. N = 78,065.
      - Gifted classification rose from 5.1 to 6.6 percent for the higher-threshold pathway and from 1.4 to 4.3 percent for the lower one. Trend-adjusted overall effect +1.5 percentage points (SE 0.3); difference-in-differences against matched schools +1.6 points (SE 0.3).
      - Odds ratios: overall +45 percent; lower-threshold pathway +174 percent; higher-threshold pathway +11 percent; Black students +74 percent; Hispanic students +118 percent; White students +12 percent.
      - The finding that transfers to a selective program: "**A full 20% of the compliers have IQs of 130 or higher** (vs. 25% of the always takers)," and "many of the newly identified Plan A gifted students had IQ scores well above the minimum 130-point threshold. We thus conclude that **the traditional referral system also misses some high-ability nondisadvantaged students.**"
      - On reversal: "because of financial pressures caused by the Great Recession, the District cut funding for IQ testing in 2007 and suspended the screening program in 2010. **By 2011, the gifted share of third graders had returned to the level of 2004–2005.**"
      - Provenance note: widely quoted figures of 180, 130, and 80 percent come from an earlier working paper; the peer-reviewed version reports 174, 118, and 74.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Holding the cutoff exactly where it was and simply testing everyone raised identification by 45 percent. The part that matters for a program with a high bar is that a fifth of the newly found children cleared the strict threshold outright, so the referral process had been losing children who were qualified by the program's own standard, for reasons that had nothing to do with the standard. The gain was also entirely contingent on funding and vanished within about a year of the screening stopping, which says the mechanism was administrative rather than developmental.
    - **Link to source:** [Card & Giuliano (2016), PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Both null findings were measured with an instrument that cannot detect acceleration**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2014); Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In the first study the outcomes are grade-level state assessments, and the gifted classrooms "follow the same curriculum as other classes."
      - The authors' own mechanism for the top-end null is a flat-of-the-curve effect: "it is very hard to raise the scores of students who are already performing very well."
      - They do address one version of this objection directly, reporting that only 2 percent of the high-IQ students near the threshold hit the maximum score, which rules out a hard ceiling on the test.
      - In the second study the treatment is "GT services" within a conventional district program, with outcomes on state tests in math, reading, language, social studies, and science.
      - Neither design measures grade-level advancement, course completion above grade, or performance on above-level material.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The two studies that find no benefit for high-ability students both measure benefit as a score on a test written for the student's enrolled grade, in classrooms teaching the enrolled grade's curriculum. That is close to the least sensitive available instrument for detecting whether a program moved a student through material faster. The authors' own explanation, that it is hard to raise the scores of students already scoring well, is a statement about the measure as much as about the students. A hard ceiling is ruled out, so the objection is about compression near the top rather than about saturation, and the nulls should be read as bounded rather than as evidence that acceleration does nothing.
    - **Link to source:** [Card & Giuliano (2014)](https://doi.org/10.3386/w20453) | [Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.6.3.30)

### Category 4: What a Fixed Threshold Costs Before Anyone Exercises Judgment

- **Subcategory 4.1: A single universally administered test misses a fifth of qualifying children at a high cut, and the level itself is worth about three points**
  - **Source:** McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014), as tabulated in McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Method: an analytic psychometric model using numerical integration of a multivariate normal, not a student sample.
      - Sensitivity of a single-assessment system with no gate, by cutoff and test reliability:

        | Cutoff | Reliability .95 | .90 | .85 | .80 | .70 |
        | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
        | 90th percentile | .843 | .776 | .725 | .680 | .604 |
        | 95th percentile | **.815** | .738 | .679 | .628 | .541 |
        | 99th percentile | **.763** | .665 | .592 | .530 | .428 |
      - So at a 95th-percentile cut with an excellent instrument, 18.5 percent of children who genuinely belong above the line are missed. At a 99th-percentile cut with a reliability of .90, a third are missed.
      - Moving the cut from the 90th to the 95th percentile at reliability .95 changes sensitivity by .028, from .843 to .815.
      - Provenance note: the primary article is closed access with no repository copy. Table 1 above is reproduced with attribution in the 2016 paper, which was read in full. The specific conjunctive-against-compensatory sensitivity figures from the 2014 paper are secondary and were not verified against the original.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Before any gate, referral, rule, or human judgment enters, a fixed cut applied to a single excellent test already loses roughly a fifth of qualifying children at the 95th percentile and closer to a quarter at the 99th. That is the floor cost of expressing the decision as a threshold, and it worsens as the cut rises. The comparison worth holding onto is the size of it: shifting the cut by five percentile points changes sensitivity by under three points, which is small next to everything in the next two subcategories.
    - **Link to source:** [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016), full text](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2016/07/Gifted-Child-Quarterly-2016-McBee-0016986216656256.pdf) | [McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986213513794)

- **Subcategory 4.2: A gate placed before the test is the most expensive decision available, and it cannot be repaired downstream**
  - **Source:** McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - From the abstract: "in nearly all conditions, identification systems that require a nomination before testing result in a large proportion of gifted students being missed. **Under commonly implemented conditions, the nomination stage can cause the false negative rate to easily exceed 60%.**"
      - With a gate whose validity against the ability test is .48, applied at the 90th percentile, sensitivity falls to **.280** and the false negative rate is **.720**, against a no-gate baseline of .843. At the 80th percentile the figure is .441; at the 50th, .709.
      - The structural mechanism, verbatim: "the confirmatory test provides strong protection against incorrect identifications because false positives with respect to the nomination are unlikely to successfully qualify on the test. In contrast, **the confirmatory assessment can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives** because those students that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory test."
      - Stated as a general rule: "**screeners cannot improve the sensitivity of an assessment system; they can only reduce it** because of their position as an additional hurdle for gifted students to clear."
      - To lose no more than 2 percent of relative sensitivity with a gate of validity .60, the gate has to sit near the 30th percentile, meaning "70% of the students can be expected to pass the nomination stage."
      - Their recommendation: "Consider abolishing the two-stage system completely."
      - Their own carve-out, which is the argument a selective program would make: a high false-negative regime may be appropriate "when programs could be dangerous or harmful for those students who are not truly ready."
      - The same asymmetry stated from Bayes' theorem six decades earlier, in a two-stage screening analysis: cases lost at stage one "are, of course, not picked up by Test 2 either, because it is not applied to them." In that worked example a second stage that was *infallible* still left the system "running only 7.5% above the base rate," and a second stage merely 90 percent accurate dropped total accuracy to 94.5 percent, "only 4.5% above that yielded by a blind guessing of the modal class." The verdict: "except in 'life-or-death' matters, the successive-screenings argument merely tends to soften the blow of Bayes' Rule."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Putting any filter in front of the real test is a one-way loss. A good instrument downstream protects against admitting the wrong child and offers nothing at all against rejecting the right one, because the rejected child never reaches it. With a plausibly valid gate at a plausible position, sensitivity falls from .843 to .280, which is a fifty-six point swing against the three points a five-percentile change in the cut is worth. The authors' own recommendation is to abolish the two-stage structure, and the same conclusion falls out of Bayes' theorem in a 1955 analysis of medical screening. The one legitimate exception they name is a program where admitting an unready child causes real harm.
    - **Link to source:** [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256) | [Meehl & Rosen (1955)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048070)

- **Subcategory 4.3: Requiring two bars instead of one collapses identification, though how much of that is the rule and how much is pool size is contested**
  - **Source:** McBee & Makel (2019); Lakin (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Using the top 5% cutoff for giftedness, a correlation of r = .27 yields a giftedness rate of **9.3% under the 'or' rule and a rate of 0.65% under the 'and' rule.** A correlation of r = .67 yields a rate of 8.2% under the 'or' rule and 1.8% under the 'and' rule."
      - So at a nominal 5 percent cutoff, the identified share can range from about 0.65 percent to about 9.3 percent depending only on how two measures are combined, a factor of roughly fourteen.
      - The less correlated the two measures, the more punishing the conjunctive rule, since independent measures rarely place the same child above both lines.
      - The contested reading, from an analysis using an ability test's own standardization data rather than simulation: "these diversity differences were due mainly to **the identified pool size and not to the effects of the particular combination rule.**"
      - Provenance note: the secondary figures often cited from the 2014 paper (conjunctive .79, complementary .87, single measure .84 at the 90th percentile with reliability .95, with conjunctive falling to .45 at reliability .70) come from an unpublished dissertation characterizing that closed-access paper, and were not verified against the original.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The combination rule moves identification more than the cutoff level does. At a stated 5 percent bar, an "and" rule across two modestly correlated measures identifies well under 1 percent while an "or" rule identifies over 9 percent, so the published cutoff describes almost nothing about who actually gets in. The honest complication is that a real-data analysis attributes composition effects mainly to how many students end up identified rather than to the rule itself, which would make the rule choice a disguised decision about program size rather than a free gain in accuracy.
    - **Link to source:** [McBee & Makel (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007) | [Lakin (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986217752099)

- **Subcategory 4.4: Category membership at the tail does not survive a second good test, and the instrument's own co-author says so**
  - **Source:** Lohman (2005)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On the nature of the label: "we speak of learning disabled or gifted students as if these labels represented discrete categories rather than **arbitrary portions of continuously varying score distributions.**" And: "Even those who understand that the boundaries between 'gifted' and 'not gifted' are arbitrary often assume that category membership would remain constant if we had perfectly reliable measures. **This is not true.**"
      - The quantified instability: with two of the best individual intelligence tests correlating at about r = .84, "**only about half of the students who score in the top 3% on one test will also score in the top 3% on the second test.** If the interval between test administrations is longer than a few weeks, then even fewer would merit the label gifted on both tests."
      - At a stricter cut: "the proportion of students scoring in the top 1% of the distribution on one test who could also be in the top 1% of the distribution for the second test... ranges from only **13% when r = .50 to 76% when r = .975.**"
      - The general statement: "**there will be considerable disagreement in classification among different tests unless the correlation is very high and the cut score is very low. Neither of these conditions typically applies.**" Correlations among full-scale scores on individually administered ability tests "range from r = .68 to r = .85," and at a top-3-percent criterion "only about one-third to one-half of the students would be expected to score in the top 3% on both tests."
      - The distributional fact that makes this bite on the upper tail specifically: "**no matter where we set the cut score on the upper tail of the score distribution, many more students will be near that cut score than far above it.** ... When the scores of these students regress towards the mean on retest, and on average they always regress, many will fall below the cut, and the scores of an equally large group of students who previously failed to make the cut will now rise above it."
      - His recommendation on cut scores: "**Simple schemes that establish an arbitrary cut score on an IQ or achievement test are administratively convenient but identify only a fraction of those who will later attain excellence.** Further, such schemes necessarily disadvantage children who have had fewer opportunities to develop the abilities measured by the tests."
      - On what to use instead: "Make better use of local norms on both ability and achievement tests... examine local percentile ranks for particular domains such as mathematics or science rather than national percentile ranks for composite scores." And: "**aptitude can only be estimated when a student's performance on a task is compared with the performance of other students who have had similar learning opportunities.**"
      - Standards language on the same point: at a high cutoff "regression to the mean pulls true scores below the threshold, so a child scoring exactly at the line is more likely to be a false positive, while a truly gifted child is easily pushed just under it," and reliability "is lowest in young children, which is exactly when screening happens."
      - Provenance note: this monograph was federally funded, and its author co-authored a widely used commercial ability test, which is a publisher-side interest worth naming even though it cuts against his own product's typical use.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The most damaging evidence against a fixed tail cutoff comes from someone who helped build the instrument programs use to apply one. On two excellent tests correlating at .84, only about half of a top-3-percent group repeats, and at the top 1 percent agreement can fall to 13 percent. His explanation is the structural fact that on the upper tail there are always more candidates just above the line than far above it, so regression on retest swaps out a substantial group in both directions. His own summary is that arbitrary cut scores are administratively convenient and catch only a fraction of those who later excel, and his recommended replacement is opportunity-referenced local comparison rather than a national composite percentile.
    - **Link to source:** [Lohman (2005), NRC/GT RM05216](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505376.pdf)

- **Subcategory 4.5: Misclassification is proportional to how many applicants sit near the line, and the two error rates move in opposite directions as the cut tightens**
  - **Source:** Rudner (2001); Livingston & Lewis (1995); Wyse & Hao (2012); AERA/APA/NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The functional form makes the two inputs explicit: "P(θ̂ > θc | θi) is the probability of having an observed score above the cut score given a true score equal to θi, f(θi) is the expected number of people whose true score is θi... Thus P(θ̂ > θc | θi) f(θi) is the expected number of people whose true score is θi that will be classified as masters." Expected misclassification is therefore an integral of error spillover across the cut multiplied by the density of examinees at that score. The author names the two required inputs as "1) the standard error... and 2) the expected examinee ability distribution."
      - The empirical demonstration: one test (observed range 52 to 183, mean 119.2, SD 23.8, reliability .91) scored against seven hypothetical cut-points. Estimated percent correctly classified: .991 at the 1.0th percentile, .969 at the 4.8th, .951 at the 10.4th, .920 at the 25.6th, **.909 at the 49.7th**, .931 at the 75th, .959 at the 90th.
      - Crucially, in the same table the conditional standard error is essentially flat across that range: 7.29 at the 51st percentile, 7.46 at the 21.5th, 7.40 at the 4.8th. Accuracy swings from .909 to .991 while measurement precision barely moves.
      - Independent confirmation that the pool's own distribution is a driver: index values "appeared to be affected by a number of factors including **the distribution of examinees**, test length, **the placement of the cut-scores**, and the proficiency estimators applied."
      - The professional standards state that error alone is insufficient: "the standard error at or near the cut scores has important implications for the trustworthiness of these decisions. **However, the standard error cannot be translated into the expected percentage of consistent or accurate decisions without strong assumptions about the distributions of measurement errors and true scores.**"
      - The 1955 version of the same point: "The effect upon p1 and p2 of a contemplated movement of a critical score or band requires a knowledge of distribution form such as only a large sample can give."
      - The direction reversal that must travel with this. Computed on a standard normal pool with the standard error held at 0.30: at a median cut, 9.3 percent of the pool is misclassified and the false-negative rate among the truly qualified is 9.3 percent; at a 99th-percentile cut, only 0.72 percent of the pool is misclassified but the false-negative rate among the truly qualified rises to **21.4 percent**, because the base rate shrinks faster than the error count. This is corroborated by the sensitivity table in 4.1, where sensitivity falls as the cutoff rises at fixed reliability.
      - Holding the cut and the error constant and varying only the pool's spread, computed the same way: pool SD 0.25 gives a 27.9 percent misclassification rate; SD 1.00 gives 9.3 percent; SD 4.00 gives 2.4 percent. A pool concentrated at the line misclassifies roughly twelve times more people than a dispersed one with identical measurement error.
      - Provenance note, and it is important. The density explanation for the accuracy pattern in the seven-cut table is an inference across sources; neither of those papers states it. The numerical figures in the last two bullets are computations performed against published equations rather than published findings, and should be labelled as such. One of the cited sources also contains internal inconsistencies between its narrative text and its own tables, so only its cross-tabulations and accuracy totals were used.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** How many people a cutoff misclassifies depends on two things, and only one of them is the test. The other is how many applicants sit near the line. The clean demonstration is a single test scored at seven different cut-points where accuracy varies from 90.9 to 99.1 percent while the conditional error stays flat, which rules out precision as the explanation. Combined with the tail fact from the previous subcategory, that there are always more candidates just above a high cut than far above it, this says a program whose applicants cluster against its published bar is operating in the worst region of its own decision function. Two disciplines apply. The direction reverses depending on which error is counted, since a stringent cut misclassifies a smaller share of the pool while missing a larger share of the qualified. And the sharpest numbers here are computed rather than observed.
    - **Link to source:** [Rudner (2001)](https://doi.org/10.7275/an9m-2035) | [Livingston & Lewis (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x) | [Wyse & Hao (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621612451522) | [AERA/APA/NCME (2014) Standards](https://testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)

### Category 5: What Decision Theory Says the Threshold Should Be

- **Subcategory 5.1: Stringency raises a valid predictor's value at an accelerating rate, which is the strongest formal argument for a high cut**
  - **Source:** Taylor & Russell (1939); Brogden (1949) via Cronbach & Gleser (1965)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The model takes three inputs and returns one output: the base rate of success, the selection ratio, and the predictor's validity, returning the proportion successful among those selected. That output is what modern language calls positive predictive value.
      - The authors' statement of direction: "the usefulness of the test with that validity increases as one is in a position to accept a smaller and smaller proportion of the candidates."
      - Their statement of *increasing* rather than diminishing returns: "in the case of correlations below about .70 these curves are positively accelerated toward the left. That is, **the usefulness of a test with a validity less than .70 increases more and more rapidly as the selection ratio becomes smaller.**"
      - Their striking comparison: "a validity coefficient of .50 is more useful when one is selecting only 20% of the applicant group than a correlation of unity would be if one were selecting 65% of the applicants."
      - With validity .50 and a 5 percent selection ratio, the success rate among those selected by base rate: .05 base rate gives .24; .10 gives .39; .20 gives .59; .30 gives .72; .40 gives .81; .50 gives .88; .80 gives .98.
      - The continuous-criterion version gives expected gain per selectee as validity times criterion standard deviation times the mean standard predictor score of those selected, where that last term equals the normal ordinate at the cut divided by the selection ratio. Utility is therefore linear in validity and nonlinear in stringency.
      - Computed values of that term: at a 50 percent selection ratio 0.798; at 30 percent 1.159; at 20 percent 1.400; at 10 percent 1.755; at 5 percent 2.063; at 1 percent 2.665. The marginal gain per unit of selection-ratio reduction rises monotonically from 1.81 to 15.06.
      - Assumptions, stated or structural: bivariate normality of predictor and criterion, linearity, homoscedasticity, a dichotomized criterion, top-down selection with no declined offers, and, in the authors' words, that "the applicant group and the present employee group are similarly constituted."
      - Provenance note: the table values above were independently recomputed from the bivariate normal definition and matched the published entries. The continuous-criterion figures are computations against a published formula rather than published findings. The utility model also requires knowing the criterion standard deviation, and plausible estimation methods for it can differ by a factor of two or more.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The formal case for selecting hard is real and it is stronger than intuition suggests. A predictor of fixed quality becomes more useful the fewer people you take, and the gain accelerates rather than flattening, to the point where a moderately valid test used at a 20 percent selection ratio beats a perfect test used at 65 percent. Anyone arguing for a lower cut has to answer this. The assumptions to watch are that the applicant pool resembles the group the validity was estimated on, and that the criterion is genuinely dichotomous, since neither holds cleanly in an admissions setting where the pool self-selects.
    - **Link to source:** [Taylor & Russell (1939)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0057079) | [Brogden (1949)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-6570.1949.tb01397.x)

- **Subcategory 5.2: A low base rate can make a valid test's positive calls wrong more often than right**
  - **Source:** Meehl & Rosen (1955)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The standard: "a psychometric device, to be efficient, must make possible a greater number of correct decisions than could be made in terms of the base rates alone."
      - Worked example. A screen detecting 55 percent of poor adjusters at a 19 percent false positive rate, base rate 5 percent, per 10,000 people: 275 true positives against 1,805 false positives. "Of these 2080, only 275, or 13%, would actually make a poor adjustment... **use of the test has yielded a drop from 95% to 79.7% in the total number of correct decisions.**"
      - A second worked example with 75 percent accuracy both ways and a 1-in-10 base rate: "**whenever we say 'tumor' on the basis of the test, the chances are 3 to 1 that we are mistaken.**"
      - A third, with perfect specificity: a pattern appearing in 8.1 percent of cases and 0.0 percent of normals, against a base rate of 0.85 percent, yields 7 true positives, 0 false positives, and 78 false negatives per 10,000. "In order to detect 7 schizophrenics, it would be necessary to test 10,000 individuals."
      - Their formal rules. A positive assertion is more likely true than false only when the odds of the condition exceed the ratio of false positives to valid positives. Shifting a cut improves decisions only when the gain in valid positives beats the worsening in false positives by more than the negatives-to-positives ratio, so a change delivering "20% more schizophrenics detected at only 5% more false positives" makes decisions worse at a base rate of .10 because 4 is not greater than 9. And "the optimal cut lies at the intersection of the two distribution envelopes."
      - Their design recommendation: "test development should (other things being equal) be concentrated on those characteristics having base rates nearer .50 rather than close to .00 or 1.00," and "When P is small, it may be advisable to explore the possibility of dealing with a **restricted population within which the base rate of the attribute being tested is higher.**"
      - On who bears the cost: "The incidence of false negatives, of potential employees screened out by the test who would actually have made good on the job if hired, is of little concern to management except as it costs money to give tests... When we make a clinical decision to treat or not to treat, we are withholding something from people who have a claim upon us in a sense that is much stronger than the 'right to work' gives a job applicant."
      - No normality assumption is needed for the arithmetic; the values are treated as exact population parameters, and normality enters only when discussing where to move a cut on continuous distributions.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Rarity is the enemy of a threshold. When the trait being selected for is uncommon, even an accurate instrument produces positive calls that are wrong more often than right, and a test can reduce the total number of correct decisions relative to simply predicting the majority outcome. Their own prescription is the one selective programs already follow without saying so: work inside a restricted population where the base rate is higher. They also draw the distinction that most selection theory skips, between an employer with no duty to those it declines and an institution that does have one.
    - **Link to source:** [Meehl & Rosen (1955)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048070)

- **Subcategory 5.3: Under unequal error costs the optimal threshold is the false-positive share of total error cost, and it requires a calibrated probability rather than a score**
  - **Source:** Metz (1978); Babii, Chen, Ghysels & Kumar (2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Average cost is minimized where the slope of the receiver operating characteristic equals the prevalence odds times the ratio of net false-positive cost to net false-negative cost.
      - The author's plain-language reading: "the optimal operating point is on the lower left portion of the curve, for example, if actually positive cases are rare or if the cost of the consequences of a false positive decision is relatively great." So rarity and false-positive cost push the threshold in the same direction, toward stringency.
      - The equivalent posterior form: predict positive when the posterior probability exceeds the false-positive share of total error cost. If false negatives cost three times as much as false positives, the threshold is one quarter. If costs are symmetric, it is one half.
      - A published econometric statement of the same rule: "The threshold corresponds to the fraction of net losses from false positives in the total net losses, hence the decision is made whenever the choice probability exceeds the fraction of false positive losses."
      - The binding requirement: the rule has optimality properties only for a **calibrated** posterior probability. An uncalibrated score thresholded at the same value has none.
      - The distributional assumption does real work. Work examining the same problem under skew-normal rather than normal distributions finds the optimal cutoff changes materially.
      - Provenance note: the formula was verified in a derivation document matching the journal article's abstract, and corroborated independently in two statistical software references, but the journal article itself was not read and no page citation is available. The skew-normal result is from an unreviewed 2026 preprint and is not independently corroborated.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is a formal answer to "which percentile," and it is not a percentile. The cost-optimal threshold is set by how rare the trait is and how much worse one error is than the other, and it reduces to a simple statement: admit anyone whose probability of success exceeds the false-positive share of total error cost. Two things follow. Rarity and false-positive aversion both argue for stringency, so a selective program's instinct is formally defensible. And the rule only works on a calibrated probability, which converts "where should the line be" into an empirical question about the instrument rather than a policy preference.
    - **Link to source:** [Metz (1978)](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0001-2998(78)80014-2) | [Babii, Chen, Ghysels & Kumar (2026), Quantitative Economics 17, 623–669](https://doi.org/10.3982/QE2385)

- **Subcategory 5.4: A fixed number of seats removes the error-weighting question for ranking, and sequential rules save less than they appear to**
  - **Source:** Meehl & Rosen (1955); Hunter, Schmidt & Le (2006); Wald sequential analysis
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On capacity: "an externally imposed selection ratio **eliminates the often troublesome value questions** about the relative seriousness of the two kinds of errors, since they are unavoidably increased or decreased at exactly the same rate."
      - The parallel point from gifted education: the right asymmetry depends on "the program to be provided and its potential for negative outcomes," with an unsuccessful attempt at after-school enrichment carrying minimal consequences while a failed grade-skip means "a logistically and socially fraught process of reintegration." Therefore "**there is no globally correct decision with regard to incorrect identifications and false negatives.**"
      - On sequential testing's theoretical efficiency: among sequential tests with error probabilities no worse than a given one, the sequential probability ratio test minimizes average sample size, and "the average savings in sampling relative to the Neyman-Pearson test with the same error probabilities is 50% or more in many cases."
      - The essential caveat: optimality holds only at the two hypothesized points, and "**the expected sample size may even exceed that of the corresponding fixed sample size test at some values of θ between**" them. Those intermediate values are exactly the borderline candidates a selective program cares most about.
      - On sequential selection in practice: "Sequential selection models are often used by employers to save testing time and testing costs, although **the savings are almost always less than the loss in selection utility.**"
      - On retest opportunities as a sequential rule, computed against published equations: with three independent attempts, a true qualifier's probability of failing all three falls to roughly 0.06 percent, while a true non-qualifier's probability of passing at least once rises to roughly 40 percent. Multiple attempts nearly eliminate false negatives and roughly two and a half times the false positives.
      - Provenance note: the three-attempt figures are computations against published formulas, and the source's own printed figure for one of them could not be reproduced. Modern sequential-efficiency bounds of 36 to 75 percent come from an unreviewed 2026 preprint, though the general 50-percent claim is corroborated by a peer-curated reference work.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Whether the false-positive against false-negative debate matters at all depends on a fact about the program rather than about the test. With a fixed number of seats every admission displaces exactly one rejection, so the weighting question disappears for ranking purposes and reappears the moment class size becomes a decision. Sequential testing is genuinely efficient in theory and disappointing in practice: its guarantees hold at the extremes rather than at the borderline, and in selection settings the testing time saved is usually worth less than the selection quality given up. Allowing retakes is the cheapest way to cut false negatives, and it multiplies false positives.
    - **Link to source:** [Meehl & Rosen (1955)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048070) | [Hunter, Schmidt & Le (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.3.594) | [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256)

- **Subcategory 5.5: What can be measured inside an admitted cohort systematically understates the truth, and the standard correction over-corrects**
  - **Source:** Thorndike (1949) case formulas; Sackett & Yang (2000); Hunter, Schmidt & Le (2006); AERA/APA/NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Selecting on a variable, or on anything correlated with it, truncates its variance in the selected group and mechanically shrinks the observed predictor-criterion correlation within that group. Direct restriction on the predictor is one case; indirect restriction through a correlated third variable is another and requires more information to correct.
      - Magnitude of the difference between the two corrections: "Range restriction in most data sets is **indirect**, but the meta-analysis methods used to date have applied the correction for **direct** range restriction... the authors estimate that previous meta-analyses have underestimated the correlation between general mental ability and job performance by about 25%."
      - A concrete instance: for medium-complexity jobs, "**The correct model estimates this correlation to be .66, whereas [the earlier work] estimated it as .49, a 26% underestimate.**"
      - Observed restriction also understates actual restriction: a mean true-score restriction ratio of .56 against an observed value of .67 means "taking [the observed value] as a measure of range restriction leads to a consistent understatement of the extent of actual range restriction."
      - Corrections are biased downward under assumption violation. In simulation the improved procedure underestimated by up to 44.5 percent where the direct-restriction procedure underestimated by 63.8 percent, so corrected validities are floors rather than point estimates.
      - Order of operations matters: correct for criterion unreliability first, then range restriction, then predictor unreliability.
      - An expanded typology identifies eleven distinct restriction scenarios, and a peer-reviewed review of it notes that the authors "also noted [that] **some situations have no solutions.**"
      - On reporting: the standards require that "**Where cut scores are specified for selection or classification, the standard errors of measurement should be reported in the vicinity of each cut score**," and note that precision "might be depicted by error bands or likely score ranges."
      - Explicit gap: no study was located demonstrating that reporting a band rather than a point score improves classification outcomes. The normative guidance exists; the empirical demonstration does not.
      - Provenance note: the case formulas were verified through consistent secondary sources rather than the 1949 original. Published confidence intervals on normed scores reflect only unreliability and omit norming-sample error, so they are narrower than they should be.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Any program trying to learn about its own cutoff from its own admitted students is looking at a systematically shrunken picture, because selection removed the variance the correlation needs. The correction for this is well developed and has one large trap: the standard formula assumes selection happened on the predictor itself, when in real settings it usually happened on something correlated with it, and applying the wrong one understates the true relationship by roughly a quarter. Corrections are also floors rather than estimates, and for some real selection designs no correction exists at all. Separately, the standards already require reporting error at the cut and permit bands, but no study was found showing that bands actually improve decisions, so that argument currently rests on principle rather than evidence.
    - **Link to source:** [Hunter, Schmidt & Le (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.3.594) | [Sackett & Yang (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.85.1.112) | [AERA/APA/NCME (2014) Standards](https://testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)

### Category 6: The Alternatives on the Record, and What Each Actually Delivers

- **Subcategory 6.1: Local norms reallocate a fixed-size pool rather than expanding it, and universal screening expands it but reverses when funding stops**
  - **Source:** Peters, Rambo-Hernandez, Makel, Matthews & Plucker (2019); Card & Giuliano (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sample for the local-norms analysis: fall third-grade reading and mathematics across ten states and ten cohorts, N = 3,163,381 and 3,305,051 across roughly 3,400 districts. Registered report with a public preregistration.
      - Representation shifts at a top-5-percent cutoff in reading, from national to building norms: African American index .22 to .76, described by the authors as "238% more represented"; Latinx .26 to .68; Asian American 2.30 down to 1.37; European American 1.29 down to 1.12.
      - The size fact the abstract does not lead with: reading identification moves from 91,622 (2.90 percent) under national norms to 96,556 (3.05 percent) under building norms, and in mathematics it **shrinks**, from 112,401 to 111,651. Only a disjunctive rule combining national and building norms expands the pool meaningfully, to 137,095 or 4.33 percent.
      - Author-stated limits: "Implementing local norms is not a panacea"; local norms require universal testing because "districts will find it impossible to take the top 5% or 15% of any group if <100% of the group is tested"; and in districts without residential segregation "using any level of norms will likely produce a similar pool."
      - Transfer limit that matters for a program without a catchment: the entire logic is within-building, so a nationally recruiting program has no unit to norm within.
      - Universal screening figures are in 3.3 above. The reversal fact bears repeating here as an alternative's durability property: funding was cut and "By 2011, the gifted share of third graders had returned to the level of 2004–2005."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** These two reforms are usually offered together and do different things. Local norms move a pool of nearly constant size across schools, changing who is in it dramatically while barely changing how many, and in one subject the pool actually got smaller. They also require testing everyone, which means they are downstream of universal screening rather than an alternative to it, and they need a local unit that a nationally recruiting program does not have. Universal screening genuinely expands identification, and it expanded it at the existing cutoff rather than by lowering it, which is the version that transfers. Its durability is poor: the gain was administrative and it disappeared within a year of the money stopping.
    - **Link to source:** [Peters et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419848446) | [Card & Giuliano (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113)

- **Subcategory 6.2: Above-level testing is the only design built to discriminate inside the top few percent, and it is fifty years old**
  - **Source:** Swiatek (2007); Assouline & Lupkowski-Shoplik (2012); Stanley (2000)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The structure: stage one is a grade-level test at the 95th to 99th percentile, and stage two is a test written for older students.
      - The result that makes it work: "Despite the fact that all talent search participants have in-grade test scores in the top 3% to 5%... **the scores they earn on above-level tests are normally distributed, with scores covering most or all of the range of the instrument.**"
      - The magnitude of what a grade-level test conceals: half of the 7th graders scoring 500 to 800 on a college-entrance mathematics test "know more algebra, as measured by the standardized algebra test, before they study the subject in school than do half of the students after completing a school year of it."
      - The ceiling problem concretely: a 12-year-old scoring 760 took a 40-item Algebra I test "on which a score of 32 correct is excellent for anyone who has completed a school year of that subject," and "The boy made no errors."
      - The operating rule that follows: "Determine what the student does not know (diagnosis) and help him or her learn just that, without needless repetition," and "**Avoid trying to teach students what they already know.**"
      - The same author's caution about his own most dramatic demonstration, teaching a year of algebra in a day to 75 students with two thirds reaching criterion: "this 1-day marathon was sort of a stunt. We did not expect such hastily acquired knowledge to 'stick.'"
      - On delivery: "Modern computer technology and adaptive testing can make that strategy feasible."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** One design in this entire corpus was built specifically for the problem a highly selective program has, which is telling apart children who all clear a high grade-level bar. Its finding is that the same students who bunch into the top three to five percent on a grade-level test spread across the full range of a test written for older students. That is a direct answer to the density problem in 4.5: the bunching is a property of the instrument rather than of the children, and changing the instrument disperses them. The approach is decades old, its inventor called his most dramatic demonstration a stunt, and he named adaptive delivery as the thing that would make it practical.
    - **Link to source:** [Swiatek (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986207306318) | [Assouline & Lupkowski-Shoplik (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0734282911433946) | [Stanley (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-8971.6.1.216)

- **Subcategory 6.3: Replacing an observed-score rule with a posterior-probability rule improved sensitivity and precision at the same time in simulation**
  - **Source:** Yu (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The proposed rule replaces "observed score at or above the cutoff" with "the probability that the true score is at or above the cutoff, given the observed scores, exceeds a stated threshold."
      - In simulation at a threshold of .50, the posterior rule beat a conventional conjunctive rule on both metrics simultaneously: sensitivity 0.88 against 0.72, and precision 0.85 against 0.82.
      - Beating both at once is the notable property, since sensitivity and precision usually trade against each other when only the cut level is moved.
      - Provenance note: this is an unpublished doctoral dissertation and the results are simulation-only, with no application to real applicant data. It is the weakest-provenance source cited in this document and is included because nothing peer-reviewed occupies the same position.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The one concrete proposal in the literature for replacing a threshold with a probability produced a simultaneous improvement in both directions rather than a trade, which is what you would expect if the conventional rule is discarding information that the posterior retains. It is also a simulation in an unpublished dissertation, so it establishes that the idea is coherent rather than that it works. The absence of anything stronger in this position is itself the finding.
    - **Link to source:** [Yu (2017), UConn Doctoral Dissertations 1524](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/dissertations/1524/)

- **Subcategory 6.4: Nothing has been published on a threshold that updates as outcome data accumulates**
  - **Source:** van der Linden & Mellenbergh (1977); de Gruijter & Hambleton (1984); search result
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A decision-theoretic cut-score literature exists, treating the cut as the solution to a utility problem rather than as a percentile, and it explicitly contemplates sequential testing.
      - Its standard caution, from within the tradition: the approach "is not without its problems," first among them "inaccurate parameter estimates."
      - The binding parameter for an admissions program is the utility of admitting a marginal candidate, which is precisely the quantity nobody has measured.
      - Searches for empirical work on programs that revise an admission threshold as outcome data accumulates, or that set a threshold by expected-utility optimization against observed outcomes, returned nothing in the education literature. The only hits were threshold-tuning methods from unrelated machine-learning domains.
      - Provenance note: this is an absence rather than a finding, and an absence found by one search process. It should be treated as "not located" rather than "does not exist."
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The framework for setting a cut from expected utility has existed since the 1970s and names its own weak point, which is that it needs parameters nobody has. The parameter it needs most is the value of admitting the marginal candidate. No published work was found on any program that estimates that from its own outcomes and moves its threshold accordingly. That is the clearest open position in this document, and it is open because of missing data rather than missing method.
    - **Link to source:** [van der Linden & Mellenbergh (1977)](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662167700100414) | [de Gruijter & Hambleton (1984)](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662168400800101)

### Category 7: Sources Commonly Used to Justify or Attack a Cutoff That Do Not Transfer to the Tail

- **Subcategory 7.1: The founding percentage is an administrative figure read backwards, so citing it as a standard inverts its meaning**
  - **Source:** Marland (1972); McClain & Pfeiffer (2012)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The figure is stated as "a **minimum** of 3 to 5 percent," and its purpose is described in the later literature as "proposed as a minimum upper limit and not a specific threshold, thus preventing any superintendent from claiming that their district had no gifted students."
      - What happened next: "However, the 3–5% upper limit for defining gifted students **became, in the minds of many, including state education policymakers, something real.**"
      - The field's own assessment of its status: it "is nothing more than a useful but clearly inexact estimate for the number of intellectually precocious and highly capable students who might require or benefit from special gifted programs."
      - Widely circulated secondary versions carry errors of both name and substance, including one policy brief that renders the report's name incorrectly.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A program citing the conventional 3-to-5-percent figure as the basis for a strict cutoff is citing a floor as a ceiling, which reverses what the source says. The number was written to stop districts denying they had any gifted children, and the literature that traces it says explicitly that it hardened into a limit in policymakers' minds rather than in the document. It cannot support an argument for stringency in either direction, and anyone who invokes it against a program's cut is making the same mistake as anyone who invokes it for one.
    - **Link to source:** [Marland Report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED056243.pdf) | [McClain & Pfeiffer (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15377903.2012.643757)

- **Subcategory 7.2: The most influential argument against a high cut is a popular-book restatement of a hypothesis with no source, which its attributed author rejected**
  - **Source:** Gladwell (2008) as quoted in Robertson et al. (2010); Jauk et al. (2013); Weiss et al. (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The popular version, quoted verbatim in the peer-reviewed literature: "The relationship between success and IQ works only up to a point. Once someone has an IQ of somewhere around 120, having additional IQ points doesn't seem to translate into any measurable real-world advantage."
      - Two independent peer-reviewed papers state that no source asserts the figure of 120, and that "even in absence of any empirical evidence for an IQ-threshold at 120, this very specific assumption... has hardly ever been questioned."
      - The author it is credited to argued the opposite: he "intended for a necessary but not sufficient condition" and "**opposed the idea of a threshold based on empirical findings (1973).**"
      - Where a threshold is found in modern work it applies to divergent-thinking performance rather than to accomplishment, and for accomplishment no breakpoint was detected at all.
      - The claim it is used to make about accomplishment is contradicted by data collected specifically to test it, with odds ratios from 2.7 to 18.2 within the top 1 percent.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The single most widely believed claim relevant to a high admissions cutoff, that ability stops mattering above roughly IQ 120, has no primary source, was rejected by the researcher it is attributed to, applies only to a different outcome than the one people cite it for, and is contradicted by the one research program designed to test it. Anyone arguing that GT's bar is set uselessly high on this basis is arguing from a popular book. This does not make a high cut correct; it removes the most common objection to it.
    - **Link to source:** [Robertson et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721410391442) | [Jauk et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2013.03.003) | [Weiss et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence8040038)

- **Subcategory 7.3: The 15-to-20-percent talent pool was validated on enrichment products under a revolving-door mechanism, not on full-time accelerated academics**
  - **Source:** Renzulli & Reis, Schoolwide Enrichment Model materials
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The recommendation: "a talent pool of 15-20% of above average ability/high potential students is identified through a variety of measures," with "alternative pathways of entrance (self-nomination, parent nomination, etc.)."
      - The empirical basis, which is real and unusual in this literature: "when a broader population of students (15-20% of the general population called the 'talent pool') were able to participate in Types I and II enrichment experiences, **they produced equally good Type III products as the traditional 'gifted' students (the top 3-5%).**"
      - The underlying study: 1,162 students in grades 1 to 6 across 11 districts, with talent pools of 15 to 25 percent, comparing students above and below a roughly top-5-percent test cut.
      - A second stated rationale is political rather than psychometric: "By extending enrichment activities to more students, the approach minimizes concerns about elitism and negative attitudes toward gifted programs."
      - The model's own framing distinguishes "gifted behaviors" from "gifted individuals," so the figure is a pool for access with a revolving door rather than a claim about how many children are gifted.
      - Conflict of interest: the model's originator evaluated his own program, and the outcome measure is a product type his own model defines.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The main published argument for a much wider pool is grounded in a real comparison, which is more than the strict-cutoff conventions can say, and the comparison is on the wrong outcome for a full-time accelerated academic program. The outcome was self-directed enrichment products in a pull-out model with a revolving door, judged against a framework the program's own author defined, by that author. The finding that a broader pool produced comparable products does not establish that a broader pool can sustain two grade levels a year of core academics, and the model does not claim it does. The political rationale is also stated openly, which is worth knowing when the figure is cited as a psychometric one.
    - **Link to source:** [Schoolwide Enrichment Model executive summary](https://gifted.uconn.edu/schoolwide-enrichment-model/semexec/) | [SEM triad research summary](https://gifted.uconn.edu/schoolwide-enrichment-model/semtriad/)

- **Subcategory 7.4: The discontinuity nulls come from a population and an outcome that differ from an accelerated program in both directions**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2014); Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The treatment in the first study is placement into a classroom that "follow[s] the same curriculum as other classes," and the outcome is a grade-level state assessment.
      - The gain that was found concentrated in students at 0.42 to 0.59 standard deviations above the mean, which is roughly the 66th to 72nd percentile, well below a 95th-percentile bar.
      - The null at the top was measured on students at 1.30 sigma, roughly the 90th percentile, and on an IQ-130 threshold group.
      - The second study's marginal students are at the threshold of a district program, and even its higher-ability lottery arm at 1.6 sigma is at roughly the 95th percentile.
      - Neither study measures grade-level advancement, above-grade course completion, or performance on above-level material.
      - The first study's top-end nulls appear only in an unpublished working paper with no published version listed.
      - Provenance note: these limitations do not make the findings wrong. They bound what the findings can be used to argue.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** These are the strongest studies available and they are the strongest studies available about something else. The programs are conventional pull-out or tracked classes teaching grade-level content, the outcome is a grade-level test, and the students who gained sit around the 70th percentile. A program admitting above the 95th percentile and promising multiple grade levels per year shares neither the population nor the treatment nor the outcome measure. Using them to argue that a high cutoff is pointless overstates them, and the top-end result specifically was never peer-reviewed. Using them to argue that the marginal student gains most is closer to what they show, and that claim is about a much lower margin than GT's.
    - **Link to source:** [Card & Giuliano (2014)](https://doi.org/10.3386/w20453) | [Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.6.3.30)

- **Subcategory 7.5: The best-known evidence that high achievers do not become exceptional adults is about class rank rather than ability, and its most-quoted numbers are not the author's**
  - **Source:** Arnold, Illinois Valedictorian Project
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sample: 81 high school valedictorians from one state's class of 1981, selected on class rank and grade point average rather than on tested ability.
      - The ability range, which is the decisive detail: "**mean ACT was 28 (95th percentile), with a range from 18 to 33.**" An ACT of 18 is near the national median.
      - Sampling: drawn from schools that publicly recognized valedictorians, from a list built by writing to all principals, of whom half responded, further constrained because a researcher had to attend each commencement. The report states plainly that "The nonprobability sample, therefore, does not represent" the population.
      - Verified outcomes: mean undergraduate grade point average 3.6; at least a dozen graduated Phi Beta Kappa; more than half of those completing graduate study were employed in science, mathematics, engineering, or business. "**About a quarter of the valedictorians are now at top professional levels for their age.**"
      - The widely circulated figures of 90 percent professionals, 40 percent in the highest tier, and no visionaries in the group trace to a popular business book via a magazine article, **not to the researcher's own writing**, and the 40 percent figure contradicts her own report's "about a quarter."
      - A related caution from the eminence literature: an elite high-IQ program's graduates had "not made unique contributions to society beyond what might be expected from their family SES and the high-quality education they received." No numeric base rate for eminence following high-ability identification was located in that source or in the surrounding literature.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The study most often used to argue that selecting top performers does not produce exceptional adults selected on class rank, not on ability, and its members' tested ability ran from the national median up to the 95th percentile. It is a non-probability sample and says so. Its findings cannot speak to what happens above a 95th-percentile ability bar, because most of its sample is below one. Separately, the numbers most people have actually heard from it are not in it, and the most striking one contradicts what the report says.
    - **Link to source:** [Illinois Valedictorian Project, ERIC ED368304](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED368304.pdf) | [Subotnik, Olszewski-Kubilius & Worrell (2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26168418/)

- **Subcategory 7.6: The strongest evidence for a high cut also does not reach the band a 95th-percentile program operates in, and this cuts against the position it is used to support**
  - **Source:** Robertson et al. (2010); Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2005); Kell, Lubinski & Benbow (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The qualifying threshold for these samples was the top 1 percent by age 13, and the bottom comparison quartile's mean scores run 441 to 495 against qualifying cutoffs of 390 and 500. The bottom of the comparison sits at the top-1-percent boundary.
      - So the demonstrated slope runs from roughly the 99th percentile upward. The band between the 95th and 99th percentiles is not represented on either side of the comparison.
      - Two related studies run on the top 0.01 percent rather than the top 1 percent and are descriptive base-rate studies. One of them does not contain a within-sample slope test at all and should not be cited for the slope claim.
      - The samples average about one standard deviation above the normative mean in socioeconomic status, and the predictor ceilings out, with top-quartile doctorate holders averaging 702 of a possible 800.
      - Female-only analyses frequently failed to reach significance on lower-base-rate outcomes.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Honesty requires applying the same standard to the evidence that supports a high cut. The finding that ability keeps mattering was established by comparing the top of the top 1 percent against the bottom of the top 1 percent, so it demonstrates a slope above the 99th percentile and says nothing directly about the 95th to 99th band where a program with a 95th-percentile bar actually admits most of its students. The predictor also hits its ceiling, and the samples are socioeconomically advantaged. The claim that survives is that no threshold has been found anywhere anyone has looked, which is weaker than the claim that a higher cut buys a measured amount.
    - **Link to source:** [Robertson et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721410391442) | [Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.97.3.484) | [Kell, Lubinski & Benbow (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612457784)
