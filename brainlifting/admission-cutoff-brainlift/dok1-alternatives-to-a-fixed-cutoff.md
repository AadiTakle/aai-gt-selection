# DOK 1 — Alternatives to a Single Fixed National-Percentile Cutoff

Fact extraction only. Every number below was read out of the source named beside it. Exact quotes are in
quotation marks. Anything I could not verify is flagged inline and listed again in the final section.

Every DOI in this document was checked against the Crossref registry (or DataCite for OSF items) and
returned a matching record. Sage, AEA and PNAS return HTTP 403 to command-line requests; that is
bot-blocking, not a dead link, and the content of those pages was retrieved successfully by other means.

Applicability note that recurs throughout: GT School admits above the 95th percentile on the CogAT, its
applicants are self-selected and nationally distributed, and its outcome of interest is far-above-grade-level
work. Almost every study below is set in a public district, uses grade-level state achievement tests as the
outcome, and studies students at or below the top few percent of a *local* distribution. Where that gap
matters I say so.

## 1. Local norms versus national norms

### 1.1 Peters, Rambo-Hernandez, Makel, Matthews & Plucker (2019)

**Source.** Peters, S. J., Rambo-Hernandez, K., Makel, M. C., Matthews, M. S., & Plucker, J. A. (2019).
"Effect of Local Norms on Racial and Ethnic Representation in Gifted Education." *AERA Open*, 5(2), 1–18.
DOI [10.1177/2332858419848446](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419848446). Free full text:
[ERIC EJ1220745](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1220745.pdf). Preregistration:
[https://osf.io/z2egy/](https://osf.io/z2egy/) (this was a registered report).

**Sample and setting.** NWEA Measures of Academic Progress (MAP), fall third-grade reading and mathematics.
Ten states chosen because they had the largest percentages of their student populations taking MAP:
California, Colorado, Illinois, Kentucky, Minnesota, Michigan, Ohio, South Carolina, Washington, Wisconsin.
Ten cohorts, 2007–2008 through 2016–2017. N = 3,163,381 students in the reading file and 3,305,051 in the
mathematics file; 3,424 districts (reading) and 3,469 (math). All school types included (public, private,
charter) if the school averaged more than five third graders tested. MAP marginal reliabilities reported as
.93 to .95; concurrent validity with state tests "around r = .80."

**What "building norms" means here.** "In this article, we use 'local norms' to refer to ranked performance
within the school building... If the cut score is the top 5% of each building, then each building will always
identify 5% of its students as gifted."

**Headline representation changes, top-5% cutoff.** "RI" is the representation index: share of the identified
group that is X, divided by X's share of the whole population. 1.0 is proportional.

| Group, reading, top 5% | National norms | Building norms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| African American | n = 2,842, RI 0.22 | n = 10,138, RI 0.76 |
| Latinx | n = 3,094, RI 0.26 | n = 8,374, RI 0.68 |
| Asian American | n = 8,082, RI 2.30 | n = 5,062, RI 1.37 |
| European American | n = 63,708, RI 1.29 | n = 58,200, RI 1.12 |

Mathematics, top 5%: African American n = 2,288 (RI 0.15) under national norms to n = 9,097 (RI 0.60) under
building norms; Latinx n = 3,555 (RI 0.24) to n = 9,551 (RI 0.64).

The authors' own framing of the reading result: "in reading, under building norms for the 5% cut score,
African Americans were 238% more represented under building norms than they were under national norms (.76
vs. .22). They remained disproportionately underrepresented under both, but the increase from .22 to .76 far
exceeds our a priori criterion for a meaningful improvement."

State norms did essentially nothing: "the use of state norms would have little to no effect on the size of
each group identified."

**The number most relevant to a selective program: the total pool barely moves.** Reading, top 5%: 91,622
students identified under national norms (2.90% of the sample) versus 96,556 under building norms (3.05%).
Mathematics, top 5%: 112,401 (3.40%) versus 111,651 (3.38%). Local norms *reallocate* a fixed-size pool
across schools; they do not enlarge it. The enlargement only appears under the "or" rule combining national
and building norms: 137,095 identified in reading at the 5% cutoff (4.33%), which the authors describe as "a
40% increase over building norms."

**Authors' stated limitations.**
- "Implementing local norms is not a panacea for addressing all systemic causes of underrepresentation in
  gifted education."
- "districts will find it impossible to take the top 5% or 15% of any group if <100% of the group is tested."
  Local norms presuppose universal testing.
- "in districts with little residential segregation (de facto or otherwise) and similar demographics across
  their schools, using any level of norms will likely produce a similar pool of identified students."
- "A move to building norms in schools with a typical proportion of minority students (about 30%) does not
  have as large an effect as it would in a building with a larger proportion of minority students... policy
  makers should not presume that building norms will have such effects in every school."
- "if a district does not expand programming and holds the number of identified students constant, moving
  from national to local norms will result in some previously identified students losing services, and a
  negative parent and student reaction should be expected."
- On the "or" compromise: "we imagine that a ≥40% increase in the population eligible to receive gifted
  services would be significant for any school district in terms of additional resources."

**Applicability flag.** This is a simulation over achievement-test data, not observed gifted placements. More
importantly, the entire logic is *within-building*: it identifies students underserved relative to the peers
in the room they are actually sitting in. A national private program has no building to norm within. The
transferable idea is the reference-group choice itself (compare an applicant against a defined pool rather
than a national standardization sample), not the specific numbers.

### 1.2 Supporting sources on local norms — abstract-level only

- Peters, S. J., & Engerrand, K. G. (2016). "Equity and Excellence: Proactive Efforts in the Identification of
  Underrepresented Students for Gifted and Talented Services." *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 60(3), 159–171. DOI
  [10.1177/0016986216643165](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216643165). **Flag: I verified the citation and
  abstract only; the full text is paywalled and I could not read it.** The abstract states the article
  "provides an overview of past efforts to mitigate inequity in both K-12 and higher education program

  any to it.

  Identification: Everything You Need to Know." *Gifted Child Today*, 44(2), 93–104. DOI
  [10.1177/1076217520985181](https://doi.org/10.1177/1076217520985181). A practitioner tutorial, not new

## 2. Universal screening

### 2.1 Card & Giuliano (2016), the Broward County screening study

**Source.** Card, D., & Giuliano, L. (2016). "Universal screening increases the representation of low-income
and minority students in gifted education." *PNAS*, 113(48), 13678–13683. DOI
[10.1073/pnas.1605043113](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1605043113). Working-paper version: NBER WP 21519
(2015), DOI [10.3386/w21519](https://doi.org/10.3386/w21519),
[https://www.nber.org/papers/w21519](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21519). Free full text of the WP:
[ERIC ED578122](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED578122.pdf).

**Sample and setting.** The papers say only "a large urban school district" in Florida. Broward County is the
identification made by the *New York Times* (April 10, 2016) and by other secondary coverage, not by the
authors in the paper. N = 78,065 for the full sample and 37,947 for the Plan B subsample; 140 larger
non-charter elementary schools. Pre cohorts: third graders in 2004 and 2005. Post cohorts: 2006 and 2007.
Pre-period demographics: 35% White non-Hispanic, 34% Black non-Hispanic, 25% Hispanic, 3% Asian; 45% FRL, 11%
ELL; 49% eligible under the lower "Plan B" IQ standard.

**The intervention.** From 2005 all second graders took the Naglieri Nonverbal Ability Test. Referral to a
district psychologist for IQ testing at NNAT ≥ 130 (non-disadvantaged) or ≥ 115 (FRL/ELL). Parent and teacher
referral and privately purchased testing all remained available. Crucially, "Without any changes in the
standards for gifted eligibility" — IQ 130 for Plan A, 116 for Plan B, unchanged throughout.

**Effects on identification.**
- Fraction of third graders with an IQ test on file rose by 8 percentage points.
- Gifted classification, Plan A eligibles: 5.1% → 6.6%. Plan B eligibles: 1.4% → 4.3%.
- Overall gifted rate: simple pre/post difference +2.2 ppt; trend-adjusted +1.5 ppt (SE 0.3); difference-in-
  differences against matched comparison schools in other Florida districts +1.6 ppt (SE 0.3). The change in
  the District was "2.65 SDs from the mean for the other districts."
- Odds-ratio results (logit, trend-adjusted): overall **+45%**; **Plan B +174%**; **Plan A +11%**; **Black
  +74%**; **Hispanic +118%**; **White +12%**. Black vs Hispanic difference not significant (P = 0.44);
  Black+Hispanic vs White significant (P < 0.01).

**Who the newly identified students were (compliers vs always-takers).** 80% of compliers were Plan B
eligible. Black 23% vs 12%; Hispanic 46% vs 16%; White 8% vs 61%; parents speak English 29% vs 74%; FRL 67%
vs 20%; ELL 18% vs 2%; female 56% vs 45%. Compliers had third-grade achievement about 0.42 SD lower and mean
IQ 7.36 points lower (124.3 vs 131.6).

**The finding that transfers to a top-percentile program.** Screening did not only find borderline
disadvantaged students. In the Plan B group, "a full 20% of the compliers have IQs of 130 or higher (vs. 25%
of the always takers)." And in the Plan A group: "many of the newly identified Plan A gifted students had IQ
scores well above the minimum 130-point threshold. We thus conclude that the traditional referral system also
misses some high-ability nondisadvantaged students."

Also: "the compliers have lower achievement scores than the always takers, but about the same mean IQ scores.
This suggests that the traditional referral system tends to miss disadvantaged students with modest
achievement levels, regardless of their cognitive abilities."

**Distributional effect across schools.** Before screening, "Half of gifted students in the pre cohort were
at schools that enrolled only 18% of the entire third-grade population, whereas half of all third-grade
students in the District were at schools that enrolled a total of only 16% of the gifted students." After
screening the distribution moved much closer to proportional.

### 2.2 What happened when screening was eliminated

This is documented, in the authors' own words, in two places.

PNAS (2016): "because of financial pressures caused by the Great Recession, the District cut funding for IQ
testing in 2007 and suspended the screening program in 2010. **By 2011, the gifted share of third graders had
returned to the level of 2004–2005.**"

NBER WP 20453 (2014): "A financial crisis in 2007 caused the District to cut funding for IQ testing, leading
to a drop in the placement rate of Plan B students. The screening program was temporarily suspended in 2010
and re-introduced with a new test in 2011, but as of 2012 the gifted fraction of disadvantaged students had
only rebounded slightly."

The entire gain was reversible and reversed within roughly a year of the program stopping. The mechanism was
budget, not evidence.

### 2.3 Two numeric discrepancies I want on the record

1. **The widely quoted "180% / 130% / 80%" figures are from the 2015 working paper, not the peer-reviewed
   article.** Secondary coverage (e.g. K-12 Dive) reports "a 180% increase in the gifted rate among all
   disadvantaged students, with a 130% increase among Latinos and an 80% increase among black students." The
   published PNAS version reports 174%, 118% and 74%. Use the PNAS numbers.
2. **A March 2014 preliminary draft of the companion paper reports different baseline rates.** A seminar
   version hosted at the University of Virginia
   ([PDF](https://economics.virginia.edu/sites/economics.virginia.edu/files/applied/giuliano.pdf), header
   reads "Does Gifted Education Work? For Which Students? … March 2014 (Preliminary/Incomplete version)")
   says screening "raised the gifted fraction of disadvantaged students from under 0.5% to over 2%." The
   September 2014 NBER version says "from around 1.5% to nearly 4%," and the published PNAS Table 1 gives
   1.4% → 4.3%. Use the published PNAS figures; treat the preliminary draft as superseded.

**Cost of screening — flagged as not peer-reviewed.** The NBER Digest summary states the district "conducted
1,300 additional IQ tests annually, incurring high overtime costs"
([https://www.nber.org/digest/nov15/who-gets-gifted-and-talented-education-programs](https://www.nber.org/digest/nov15/who-gets-gifted-and-talented-education-programs)).
A University of Texas master's report cites $250,000–$300,000 per year and a $240,850/year CogAT contract for
Broward. **That report is a student capstone, not peer-reviewed; I would not cite its dollar figures without
a primary source.**

## 3. What crossing the gifted threshold actually buys — and for whom

**This is the most important section for GT's question, so I am reporting it in full detail.**

### 3.1 Source and publication status

Card, D., & Giuliano, L. (2014). "Does Gifted Education Work? For Which Students?" NBER Working Paper 20453.
DOI [10.3386/w20453](https://doi.org/10.3386/w20453). Full text:
[https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20453/w20453.pdf](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20453/w20453.pdf).

**Flag: the NBER landing page for w20453 lists no published version.** The high-achiever half of the analysis
was published as Card, D., & Giuliano, L. (2016), "Can Tracking Raise the Test Scores of High-Ability Minority
Students?", *American Economic Review*, 106(10), 2783–2816, DOI
[10.1257/aer.20150484](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20150484) (NBER WP 22104, DOI
[10.3386/w22104](https://doi.org/10.3386/w22104)). As far as I can verify, **the Plan A and Plan B null
results exist only in the unpublished working paper.** That is a real limitation on the strongest single
finding here.

### 3.2 Sample and setting

Same large Florida district. Third graders 2004–2011, N = 159,895 across 140 regular elementary schools.
Program: from 2004, every school had to create a separate gifted classroom for all fourth and fifth graders
identified gifted, with remaining seats filled by the highest scorers on the previous year's state tests.
Average school had 8.5 gifted fourth graders in 2007-08; only 8% of schools had over 20.

Three admission pathways into the *same* classroom:

| Group | Rule | Analysis sample |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan A gifted | Non-disadvantaged, IQ ≥ 130 | N = 2,679, IQ 120–140 |
| Plan B gifted | FRL or ELL, IQ ≥ 116 | N = 4,472, IQ 105–125 |
| High achievers | Top ranks on prior-year state tests within school/grade, no IQ threshold | N = 2,098 advantaged + 2,046 disadvantaged, fourth grade 2009–2012 |

Between-school design: 255 fourth-grade school/year cohorts at 94 elementary schools, 2009–12.

Plan A students score 1.2–1.4 SD above the district mean in reading and math. This is the group closest to
GT's applicant pool.

### 3.3 Result 1 — no effect at the high IQ threshold

Plan A, RD at IQ 130. First stage is strong: +26.7 ppt probability of being in a gifted classroom, +49.4 ppt
probability of being classified gifted, peer lagged scores +0.143 SD. The outcomes (cohort and school fixed
effects plus student controls, SEs clustered by school):

- Fourth-grade reading: −0.052 (0.050)
- Fourth-grade math: −0.084 (0.064)
- Fourth-grade writing: −0.083 (0.081)

Donut-hole and first-differenced specifications give the same answer.

> "For non-disadvantaged (Plan A) gifted students our alternative specifications yield uniformly small and
> insignificant reduced form impacts of gifted program participation on standardized achievement in math,
> reading, and writing."

> "Importantly, the data for both groups suggest that even infra-marginal Plan A and Plan B students gain
> little from the receipt of gifted services."

Plan B, RD at IQ 116: reading 0.013 (0.032), math 0.004 (0.035), writing 0.116 (0.047, p < .05). Writing is
the one positive; scaled by the first stage it implies "a treatment effect per Plan B student who is assigned
into a gifted classroom of about 0.3 standard deviation units."

### 3.4 Result 2 — a real effect at the achievement-rank threshold

High achievers, RD on within-cohort test-score rank. First stage +31.9 ppt, peer lagged scores +0.277 SD.
Reduced form: reading 0.093 (0.031, p < .01), math 0.087 (0.035, p < .05), writing ≈ 0. Scaled up,
"treatment-on-the-treated effects of being placed in a gifted classroom of roughly 0.3 σ's."

By subgroup, the TOT estimates for fourth-grade reading and math are "in the range of 0.4–0.6 σ's" for
FRL-eligible students and minorities — "large enough to close the corresponding achievement gaps between
FRL-eligible and ineligible high achievers, or between minority and white high achievers."

Notably this is *not* a peer-quality story: "FRL-eligible high achievers experience a smaller average gain in
peer quality when they enter a gifted classroom (0.75 σ's) than FRL-ineligible students (0.98 σ's).
Nevertheless, FRL-eligibles gain more, casting some doubt on the importance of peer effects as a mediator."

### 3.5 Result 3 — the answer to "top of the distribution or the margin?" It is the margin, monotonically

Table 7 of the working paper splits the top-20 rank group into four bands and estimates the effect of having
at least one gifted child in the cohort (and therefore a gifted classroom) on each band separately. Baseline
achievement is the mean third-grade reading-and-math composite in SD units.

| Rank band | Baseline achievement | First stage | Reduced form | 2SLS (treatment on treated) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ranked 1–5 | 1.30 σ | 0.42** | 0.04 (0.07) | **0.11 (0.15)** |
| Ranked 6–10 | 0.83 σ | 0.41** | 0.07 (0.04) | 0.16 (0.11) |
| Ranked 11–15 | 0.59 σ | 0.32** | 0.13** (0.05) | 0.39* (0.15) |
| Ranked 16–20 | 0.42 σ | 0.21** | 0.10* (0.04) | **0.50* (0.22)** |

(Sample: 255 fourth-grade school/year cohorts. Controls: class-wide mean reading and math, class-wide FRL
fraction, class size, number with valid scores. `+` p<.10, `*` p<.05, `**` p<.01.)

The authors explicitly note that the top band is the Plan A analogue: students ranked 1–5 "have much higher
achievement (1.3σ's) — **about the same as the Plan A analysis group**." Their effect estimate is 0.11 SD and
statistically indistinguishable from zero.

The authors' own reading of the pattern:

> "the 2SLS (treatment on the treated) estimates in column 4 show a pattern of increasing effects for
> lower-ranked groups."

> "we suspect that the variation reflects systematically larger treatment effects for students with lower
> initial levels of achievement, who have the most to gain."

And from the conclusions:

> "**The gains appear to be largest for students who are closest to the threshold and furthest away from the
> traditional thresholds that determine entry to gifted programs in other settings.**"

They also rule out composition as the explanation: "The variation in the estimated treatment effects across
the rank groups in Table 7 is too large to be explained by the (minor) variation in the fractions of
low-income and minority children across the groups... It is also not explained by heterogeneity across
schools or teachers, since the subgroups are balanced across classes."

### 3.6 Why the top gets nothing — the authors' three candidate explanations

1. **Wrong outcome measure.** "Since most Plan A students already score in the top percentiles of the state
   tests, parents and teachers may be less concerned with mastering the basic curriculum and more concerned
   with higher-level learning objectives (and in simply keeping Plan A students engaged in school)."
   Consistent with this, there *is* "a positive jump in student satisfaction at the 130 IQ bar for Plan A
   students."
2. **Flat of the curve.** "A related explanation is that it is very hard to raise the scores of students who
   are already performing very well - a 'flat of the curve' effect that may also explain the variation in
   treatment effects across the different rank-groups in Table 7."
3. **Non-cognitive deficit, for Plan B specifically.** "Students selected on the basis of previous test
   scores have a combination of cognitive abilities and non-cognitive traits like attention-to-task and
   willingness to meet social expectations that are necessary to perform well on low-stakes tests of routine
   knowledge. **Students selected entirely on the basis of IQ may lack these traits.**" Supporting evidence:
   Plan B compliers "score roughly 1.6 σ's above the mean on the NNAT test (a measure of cognitive ability)
   but only 0.7 σ's above the mean in third-grade reading and math achievement" — i.e., they are
   underachievers relative to their ability. Plan B students also show a *fall* in satisfaction on entering a
   gifted class, unlike Plan A.

They explicitly test and reject ceiling effects: only 2% of Plan A students with IQ 125–135 hit the maximum
score. "Overall we believe that topping out cannot account for the absence of impacts on Plan A students."

### 3.7 The published AER version adds a sharper heterogeneity result

From the AER 2016 / WP 22104 abstract: "participation in a GHA class leads to significant achievement gains
for non-gifted participants, **concentrated among black and Hispanic students, who gain 0.5 standard
deviation units in fourth grade reading and math scores, with persistent effects to at least sixth grade.
Importantly, we find no evidence of spillovers on non-participants.**"

The white/minority contrast is stark and is not an income story:

> "there are small and insignificant effects for whites, but large positive effects for minorities."

> "We have also fit a parallel set of models for subgroups of whites. The resulting estimates are all small in
> magnitude and uniformly insignificant. Even for lower-income white students who are FRL eligible we find no
> evidence of an effect of participating in a GHA class. Hence we conclude that race/ethnicity - not family
> income - is the fundamental dimension of heterogeneity in the GHA treatment effect."

Overall average TOT was "about 0.3 σ's per treated student" in fourth grade; by sixth grade, "marginally
significant impacts of about 0.2σ's for reading and significant impacts of about 0.4σ's for math."

Mechanism: not teachers, not peers. "we find small and insignificant changes in TVA [teacher value added] at
the GHA threshold for both whites and minorities." Peer lagged test scores jumped by essentially the same
amount for both groups (TOT 0.88 white, 0.83 minority) — "we cannot reject an effect of 0.85 for any
subgroup." The authors instead hypothesize that "higher-ability minority students face obstacles in the
regular classroom environment that cause them to underperform relative to their potential."

### 3.8 Caveats the authors state themselves

- Running-variable manipulation: "A key problem is that the IQ scores of marginally eligible students are
  often boosted to meet the gifted thresholds." They address it with donut-hole, first-differenced and
  donut-hole-differenced specifications, all of which agree.
- The gifted classrooms are not an accelerated program: "gifted classes follow the same curriculum as other
  classes, and the students write the same statewide achievement tests each year." Teachers "receive no
  higher pay, and are on average only slightly more experienced." The main difference reported is pacing
  flexibility.
- Only 55% of Plan B students just above the IQ threshold are actually classified gifted, "possibly because
  of concerns that marginally eligible Plan B students will be" mismatched.

**Applicability flag for GT.** The outcome is grade-level state test scores in a program that teaches the
grade-level curriculum. A null at the top of the ability distribution on *that* outcome does not establish a
null for an accelerated program measured on far-above-grade-level attainment. What it does establish, and
establishes cleanly, is that the marginal admit at a high threshold in this setting gained nothing measurable,
while students well below the threshold gained the most.

## 4. Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)

**Source.** Bui, S. A., Craig, S. G., & Imberman, S. A. (2014). "Is Gifted Education a Bright Idea? Assessing
the Impact of Gifted and Talented Programs on Students." *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 6(3),
30–62. DOI [10.1257/pol.6.3.30](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.6.3.30). Working paper: NBER WP 17089 (2011),
titled "…on Achievement," full text
[https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17089/w17089.pdf](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17089/w17089.pdf).

**Flag: the published AEJ text is paywalled. The published conclusion below is quoted from the AEA's own
published abstract. All effect-size detail is from the 2011 NBER working paper and may differ from the
final published version.**

**Sample and setting.** "LUSD," described as "a very large school district in the Southwestern US with over
200,000 students," "heavily minority and very low income." All fifth graders are universally evaluated for GT
eligibility regardless of prior status. Administrative data 2007–08 to 2009–10; outcomes measured in sixth
and seventh grade. RD sample after restricting to a 20-unit band around the cutoff: approximately 2,600
students in one seventh-grade cohort and 5,500 in two sixth-grade cohorts. Lottery sample: 542 students
entering lotteries for two oversubscribed GT magnet middle schools; 18.8% had left the district by seventh
grade.

Eligibility is a matrix combining NNAT points, Stanford/Aprenda achievement points, socioeconomic "obstacle"
points, fifth-grade grades and a teacher recommendation. The authors convert two thresholds into a single
Euclidean-distance cutoff.

**Authors' own stated conclusion, from the published abstract:**

> "We show that GT students in each analysis are exposed to higher achieving peers and, in the RD sample, a
> more advanced curriculum. **We find that achievement for marginal students neither improves nor worsens
> from GT services in the short run. We also find that lottery winners only perform better in science.** Using
> a bounding analysis we cannot rule out zero, though we do not find any significant negative effects."

**Working-paper abstract:** "for students on the margin there is no discernable impact on achievement even
though peers improve substantially… despite being exposed to higher quality teachers and peers that are one
standard deviation higher achieving, only science achievement improves. We argue that these results are
consistent with an invidious comparison model of peer effects offsetting other benefits."

**Effects and precision (2011 WP).**
- RD: 2SLS estimates near zero; math, reading and social science point estimates negative, all t-statistics
  below one. "Drawing 95% confidence intervals around the estimates, we can rule out modest positive impacts
  of GT on marginal students of **0.06 sd in math, 0.09 sd in reading, 0.15 sd in language, 0.13 sd in social
  studies and 0.23 sd in science.** The point estimates themselves, however, clearly suggest a zero effect."
- Peer quality did change: "peer achievement is between 0.24 and 0.35 standard deviations higher for GT
  students relative to non-GT students" in the RD; "0.7 to 1.2 standard deviations" in the lottery.
- Lottery: science +0.28 sd; nothing elsewhere. "the point estimates for math, reading and social studies are
  negative and the estimate for language is effectively zero."
- Attendance: a marginally significant −1.1 percentage point effect, "equivalent to attending school two
  fewer days in a 180 day school year," but "sensitive to specification."
- Teachers: "GT students do not appear to get assigned better teachers as measured by teacher fixed effects"
  in the RD; lottery winners did get higher-quality teachers.

**Why this matters for a selective program.** The lottery arm is *not* a marginal-student sample. "the
average GT student in 7th grade scores 1 standard deviation above the mean 7th grade student, [but] the
lottery participants score 1.6 standard deviations above the mean." So the higher-ability arm of this study
also found nothing but science. The authors' framing: "the lottery results reflect a LATE for students in the
upper portion of the GT distribution while the RD estimates reflect impacts on the 'marginal' GT students."

**Caveat the authors state.** "The caveat in our strategies, however, is that the alternative to GT treatment
varies. Marginal students not admitted to the GT program take 'regular' classes, while students that lose the
lottery receive GT services but in a less intensive atmosphere." The lottery contrast is magnet-GT versus
neighbourhood-GT, not GT versus nothing.

## 5. Multiple-criteria and combination rules: "and" versus "or"

### 5.1 The primary source is paywalled — stating this up front

McBee, M. T., Peters, S. J., & Waterman, C. (2014). "Combining Scores in Multiple-Criteria Assessment
Systems: The Impact of Combination Rule." *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 58(1), 69–89. DOI
[10.1177/0016986213513794](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986213513794).

**I could not obtain the full text.** Unpaywall returns `"oa_status": "closed"`, `"has_repository_copy":
false`, and no OA locations. The Sage page serves only the abstract and reference list. ERIC (EJ1020284) has
metadata only. **Every AND-versus-OR sensitivity number attributed to this paper below is therefore taken
from a secondary source that cites it, and I mark each one.**

### 5.2 What I *can* verify from the 2014 paper: its single-assessment baseline table

Table 1 of McBee, Peters & Miller (2016) is labelled "Table adapted from McBee et al. (2014)" and reproduces
the 2014 paper's single-assessment results in full. Sensitivity = proportion of truly qualifying students who
are actually identified.

| Cutoff | Reliability .95 | .90 | .85 | .80 | .75 | .70 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 90th percentile | .843 | .776 | .725 | .680 | .641 | .604 |
| **95th percentile** | **.815** | .738 | .679 | .628 | .582 | .541 |
| 99th percentile | .763 | .665 | .592 | .530 | .476 | .428 |

(Perfect reliability gives 1.000 at every cutoff. False negative rate is 1 − sensitivity.)

**The number that matters most for GT.** At the 95th percentile with a .95-reliability test and *no gate of
any kind* — a single test given to everyone — sensitivity is .815. **18.5% of children who truly belong above
the line are rejected, before any other feature of the process is considered.** The 2016 authors' comment on
the .95 assumption: "this is as efficacious as a single-assessment system can get in the real world because
it is extremely difficult to design instruments that can achieve reliabilities of greater than .95. In fact,
most identification instruments do not achieve reliabilities that high."

### 5.3 AND versus OR sensitivity figures — secondary attribution, flagged

Source of the characterization: Yu, H. (2017). *Novel Methods to Address Measurement Error Issues in Gifted
Identification.* Doctoral dissertation, University of Connecticut, No. 1524.
[https://opencommons.uconn.edu/dissertations/1524](https://opencommons.uconn.edu/dissertations/1524).

Yu describes McBee et al. (2014) as having tested the conjunctive ("and") and complementary ("or") rules on
two strongly correlated true scores (r = .80) with equal reliability, and reports:

- **90th percentile cutoff, two observed scores, reliability .95:** conjunctive sensitivity **.79** ("misses
  about 20% of TG students"); complementary sensitivity **.87** ("misses about 13%"); single-measure **.84**.
- **Reliability degrading .95 → .70 at the 90th percentile:** conjunctive .79 → .45; complementary .87 → .71;
  single-measure .84 → .60.
- **Cutoff rising 90th → 99th percentile at reliability .95:** conjunctive .79 → .69; complementary .87 →
  .79; single-measure .84 → .76.
- Ordering: "in terms of sensitivity, the complementary rule performs the best, the conjunctive rule performs
  the worst, and the compensatory rule performs in the middle."

**Flag: all six numbers in this subsection are Yu's characterization of McBee et al., not text I read in
McBee et al. I would want the primary before putting them in a client-facing document.**

The directional claim ("requiring two bars caps sensitivity near half") is well supported by the reliability-
degradation row: a conjunctive rule with two tests of realistic reliability lands between .45 and .79.

### 5.4 A verifiable open-access substitute for the identification-rate question

McBee, M. T., & Makel, M. C. (2019). "The Quantitative Implications of Definitions of Giftedness." *AERA
Open*, 5(1). DOI [10.1177/2332858419831007](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419831007). Open access. Method:
analytic integration of the multivariate normal distribution, plus Monte Carlo for the complex cases. No human
sample.

Two criteria, top-5% cutoff on each:
- "under the 'and' rule, the proportion of students meeting the criteria for giftedness ranges from around
  **0.25% if the two variables are uncorrelated** scores to **5% if they are perfectly correlated**."
- "the proportion identified as gifted under the 'or' rule ranges from **nearly 10% when the criteria are
  perfectly uncorrelated to 5% when they are perfectly correlated**."
- Worked realistic cases: "**Using the top 5% cutoff for giftedness, a correlation of r = .27 yields a
  giftedness rate of 9.3% under the 'or' rule and a rate of 0.65% under the 'and' rule. A correlation of r =
  .67 yields a rate of 8.2% under the 'or' rule and 1.8% under the 'and' rule.**"
- The design implication in the authors' words: "for a definition to remain internally consistent,
  multiple-criteria systems based on the 'and' rule must be designed around the assumption that **2% or less
  of the student population will qualify**, whereas 'or'-based definitions must presume that **8% to 10% of
  the population will qualify**."

Adding a third criterion "makes the 'and' rule even more restrictive… Conversely, a third criterion makes the
'or' rule even more permissive."

The same paper summarizes the nomination literature: "McBee, Peters, and Miller (2016) found that the
sensitivity of a typical identification process is on the order of 30% to 40% due to poor psychometric
performance."

### 5.5 The important qualifier: combination rule may be a proxy for pool size

Lakin, J. M. (2018). "Making the Cut in Gifted Selection: Score Combination Rules and Their Impact on Program
Diversity." *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 62(2), 210–219. DOI
[10.1177/0016986217752099](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986217752099). Named GCQ Paper of the Year.

Sample and setting: real data, "Grades K to 5 from the CogAT 7 national standardization data."

Verified from the primary abstract: "Past research has indicated that OR combination rules… have been shown
to identify the largest number of students compared with AND… and AVERAGE… rules. We extended this work to a
real data set… and found that the different combination rules led to differences in the number and diversity
of students identified. **However, we found these diversity differences were due mainly to the identified pool
size and not to the effects of the particular combination rule.**"

**Flagged as secondary:** a blog post by Russell T. Warne
([link](https://russellwarne.com/2019/11/20/combining-cut-scores-for-gifted-identification-no-impact-on-diversity/))
reports the following from Lakin's tables, which I could not verify against the paywalled primary — conjunctive
n = 619, mean IQ 134.6 (SD 6.7); compensatory n = 2,646, mean IQ 127.5 (SD 6.2); disjunctive n = 5,602, mean
IQ 120.9 (SD 8.1); and that when group sizes are equated "the methods identify 95-99% of the exact same
children as gifted." **Blog, not peer-reviewed. Treat the n's and means as unverified.**

If Lakin's conclusion holds, the honest framing for GT is: choosing "or" over "and" is a decision about *how
many* children to admit, not a free accuracy gain. The accuracy gain claimed for "or" in the McBee line of
work is real at fixed cutoffs, but the pool grows at the same time.

## 6. The nomination or referral gate placed before testing

**Source.** McBee, M. T., Peters, S. J., & Miller, E. M. (2016). "The Impact of the Nomination Stage on
Gifted Program Identification: A Comprehensive Psychometric Analysis." *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 60(4),
258–278. DOI [10.1177/0016986216656256](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256). Free full text:
[PDF](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2016/07/Gifted-Child-Quarterly-2016-McBee-0016986216656256.pdf).

**Method and "sample."** This is not an empirical study of children. It is an analytic psychometric model:
the joint distribution of nomination true score, nomination observed score, confirmation true score and
confirmation observed score is assumed multivariate normal, and the required probabilities are computed by
numerical integration (R, `sadmvn` from `mnormt`). The parameters that are empirical are the validity
coefficients, taken from published test manuals. **No student sample, no district, no years.** That is both
its strength (exact, general) and its limitation (it inherits its realism entirely from the assumed
parameters).

**Baseline with no gate.** Test reliability .95, cutoff at the 90th percentile: sensitivity **.843**. "This
value is the maximum possible performance." Screeners "cannot improve the sensitivity of an assessment system;
they can only reduce it."

**Table 3 — the effect of a nomination gate, by the gate's validity and cutoff.** Nomination instrument is the
SIGS General Intellectual Ability subscale; confirmation test has reliability .95 and a 90th-percentile
cutoff.

| Confirmation test (validity of gate against it) | Gate at 50th pct | 60th | 70th | 80th | 90th |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| WISC-III (r = .67) | .798 | .759 | .694 | .588 | .404 |
| TCS-2 (r = .73) | .818 | .790 | .738 | .640 | .453 |
| **CogAT (r = .48)** | .709 | .643 | .556 | .441 | **.280** |

**The .843 → .280 figure is confirmed against the primary source.** Read precisely: sensitivity falls from
.843 with no gate to .280 when a gate whose validity against the CogAT is only .48 is set at the 90th
percentile. False negative rate at that point is .720, and only 3.1% of the population ends up identified.

**Table 5 — sensitivity by gate cutoff at nomination validity .70, nomination reliability .90.**

| Gate cutoff | .05 | .25 | .50 | .65 | .75 | .80 | .85 | .90 | .95 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Absolute sensitivity | .843 | .840 | .809 | .750 | .672 | .614 | .535 | .428 | .272 |
| Relative to no gate | 1.000 | .996 | .960 | .889 | .797 | .728 | .635 | .508 | .323 |

**Table 6 — how low the gate has to sit to be nearly free.** To lose no more than 2% of relative sensitivity
with a gate of validity .60, the gate cutoff must be at about the 30th percentile. In the authors' worked
example: "This implies that **70% of the students can be expected to pass the nomination stage** and should
receive the Stage-II confirmatory assessment." Accepting a 20% relative sensitivity loss lets you gate at the
67th percentile and test 33% of students, but "The 20% loss of relative sensitivity implies an absolute
sensitivity of 67.4%, so roughly a third of the school's gifted students would not be identified even though
they should have been."

**Why the asymmetry exists — the clearest statement of the mechanism.**

> "the confirmatory test provides strong protection against incorrect identifications because false positives
> with respect to the nomination are unlikely to successfully qualify on the test. In contrast, **the
> confirmatory assessment can provide no protection whatsoever against false negatives** because those
> students that get a false negative with respect to the nomination are denied access to the confirmatory
> test."

Consequently, "The optimal nomination cutoff… is therefore found at the point of maximum sensitivity, and
this value will be found at the lowest possible cutoff on the nomination instrument. A ROC curve analysis for
the two-stage identification system would therefore suggest that the screening process be removed."

**Abstract and discussion conclusions, verbatim.**

> "Results showed that in nearly all conditions, identification systems that require a nomination before
> testing result in a large proportion of gifted students being missed. **Under commonly implemented
> conditions, the nomination stage can cause the false negative rate to easily exceed 60%.**"

> "unless nomination stages are carefully constructed with high validity (which requires high reliability)
> and low cutoffs (low especially when compared to traditional gifted education cutoffs of 90th percentile or
> higher), they are almost always extremely detrimental to identification system performance."

> "Currently implemented nomination practices likely result in very few false positive identification
> decisions (meaning few students are identified 'on accident') at the cost of a tremendous numbers of false
> negatives."

> "Consider abolishing the two-stage system completely. Testing all students with simple and (relatively)
> lower-quality assessments will almost always result in better system sensitivity than two-stage systems in
> which a poor nomination process is followed by elaborate, expensive, and high-quality testing."

**The authors' own carve-out, which is directly relevant to a selective program.** They do not claim a
high-false-negative regime is always wrong:

> "In some cases this may be an appropriate balance—such as **when programs could be dangerous or harmful for
> those students who are not truly ready.**"

That is the exact argument a program with an intensive, accelerated curriculum would make. The authors'
position is that it must be argued, not assumed: "we believe that in most cases, this practice is needlessly
exclusionary."

They also state their own honest uncertainty, adapting Freedman (1991): the truth about nomination stages "is
bracketed by options two and three" — "Nomination stages sometimes work in the hands of skillful
practitioners, but aren't suitable for routine use" and "Nomination stages might work, but they haven't yet"
— "and we lean toward option three."

## 7. Adaptive, sequential and decision-theoretic thresholds

**Headline, stated honestly: I found no empirical study of a gifted or selective-admission program that
updates its admission threshold as outcome data accumulates.** Nothing in this literature does what GT would
be doing if it built a threshold that learns. What does exist falls into three groups.

### 7.1 Sequential two-stage designs with a different cut score at each stage — the Talent Search model

This is a real, long-running, two-stage design where the second stage uses a *different instrument* rather
than a higher cut on the same one.

**Source.** Assouline, S. G., & Lupkowski-Shoplik, A. (2012). "The Talent Search Model of Gifted
Identification." *Journal of Psychoeducational Assessment*, 30(1), 45–59. DOI
[10.1177/0734282911433946](https://doi.org/10.1177/0734282911433946).

Stage 1: national percentile 95–99 on a grade-level achievement test — students who "have hit a ceiling on
that test." Stage 2: an above-level test designed for older students. On the stage-1 cutoff: "The use of
qualifying scores at the 95th percentile on in-grade achievement tests has been supported empirically."

The empirical claim that makes the design work, from Swiatek, M. A. (2007), "The Talent Search Model: Past,
Present, and Future," *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 51(4), 320–329, DOI
[10.1177/0016986207306318](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986207306318)
([PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2007-swiatek.pdf)):

> "Despite the fact that all talent search participants have in-grade test scores in the top 3% to 5% of U.S.
> students in their grade, **the scores they earn on above-level tests are normally distributed, with scores
> covering most or all of the range of the instrument.**"

Illustrative magnitude: "two students of the same age have scores at the 98th percentile on in-grade
achievement tests in mathematics… One student may earn an above-level test score at the 85th percentile in
math, whereas the other may score at the 30th."

**Source for the ceiling claim.** Warne, R. T. (2012). "History and Development of Above-Level Testing of the
Gifted." *Roeper Review*, 34, 183–193. DOI
[10.1080/02783193.2012.686425](https://doi.org/10.1080/02783193.2012.686425).
[PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2012-warne.pdf).

> "the empirical literature supports the view that the test ceiling for gifted children is raised through
> above-level testing… **I have been unable to find an example in the literature of a group of gifted children
> who have not obtained higher scores on a test that was at least two levels above their age group than the
> maximum scale score of the grade-level test.** The fact that above-level testing has raised the test ceiling
> for high-ability examinees is probably the most consistent finding presented in this literature review and
> one of the hardest to ignore."

Warne's own stated limit: "there is no strong consensus about what constitutes an observed 'ceiling effect,'
beyond obtaining the maximum score possible on a grade-level test." Validation studies "have frequently found
that children who score at the 95th percentile or higher on a grade-level test tend to obtain scores on an
above-level test that would be approximately average for students 4 or more years older than them."

**Relevance to GT.** This is the one design in the literature that is *about* discriminating within the top
5%, which is precisely GT's problem. It is also the design GT's current single 95th-percentile CogAT cut does
not implement: the CogAT is the stage-1 instrument, and there is no stage 2.

### 7.2 A posterior-probability admission rule applied to gifted identification

**Source.** Yu, H. (2017). *Novel Methods to Address Measurement Error Issues in Gifted Identification.*
Doctoral dissertation, University of Connecticut, No. 1524.
[https://opencommons.uconn.edu/dissertations/1524](https://opencommons.uconn.edu/dissertations/1524).
**Flag: unpublished dissertation, simulation only, not peer-reviewed.**

**Method and sample.** Simulation. Three tests generated from a one-parameter IRT model with marginal
reliabilities .95 (intelligence), .90 (math), .80 (reading). 1,000,000 pairs of true and observed abilities
per domain. No real students.

**The idea.** Replace the rule "observed score ≥ cutoff" with "P(true score ≥ cutoff | all observed scores) ≥
τ," and treat τ as the dial. Because the posterior is a continuous function of the evidence, τ produces a
sensitivity/precision frontier rather than a single point.

Identifying students truly gifted in both math and intelligence (true score ≥ 2.0 SD on both). PPR = positive
predictive rate.

| Rule | Sensitivity | PPR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional observed-score AND rule (X₂ ≥ 2 & X₁ ≥ 2) | 0.72 | 0.82 |
| Posterior rule, τ = .20 | 0.98 | 0.72 |
| Posterior rule, τ = .40 | 0.90 | 0.83 |
| Posterior rule, τ = .50 | 0.88 | 0.85 |
| Posterior rule, τ = .70 | 0.71 | 0.97 |
| Posterior rule, τ = .90 | 0.65 | 1.00 |

At τ = .50 the posterior rule beats the conventional AND rule on *both* metrics (0.88 vs 0.72 sensitivity,
0.85 vs 0.82 PPR). For reading and intelligence the conventional rule gives sensitivity 0.63 / PPR 0.74; the
posterior rule at τ = .50 gives 0.84 / 0.81.

Author's stated conclusion: "using the posterior probability of being gifted given multiple observed
abilities to identify gifted students has the potential to simultaneously improve the PPR" and sensitivity.

### 7.3 Decision-theoretic and expected-utility cut scores — a mature literature, almost none of it about gifted education

- **van der Linden, W. J., & Mellenbergh, G. J. (1977).** "Optimal Cutting Scores Using a Linear Loss
  Function." *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 1(4), 593–599. DOI
  [10.1177/014662167700100414](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662167700100414). "Using a linear loss function, a
  procedure is described for computing a cutting score that minimizes the risk for the decision rule. The
  procedure is demonstrated with a criterion-referenced achievement test of elementary statistics administered
  to **167 students**."
- **van der Linden, W. J. (1987).** "Applications of Decision Theory to Test-Based Decision Making." Research
  Report 87-9, Department of Education, University of Twente. ERIC
  [ED309189](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED309189.pdf). Distinguishes selection, mastery, placement and
  classification decisions, and explicitly names **sequential testing** among the generalizations. Traces the
  framework to "the publication of the Taylor-Russell (1939) tables… and Cronbach and Gleser's (1965)"
  work. **Flag: Taylor & Russell (1939) and Cronbach & Gleser (1965) are cited here at second hand; I did not
  independently verify either. Cronbach & Gleser, *Psychological Tests and Personnel Decisions*, 2nd ed.,
  University of Illinois Press, is a book with no DOI I verified.**
- **de Gruijter, D. N. M., & Hambleton, R. K. (1984).** *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 8(1), 1–8. DOI
  [10.1177/014662168400800101](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662168400800101). **Note: the Crossref record
  carries a corrupted title, "On de Encountered Using Decision Theory to Set Cutoff Scores"; the intended
  title concerns problems encountered using decision theory to set cutoff scores. I am flagging this rather
  than silently correcting it.** This is the standard cautionary paper and the abstract states the caution
  precisely:

  > "In the decision-theoretic approach to determining a cutoff score, the cutoff score chosen is that which
  > maximizes expected utility of pass/fail decisions. This approach is not without its problems… inaccurate
  > parameter estimates, choice of test model and consequences, choice of subpopulations, optimal cutoff
  > scores on various occasions, and cutoff scores as targets. It is suggested that these problems will need
  > to be overcome and/or understood more thoroughly before the full potential of the decision-theoretic
  > approach can be realized in practice."

  The first-listed problem — inaccurate parameter estimates — is the binding one for GT, because the utility
  of admitting a marginal child is exactly the quantity nobody has measured.

- **A registered but unverified study directly on this question.** Long, D. A., McCoach, D. B., & Peters, S.
  J. (2021). "The Implications of Modified Selection Criteria on the Size, Academic Profile, and Diversity of
  Students Identified for Gifted Services in Two- and One-Stage Gifted Identification Systems." OSF, DOI
  [10.17605/osf.io/vqxjn](https://doi.org/10.17605/osf.io/vqxjn),
  [https://osf.io/vqxjn/](https://osf.io/vqxjn/). Verified via DataCite. The protocol proposes exactly what
  GT would want: comparing one-stage (universal consideration) against two-stage (universal screening)
  systems, across cutoff levels, building versus district norms, and AND/OR/MEAN plus "more complex
  combination rules that have not been empirically tested," using teacher-rating, achievement and CogAT data
  from a cohort of elementary students in eight districts. **Flag: this is a registered report protocol. I
  did not verify that results have been published, and I extracted no findings from it.**

### 7.4 What I searched for under "dynamic cutoffs" and did not find

Searches for adaptive or dynamic admission thresholds in education returned only machine-learning threshold-
tuning work from unrelated domains (at-risk-student early warning, clinical risk scoring, plagiarism
detection, drug discovery). None concerns admission to a selective academic program, none involves a
threshold that updates from realized student outcomes, and I am not citing any of it as evidence. **If a
literature on outcome-updating admission thresholds exists, I did not find it.**
