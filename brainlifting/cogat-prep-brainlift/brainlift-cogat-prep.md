# BrainLift: Preparing a Child for a Reasoning Test Without Merely Teaching the Test

## Owners

- Tiffany Lam

## Purpose

### Purpose

A family whose child is about to endure reasoning test for a selective program wants two completely
different things: a better result, and a child who reasons better. The preparation industry
mostly sells the first and implies the second. Instead, a preparation product should be able
to do both. The purpose of this BrainLift is to outline a placement test and remedial coursework
following the discipline that *a score moved*, *a skill improved*, and *the improvement generalizes beyond the practiced format*
are three separate claims with three separate evidence bases. A product which conflates them
is selling the weakest one while addressing the strongest. It argues from external evidence and
general principles only and is written to be read with no knowledge of any particular system or
implementation.

### In Scope

- **What practice and coaching do to ability-test scores.** Retest and practice-effect
  sizes, coaching-effect sizes on admissions-style tests, how much of a gain is format familiarity
  versus construct change, how gains decay, and how the size of the effect depends on the number of
  prior exposures.
- **The line between defensible preparation and score inflation.** The mechanism by
  which scores rise while the underlying skill does not, and what evidence distinguishes the two.
- **Whether reasoning itself is trainable, and how far a trained gain travels.** The transfer
  record for cognitive and working-memory training, the contrasting evidence that schooling raises
  measured ability, inductive-reasoning training in children, and the conditions where
  transfer has been observed.
- **Instructional methods with evidence for durable and transferable learning.** Retrieval practice,
  distributed practice, interleaving, worked examples and faded guidance, self-explanation, analogical
  comparison, and strategy or metacognitive instruction.
- **What each reasoning battery actually demands, and which demands are teachable.** The skills
  spanning verbal, quantitative, and figural reasoning question families, coachability
  across them, and developmental constraints across the elementary and middle-grade range.

### Out of Scope

- **Measuring how fast a child learns.** Mentioned in the `learning-rate-scoring` BrainLift.
- **Instrument-level measurement validity and quality at a selection cut.** Mentioned in the
  `gifted-assessment-quality` BrainLift.
- **The structure and sequencing of a test experience.** Adaptive routing, item ordering, session
  design, and retake policy are mentioned in the `test-structure` BrainLift.
- **Statistical methodology for validating one instrument against another.** Whether a practice score predicts an operational score, mentioned in the
  `test-evaluation` BrainLift.
- **Separating a program's effect from selection into it.** Mentioned in the
  `gt-school-counterfactual` BrainLift. Nothing here licenses a causal claim about a program.
- **Selection policy.** What cut a program should set, which children it should admit, and whether
  selective identification is the right model at all.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

**SPOV 1: A prep program should be judged on whether its gains hold up on a test it never taught. Otherwise, it's not improving how a child thinks.**

- **Elaboration:** The trap is easy to fall into. Simply taking a test a second time raises the score with no teaching involved, so a program reporting that "scores went up" may only be reporting that the child took the test twice while the child's underlying reasoning ability remained stagnant (1.1). That result is the one every prep claim has to survive. However, the fix already exists. Instead, the same children should receive a different test that nobody prepared them for. If they still do better, something real improved. If they only improve on the test they practiced, then all they learned was specialization within that test structure (2.2). **A program claiming to build reasoning should be measured on a test it did not teach to and should publish that result, even when it is unflattering.** A program that never runs the check has not left the claim untested but dropped it.

**SPOV 2: Teach a child a method for working things out, not a way of answering one kind of question.**

- **Elaboration:** Three bodies of evidence make sense when you read them together. The first is working memory and "brain training" software, where children improve substantially on the trained game but the improvement does not reach reasoning. Children who improved most at the game gained no more in reasoning than children who barely improved at all, which removes the explanation the approach depends on (3.1). Secondly, each additional year in ordinary schooling raises measured reasoning scores by a few points, and the gain lasts (3.2). Thus, reasoning is not fixed but rather does not move because of drills. The third lies between them. Programs that teach an explicit step-by-step method for finding patterns raised both reasoning scores and classroom learning. The gains grew over time instead of fading, and they helped schoolwork more than they helped the test (3.3). That ordering is the important part, because coaching produces the opposite pattern. Coaching only helps test-taking capabilities, not actual growth. The strategy a child uses explains most of the difference between the items they get right and wrong, and teaching the effective strategy made working memory stop predicting performance (5.3). That is worth teaching because it reframes the target. The thing to teach is not mental capacity a child lacks but a procedure nobody has shown them. **Every unit should teach a named method and then assess the child on problems that method was never practiced on.**

**SPOV 4: For optimal results, children taking standardized tests should be familiar with the testing format, but shouldn’t be exposed to real reproduced questions.**

-	**Elaboration:** Professional standards for test practice prohibit preparation that will raise scores without any real learning. However, to be effective, standards also require that test-takers be familiar with the format before taking the test (6.2). Therefore, if GT hides what the CogAT looks like and how it works from its test-takers, it is in-fact sabotaging the unfamiliar test-takers by making their test-taking experience worse. Thus, GT needs an introduction to the CogAT that gets the test-taker familiar with the format of the test without providing any real questions that may bias the test. At first, these seem contradictory, but a published continuum found that the professional standard line is breach when you start to teach skills that are matched specifically to the test (2.1). This is also where legally the boundary is drawn when it comes to infringing on test content as well, which means that this is a hard boundary that shouldn’t be crossed. Thus, any solution we come up with cannot even match the exact question formats seen on the test. Instead, we propose writing our own questions that test kids in the same general areas and ideas of the test, since that is permitted (6.1). For the CogAT, this means building a test that could cover the nonverbal, spatial, and verbal reasoning areas holistically. **Such a prep tool would need demonstrate correlation between performance in the tool and performance on the CogAT exam in order to demonstrate its effectiveness in predicting and preparing kids on CogAT style questions**.

## Experts

- **Expert 1 — Karl Josef Klauer (with Gary D. Phye)**
  - **Who:** Educational psychologist at RWTH Aachen who spent decades building and testing inductive-reasoning training programs for children, with Phye at Iowa State as his English-language collaborator and meta-analyst.
  - **Focus:** Teaching a single explicit procedural strategy, systematic comparison to detect regularities and irregularities across attributes and relations, and then measuring whether it moves fluid-intelligence scores and classroom learning.
  - **Why Follow:** His results carry the one signature that distinguishes learning from coaching, where transfer to academic learning that *exceeds* transfer to the test, with effects that grow rather than decay. This body of work licenses building a program that claims to improve reasoning, which is exactly why it matters.
  - **Where:** [Inductive Reasoning: A Training Approach](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ787070), [Training des induktiven Denkens — Fortschreibung der Metaanalyse von 2008](https://doi.org/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123)

- **Expert 2 — Monica Melby-Lervåg and Charles Hulme (with Thomas Redick)**
  - **Who:** Developmental and cognitive psychologists at Oslo and UCL, with Redick at Purdue, whose successive meta-analyses became the field's standard reference on whether cognitive training generalizes.
  - **Focus:** Working-memory and cognitive training, assessed specifically against active control groups and at follow-up rather than immediately after training.
  - **Why Follow:** Their finding is not merely that transfer is small but that the *size of a study's working-memory gain does not predict its transfer at all*, which removes the mechanism a capacity-training claim would need. Any training claim in this document has to survive their design standard, such as active control, and delayed measurement, or admit it has not been tested.
  - **Where:** [Working Memory Training Does Not Improve Performance on Measures of Intelligence or Other Measures of "Far Transfer"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033/), [Is working memory training effective? A meta-analytic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22612437/)

- **Expert 3 — William A. Mehrens (with Jacqueline Kaminski)**
  - **Who:** Measurement specialist at Michigan State University who wrote the field's most-cited attempt to say precisely which test-preparation activities are defensible and which are not.
  - **Focus:** A seven-point continuum running from general instruction in the domain to practice on the operational items themselves, with an argued position on where along it legitimacy ends.
  - **Why Follow:** His placement of the line between the third and fourth points is the single most actionable constraint in this BrainLift. He also holds that the line *moves with the inference the score user wants to draw*, which is the part most often dropped when he is cited.
  - **Where:** [Methods for Improving Standardized Test Scores: Fruitful, Fruitless, or Fraudulent?](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1989.tb00304.x)

- **Expert 4 — Daniel Koretz (with Daniel McCaffrey and Laura Hamilton)**
  - **Who:** Educational measurement scholar at Harvard, and the central figure documenting what happens to scores on tests that carry consequences.
  - **Focus:** Score inflation, the divergence between gains on a focal high-stakes test and gains on an independent audit test measuring the same domain, and the framework for validating gains under high-stakes conditions.
  - **Why Follow:** He counteracts Mehrens, not because he denies a line exists but because his evidence is that it is routinely crossed while everyone believes themselves on the right side of it, and that divergence between two instruments can license the word "inflation" without quantifying how much of a gain was real. He supplies the method this document adopts for proving a gain and the reason to distrust any program that skips it.
  - **Where:** [Toward a Framework for Validating Gains under High-Stakes Conditions](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED462410.pdf)

- **Expert 5 — David F. Lohman (with Katrina Korb and Joni Lakin)**
  - **Who:** Psychometrician at the University of Iowa, a co-author of a widely used group reasoning battery, and its most informed internal critic.
  - **Focus:** What nonverbal reasoning tests actually measure, how they perform for English-language learners, and whether substituting a nonverbal battery solves the identification problem it is adopted to solve.
  - **Why Follow:** He argues nonverbal items are verbally mediated and shows English learners scoring substantially below their peers on nonverbal instruments. Any product that treats the figural battery as the culture-fair one, or as safe ground for coaching, has to answer him. He is more credible for it precisely because he helped build the instrument.
  - **Where:** [Identifying Academically Gifted English-Language Learners Using Nonverbal Tests](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986208321808)

- **Expert 6 — David H. Uttal and Nora S. Newcombe**
  - **Who:** Cognitive and developmental psychologists at Northwestern and Temple, whose meta-analysis of spatial training is the most-cited positive transfer result in the cognitive-training literature.
  - **Focus:** Whether spatial skills are malleable, whether trained gains endure, and whether they transfer to spatial tasks that were never practiced.
  - **Why Follow:** They are the counterexample that stops Melby-Lervag's null from being read as "nothing transfers," such as durable gains, transfer to untrained tasks, and a causal demonstration in eight-year-olds. They also mark the boundary of the claim, since transfer within a domain is a far weaker result than transfer to reasoning at large.
  - **Where:** [The malleability of spatial skills: A meta-analysis of training studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028446), [First demonstration of effective spatial training for near and far transfer at 8 years](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12909)

- **Expert 7 — Dedre Gentner (with Louis Alfieri, Timothy Nokes-Malach and Christian Schunn)**
  - **Who:** Cognitive scientist at Northwestern who formalized analogy as structure mapping, with Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn supplying the meta-analytic test of comparison as an instructional method.
  - **Focus:** How comparing cases strips away surface features and leaves a relational schema, and whether that schema then applies to instances sharing no objects with the examples.
  - **Why Follow:** She supplies the *mechanism* the rest of this document needs. If reasoning training works by anything, the best-specified candidate is relational abstraction through comparison, demonstrated in children young enough to rule out verbal instruction as the route. Her work also carries an awkward moderator for program design. Comparison's advantage is largest immediately and shrinks with delay, the opposite of the methods that make learning stick.
  - **Where:** [Structure-Mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3), [Learning Through Case Comparisons: A Meta-Analytic Review](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)

- **Expert 8 — Corentin Gonthier (with Nicolas Roulin and Jean-Luc Thomassin)**
  - **Who:** Cognitive psychologist working on how people actually solve matrix reasoning items, rather than on what those items are assumed to measure.
  - **Focus:** Strategy on figural matrices, constructive matching versus response elimination, and what strategy explains once working-memory capacity is accounted for.
  - **Why Follow:** His result is the most design-relevant finding. Strategy accounts for the majority of item-to-item accuracy variance and *fully mediates* the relationship between working-memory capacity and performance, which reframes the trainability question. The target is not capacity, which Melby-Lervag shows is not trainable, but the procedure a capable child may simply never have been taught.
  - **Where:** [Strategy use fully mediates the relationship between working memory capacity and performance on Raven's matrices](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000101), [Intraindividual strategy shifts in Raven's matrices](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000660)

## DOK 3: Insights

**Insight 1 - A score gain is only meaningful if it can transfer to an independent measure of the same domain**
- A score gain is only meaningful if it generalizes to other IQ sectors. Gains reflecting real growth in the tested domains are entirely separate from gains reflecting familiarity with a particular test's items and formats. If it the rise in score doesn't transfer to an independent measure of the same domain, then it does not settle where the line falls for any specific preparation activity. Most sources say the boundary depends on the inference the score user wants to draw, and it does not take into account for contested middle cases.

**Insight 2 - A program that wants real reasoning skill rather than format familiarity has to be built from methods evidenced for durability and transfer, which is unevenly distributed**
- A preparation program that wants to build real reasoning skill rather than format familiarity has to be built out of methods whose evidence base is about durability (survives a delay) and transfer (works on material that was not practiced), not just immediate post-test gains. Eight method families carry serious empirical weight: retrieval practice, distributed practice, interleaving, analogical comparison, worked examples with faded guidance, self-explanation, metacognitive strategy instruction, and feedback design. However, their evidence is very unevenly distributed across populations and across transfer distances. Several of the strongest-looking effects rest mainly on undergraduate samples learning verbal materials over short intervals, and several show transfer only to items structurally identical to those practiced. The two families with the most direct claim to teaching *relational reasoning itself* rather than content are analogical comparison and interleaved category discrimination. The two with the most robust child-sample evidence are retrieval practice and metacognitive strategy instruction.

**Insight 3 - Each battery's question families make measurably different cognitive demands, and the trainability of those components is not uniform**
- Group reasoning tests for gifted identification are organized into verbal, quantitative, and nonverbal/figural batteries, and each battery's question families make measurably different cognitive demands. Published cognitive analyses decompose these items into identifiable component processes: relational encoding, rule induction, goal management in working memory, and mental transformation of imagined objects. They identify which item features drive difficulty. However, the trainability of those components is not uniform. For example, spatial visualization has the strongest evidence of durable and transferable improvement while verbal analogy performance is entangled with vocabulary depth that grows slowly. Developmental limits on relational reasoning and working memory further constrain what can be taught at this age.

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

### Category 1: What practice and coaching measurably do to ability-test scores

- **Subcategory 1.1: The construct-level test**
  - **Source:** Reeve & Lam (2005); Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad (2007); Scharfen et al. (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Either retest gains are genuine change in *g*, in which case the field's assumption that *g* is stable must be revised, or they are not, in which case measurement invariance is violated and score-based inferences are undermined *(Reeve & Lam, 2005, *Intelligence* 33(5):535–549, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2005.05.003)* `T2`
      - Full measurement invariance existed across repeated administrations, extending to equality of the ability factor means and variances. *g*, the narrower group factors, and the tests' relation to those variance sources remain unchanged *(Reeve & Lam, 2005, as recorded in ERIC EJ723848)* `T2`
      - Lievens, Reeve and Heggestad examined psychometric bias due to retesting on cognitive ability tests in selection settings, the companion analysis in this line of work *(Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad, 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(6):1672–1682)* `T2`
      - Scharfen et al. state that if the latent construct were genuinely enhanced by retesting, "taking a test several times would not have an effect on the validity of the test," but "several studies contradict this view and imply that validity changes as a consequence of retesting" *(Scharfen et al., 2018, p. 45, citing Hausknecht et al., 2002; Lievens et al., 2005; te Nijenhuis et al., 2007)* `T2`
      - Scharfen et al. organize the causes of retest effects into three categories: genuine latent-construct enhancement, reduction of distorting and construct-irrelevant factors (anxiety, rule incomprehension, unfamiliarity), and development of test-specific strategies and skills. Only the first would constitute real ability change *(Scharfen et al., 2018, pp. 45–46, following Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad, 2007)* `T2`
      - Evidence is still mixed on how and why retesting changes the validity of a test *(Scharfen et al., 2018, p. 63)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Latent testing solves attribution and is unfavorable to the "real ability gain" reading. Reeve and Lam tested it at the level of the latent factor rather than the observed score and found the ability factor remains unchanged across administrations. The score moved while the construct did not, which means the gain was test-specific rather than construct-based. Two of the three mechanism categories (reduced anxiety/unfamiliarity, and test-specific strategy acquisition) are by definition construct-irrelevant, and the surviving literature is still "mixed" on whether validity changes. No source demonstrates that a retest or coaching gain represents an increase in the underlying reasoning ability, while at least one construct-level analysis affirmatively finds that it does not.
    - **Link to source:** [Reeve & Lam (2005) ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ723848), [Reeve & Lam on ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289605000565), [Scharfen et al. full text PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/2018-scharfen.pdf)

### Category 2: Score inflation and the boundary of legitimate preparation

- **Subcategory 2.1: The continuum of test-preparation practices and where the line falls**
  - **Source:** Mehrens & Kaminski (1989); Mehrens (1989, ERIC Digest ED314427)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Seven points on test-preparation continuum exist: (1) general instruction on district objectives without reference to what the standardized test measures, (2) teaching test-taking skills, (3) instruction on objectives that may have been determined by looking at the objectives a variety of standardized tests measure, (4) instruction based on objectives, skills and subskills that specifically match those on the test to be administered, (5) instruction on specifically matched objectives where the practice follows the same format as the test questions, (6) practice or instruction on a published parallel form of the same test, and (7) practice or instruction on the test itself *(Mehrens, 1989, ERIC Digest ED314427, pp. 2, summarizing Mehrens & Kaminski, 1989, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 8(1):14–22, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1989.tb00304.x)* `T2` `[continuum quoted from the digest, which restates the 1989 journal article]`
      - Point 1 is always ethical, Point 2 is typically considered ethical, and Points 6 and 7 are never ethical, so the crossover from legitimate to illegitimate practice lies somewhere between Points 3 and 5 *(Mehrens, 1989, ERIC Digest ED314427, p. 2)* `T2`
      - The line establishing legitimate from illegitimate teaching of the test must be drawn between Points 3 and 4 *(Mehrens, 1989, ERIC Digest ED314427, p. 3)* `T2`
      - The line moves because its location depends on a broad domain rather than the specific sampled objectives, instruction limited to the tested sample invalidates that inference *(Mehrens, 1989, ERIC Digest ED314427, pp. 2–3)* `T2`
      - Practice on a *parallel* form of the same test being unethical was contested at the time, with Bauernfeind (1989) advocating that practice *(secondary characterization in the same literature; Bauernfeind's original not retrieved)* `T2` `[secondhand]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Legitimacy is not a property of the activity but of whether the activity preserves the inference the score is used to support. The authors refuse to fix a single cut point and instead bracket it between Points 3 and 5, with Point 3/4 as the working boundary for ordinary uses. The clear verdicts are at the ends. Broad domain instruction and test-taking-skill instruction are fine, but parallel-form practice and operational-item practice are not. The unresolved zone includes format-matched practice on objectives inferred from the test blueprint.
    - **Link to source:** [Methods for Improving Standardized Test Scores: Fruitful, Fruitless, or Fraudulent?](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1989.tb00304.x) | [ERIC Digest ED314427 (full text, reproduces the seven points)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED314427.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.2: Audit-test logic and its limits**
  - **Source:** Koretz, McCaffrey & Hamilton (2001); Koretz & Hamilton (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Koretz, McCaffrey and Hamilton name the two instruments in a validation design the "focal test" (the high-stakes test being prepared for) and the "audit test" (an independent, lower-stakes measure of the same domain) *(Koretz, McCaffrey & Hamilton, 2001, CSE Technical Report, ERIC ED462410, p. 3)* `T2` `[technical report]`
      - They decompose gains on a focal test into three parts: (a) gains stemming from score inflation, such as teaching the specific items on the test, (b) meaningful gains that do not generalize to the audit test because the two tests were built for different inferences, and (c) meaningful gains that do generalize *(Koretz, McCaffrey & Hamilton, 2001, ERIC ED462410, pp. 3–4, Figure 2)* `T2`
      - The disparity between focal and audit trends is great enough to warrant an inference of appreciable inflation, but it is not possible to estimate the degree of inflation with any precision *(Koretz, McCaffrey & Hamilton, 2001, ERIC ED462410, p. 3)* `T2`
      - Divergence does not necessarily *overstate* inflation. If the audit test covers substantial material the focal test omits and students are learning more of it, the observed divergence may actually understate inflation *(Koretz, McCaffrey & Hamilton, 2001, ERIC ED462410, p. 4)* `T2`
      - Generalizability of gains is likely to be incomplete even when gains are meaningful, so the simple two-test trend comparison is useful only when the disparity is very large. Instead, they propose to discuss specific aspects of performance increased and comparing those to the inferences users draw *(Koretz & Hamilton, 2003, CSE Report 610, ERIC ED483028, abstract)* `T2` `[technical report; pilot study]`
      - Score inflation need not change correlations between the focal and audit tests. KIRIS–ACT mathematics correlations were reasonably stable across 1992–1995 (student level 0.54, 0.71, 0.70, 0.72; school level 0.69, 0.75, 0.58, 0.74) while the standardized mean trends diverged sharply *(Koretz, McCaffrey & Hamilton, 2001, ERIC ED462410, Table 1, adapted from Koretz & Barron, 1998)* `T2`
      - As of 2001, techniques for judging whether score gains are valid remained underdeveloped and describe "the inadequacy of traditional validation approaches" *(Koretz, McCaffrey & Hamilton, 2001, ERIC ED462410, abstract)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** To determine if a gain is real, administer an independent audit measure of the same construct and see how much of the focal gain reappears. Honesty about what that comparison can and cannot deliver can license a qualitative inference of appreciable inflation when the divergence is large, but it cannot quantify the inflation, because part of any non-generalizing gain (area B) is meaningful learning that the audit test simply was not built to detect. Two practical cautions follow. Correlations between the two tests can stay stable while means diverge, so corrrelation-based checks are not sufficient; and divergence can understate rather than overstate inflation. The more sensitive alternative the authors recommend, identifying which specific aspects of performance rose and matching them against the intended inference — was still at pilot stage in 2003.
    - **Link to source:** [Toward a Framework for Validating Gains under High-Stakes Conditions (ERIC ED462410, full text)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED462410.pdf) | [Teachers' Responses to High-Stakes Testing and the Validity of Gains (ERIC ED483028, full text)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED483028.pdf)

### Category 3: Is reasoning itself trainable, and how far does a trained gain travel?


- **Subcategory 3.1: The far-transfer null — working-memory training**
  - **Source:** Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016) [meta-analysis]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The meta-analysis pooled 87 publications and 145 experimental comparisons of working-memory training with pretest-posttest control-group designs. *(Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, 2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(4):512–534, DOI 10.1177/1745691616635612)* `T2`
      - Against active (treated) control groups, transfer to nonverbal ability was g = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.13], k = 67 — statistically indistinguishable from zero. *(same source, Table 1)* `T2`
      - Against untreated (no-contact) controls the same nonverbal-ability estimate rose to g = 0.20, 95% CI [0.11, 0.28], k = 53, and the difference between control types was significant, Q(1) = 6.09, p = .01. *(same source)* `T2`
      - Against active controls, verbal ability was g = 0.05 [−0.07, 0.17], word decoding g = 0.08 [−0.09, 0.24], and arithmetic g = 0.06 [−0.08, 0.19] — all non-significant. *(same source, Table 1)* `T2`
      - Reading comprehension against active controls reached g = 0.15 [0.03, 0.27] but fell to g = 0.08 after excluding comparisons with unexplained control-group declines. *(same source)* `T2`
      - Gains were large on the trained criterion tasks themselves (g = 0.80 [0.62, 0.97] vs. treated controls; g = 1.88 [1.33, 2.42] vs. untreated controls) and moderate on intermediate transfer — verbal working memory g = 0.31 [0.19, 0.42] and visuospatial working memory g = 0.28 [0.16, 0.40] vs. treated controls. *(same source)* `T2`
      - At follow-up roughly five months post-training, nonverbal ability against treated controls was g = −0.05 [−0.21, 0.11], k = 12. *(same source, Table 2)* `T2`
      - Meta-regression found that the magnitude of a study's working-memory gain did not predict its far-transfer gain (nonverbal ability β = −.01, p = .86, k = 26; verbal ability β = .11, p = .19, k = 29). *(same source)* `T2`
      - A sample-size split showed nonverbal-ability transfer of g = 0.01 in the 34 comparisons with at least 20 participants per group versus g = 0.26 in the 25 smaller comparisons. *(same source)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that repetitive computerized working-memory practice is unlikely to produce generalized cognitive benefits and recommended training skills that resemble the target outcome directly. *(same source)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest single statement of the far-transfer null, and its structure is the important part: effects are enormous on the trained task, moderate on tasks that share the trained format, and zero on ability measures once an active control absorbs expectancy and test-retest effects. The apparent transfer to nonverbal ability exists only against untreated controls and only in small samples, which is the signature of a design artefact rather than a cognitive gain. The mediation null is especially damaging to the underlying theory: if training worked by expanding working-memory capacity, studies that raized capacity more should have transferred more, and they did not. Notably, the authors' own remedy is not "abandon training" but "train the thing you want to improve" — which leaves open the question of what happens when the trained thing *is* reasoning.
    - **Link to source:** [Working Memory Training Does Not Improve Performance on Measures of Intelligence or Other Measures of "Far Transfer"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033/)
  - **Source:** Melby-Lervåg & Hulme (2013) [meta-analysis]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - This earlier meta-analysis covered 23 studies contributing 30 group comparisons of working-memory training in clinical and typically developing samples. *(Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013, *Developmental Psychology* 49(2):270–291, DOI 10.1037/a0028228)* `T2`
      - The authors reported reliable short-term improvements in working-memory skills but no convincing evidence of generalisation to nonverbal ability, verbal ability, attentional inhibition, word decoding, or arithmetic. *(same source)* `T2`
      - Verbal working-memory gains were not sustained at follow-up, while there was only limited evidence that visuospatial working-memory gains might persist. *(same source)* `T2`
      - The PubMed abstract does not report numeric effect sizes; No *g* or *d* values for this meta-analysis were retrieved. `[absence recorded]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The 2013 meta-analysis established the pattern that the 2016 paper then confirmed with three times the study pool. Near transfer yes, far transfer no, durability weak. Its inclusion of clinical populations matters because the strongest commercial claims for working-memory training were originally about ADHD remediation. The consistency across the two reviews, with independent and larger evidence bases, is what makes this null hard to dismiss as a sampling accident.
    - **Link to source:** [Is working memory training effective? A meta-analytic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22612437/)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The contrasting evidence — a year of schooling raises measured intelligence**
  - **Source:** Ritchie & Tucker-Drob (2018) [meta-analysis, quasi-experimental designs]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The meta-analysis comprized 142 effect sizes from 42 data sets analysed in 28 studies, with a total sample of 615,812 participants. *(Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, 2018, *Psychological Science* 29(8):1358–1369, DOI 10.1177/0956797618774253)* `T2`
      - Three quasi-experimental design families were meta-analyzed separately: controlling for prior intelligence, using compulsory-schooling policy changes as instrumental variables, and regression discontinuity on school-entry age cutoffs. `T2`
      - The control-prior-intelligence design gave 1.197 IQ points per additional year of education (SE = 0.203, p = 3.84×10⁻⁰⁹). `T2`
      - The policy-change design gave 2.056 IQ points per year (SE = 0.583, p = 4.23×10⁻⁰⁴). `T2`
      - The school-age-cutoff design gave 5.229 IQ points per year (SE = 0.530, p = 6.33×10⁻²³), significantly larger than both other designs. `T2`
      - All three designs gave 3.394 IQ points per year of education (SE = 0.503, p = 1.55×10⁻¹¹). `T2`
      - Age at the earlier test barely moderated the effect: 1.243 IQ points when the early test was at age 10 versus 1.099 at age 16. `T2`
      - Time lag between tests was a significant moderator (−0.031 IQ points per year, SE = 0.015, p = .033): 2.398 IQ points at a 5-year gap versus 0.317 at a 72-year gap, though lag was almost fully confounded with outcome-test age (r = .988). `T2`
      - In the control-prior-intelligence design, the effect remained statistically significant into the eighth and ninth decades of life. `T2`
      - Egger's test found no funnel-plot asymmetry for any design (control prior intelligence z = −1.378, p = .168; policy change z = −0.486, p = .627; school-age cutoff z = 0.941, p = .347), and p-curve indicated evidential value for all three. `T2`
      - PET-PEESE estimates under a counterfactual of no publication bias were 1.091 IQ points (control prior intelligence, SE = 0.117), 1.371 (policy change, SE = 0.142), and 4.244 (school-age cutoff, SE = 0.718). `T2`
      - Educational effects were found on all broad test categories measured, but the vast majority of studies used specific tests rather than a latent g factor, so they could not determine whether general ability or specific skills were improved. `T2`
      - The authors identified no studies testing whether the large school-age-cutoff effects persisted into adulthood, and advized those estimates be regarded with caution. *(same source)* `T2`
      - The authors are not aware of any study using rigorous quasi-experimental methods that tested whether educational effects on intelligence are additive across multiple years. *(same source)* `[absence recorded]` `T2`
      - The authors describe education as the most consistent, robust, and durable method yet identified for raising intelligence. *(same source)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Something in the ordinary experience of being schooled raises scores on intelligence tests by a genuinely large amount. The design spread from 1.2 to 5.2 points shows that school-age-cutoff studies measure children tested at the same age with differing exposure, which is the design most vulnerable to capturing recently taught content and the one with no adult follow-up. The durable estimate is nearer the low end. However, the authors explicitly cannot say whether g or peripheral skills improved, and they cannot say whether the effect is additive year over year. What it does establish is that measured reasoning ability is not fixed, and that the successful intervention is broad, sustained, and content-rich rather than a short drill.
    - **Link to source:** [How Much Does Education Improve Intelligence? A Meta-Analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797618774253)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Training the reasoning operation itself**
  - **Source:** Klauer & Phye (2008) [meta-analysis]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Training taught a single explicit procedural strategy, which was systematic comparison to detect regularities and irregularities across attributes and relations, rather than practicing test items. `T2`
      - Training raised fluid-intelligence test performance and improved academic learning of classroom subject matter. `T2`
      - Training effects on intelligence-test performance increased over time rather than decaying. `T2`
      - Positive problem-solving transfer to academic learning was greater than transfer to intelligence-test performance. `T2`
      - The authors state the results cannot be explained by placebo or test-coaching effects. `T2`
      - Children across a broad age range and a broad range of intellectual capacity benefit from such training. `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the closest thing in the literature to training the exact cognitive operation that a nonverbal reasoning test samples, and unlike working-memory training, it reports both transfer to fluid-intelligence measures and transfer to school learning. Two features of the result run directly against the working-memory pattern, though. The effect grew rather than faded over follow-up, and transfer to academic learning exceeded transfer to the test, the opposite of what coaching or teaching-to-the-test would produce. Children are given a general procedure for finding rules, not more mental storage.
    - **Link to source:** [Inductive Reasoning: A Training Approach](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ787070)
  - **Source:** Klauer (2014) [updated meta-analysis]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Inductive training substantially improves fluid intelligence, the effect does not diminish over the medium term, and classroom learning benefits at least as much as fluid intelligence. `T2`
      - 35 more experimental studies with 1,325 participants were added, extending the age coverage of the training programs across almost the entire lifespan. *`T2`
      - Across the full pooled body of 109 studies and 4,668 participants, effects were d = 0.60 on fluid intelligence, d = 0.68 on learning during classroom lessons, and d = 0.65 on further cognitive variables. `T2`
      - Klauer (1998) reported mean effect sizes on inductive subtests of intelligence measures of d = 0.60 for individual training, d = 0.73 for dyadic training, and d = 0.54 for small-group training. *(reported in secondary summaries of Klauer, 1998, *Learning and Instruction*)*
      - No independent meta-analysis of Klauer's inductive-reasoning training by authors unconnected to the program was identified during this research. `[absence recorded]`
      - No breakdown of these effect sizes by active-control versus no-treatment-control comparison was retrieved, which is the moderator that collapsed the working-memory literature. `[absence recorded]`
      - Independent verification against the 2008 record confirmed the study count, the participant count, the growth of effects over time, the ordering in which academic-learning transfer exceeds intelligence-test transfer, and the authors' claim that placebo and test-coaching effects do not account for the results; the numeric d values are reported only in the 2014 continuation and were not confirmed against the 2008 full text. `[verification note]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A pooled d of 0.60 on fluid intelligence across 109 studies and 4,668 participants is a far larger and better-evidenced far-transfer claim than anything in the working-memory literature, and the fact that classroom learning benefits more than the test (d = 0.68 versus 0.60) is the pattern from general reasoning strategy rather than from test familiarity. The claim that the effect does not fade over the medium term contrasts with the roughly five-month working-memory follow-up nulls. However, the two absences above are load-bearing. Without an active-control moderator analysis and without independent replication at meta-analytic scale, the estimate cannot be assumed immune to the design-quality attenuation that Sala & Gobet documented elsewhere.
    - **Link to source:** [Training des induktiven Denkens – Fortschreibung der Metaanalyse von 2008](https://doi.org/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123)
  - **Source:** Barkl, Porter & Ginns (2012) [independent single study]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - This independent Australian study tested individual and small-group Cognitive Training for Children against a no-treatment control in a primary-school cohort. *(Barkl, Porter & Ginns, 2012, *Psychology in the Schools* 49(9):828–842, DOI 10.1002/pits.21638)* `T2`
      - Students receiving individual training outperformed controls on measures of both inductive and deductive reasoning. `T2`
      - The small-group condition also outperformed the control on a measure of inductive reasoning three months after training. `T2`
      - Hypotheses regarding transfer to mathematics achievement were not supported. `T2`
      - The comparison condition was a no-treatment control, not an active control. `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the most useful independent test located. Reasoning gains replicated and persisted three months out, including to deductive reasoning that was not itself trained, but the academic transfer that Klauer's meta-analysis reported as the *larger* effect did not appear. That split between reasoning transfer and no mathematics transfer places Klauer-type training in the intermediate zone rather than in unambiguous far transfer. The no-treatment control is a real limitation given how much that design choice inflated estimates in the working-memory literature.
    - **Link to source:** [Cognitive training for children: Effects on inductive reasoning, deductive reasoning, and mathematics achievement in an Australian school setting](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.21638)

### Category 4: Instructional methods with evidence for durable, transferable learning

- **Subcategory 4.1: Interleaving and category discrimination**
  - **Source:** Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - 126 seventh-grade students worked identical practice problems over three months differing only in interleaved versus blocked order, and interleaving produced effect sizes of 0.42 on an unannounced test one day after a review session and 0.79 on an unannounced test 30 days after. *(Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic, 2015, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 107(3):900–908, DOI 10.1037/edu0000001)* `T2`
      - The authors motivate interleaving by noting that blocked assignments let students know the required strategy before reading the problem, whereas cumulative exams and later coursework require selecting the strategy from the problem itself. *(Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic, 2015, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 107(3):900–908, DOI 10.1037/edu0000001)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is one of the few interleaving studies with an actual child sample, real classroom, and month-long retention interval, which makes it the strongest single piece of child evidence for the technique. The interleaving advantage nearly doubled as the delay lengthened from one day to thirty, which is the signature of a durability effect rather than a performance effect. The transfer distance tested is moderate, novel problems of previously practiced types, requiring unaided strategy selection rather than structurally new problem categories.
    - **Link to source:** [Interleaved practice improves mathematics learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000001)

- **Subcategory 4.2: Analogical comparison and structure mapping**
  - **Source:** Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A random-effects meta-analysis of 57 experiments with 336 tests found that case comparison activities led to greater learning outcomes than sequential cases, single cases, nonanalogous cases, traditional instruction, and control, d = .50, 95% CI [.44, .56]. *(Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn, 2013, *Educational Psychologist* 48(2):87–113, DOI 10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
      - Of 15 candidate moderators, four reliably moderated effectiveness: the objective of the comparison, the presentation of a principle, the content, and the lag between comparison and testing. *(Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn, 2013, *Educational Psychologist* 48(2):87–113, DOI 10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
      - Greater learning was associated with asking learners to find similarities between cases, providing principles after rather than before the comparisons, using perceptual content, and testing learners immediately. *(Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn, 2013, *Educational Psychologist* 48(2):87–113, DOI 10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Prompt for similarities rather than differences, and withhold the general principle until after the learner has compared. However, lag was a reliable moderator in the direction that immediate testing showed larger effects, so unlike retrieval practice and interleaving, comparison's measured advantage is largest at short delays and its durability over long intervals is the weakest link in the evidence. The collected data mixes laboratory and classroom contexts across a wide age range and is not specifically a child-sample synthesis, so the pooled d should not be read as a child effect size.
    - **Link to source:** [Learning Through Case Comparisons: A Meta-Analytic Review](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)
  - **Source:** Gentner (1983)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Structure-mapping theory outlines rules for mapping knowledge on a base domain to a target domain, with the rules depending only on how knowledge is represented rather than the specific content of the domains. *(Gentner, 1983, *Cognitive Science* 7(2):155–170, DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3)* `T2`
      - The two mapping principles are that relations between objects rather than attributes of objects are mapped from base to target, and that which relations are mapped is determined by systematicity, defined by the existence of higher-order relations. *(Gentner, 1983, *Cognitive Science* 7(2):155–170, DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This study specifies what it means to represent a relation independently of the objects that instantiate it, which is exactly what a reasoning test samples. The unit of instruction should be the relation and its higher-order structure, and surface attributes of items are distractors rather than the content. Because the mapping rules are content-independent by construction, the theory predicts transfer across domains.
    - **Link to source:** [Structure-Mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3)

- **Subcategory 4.3: Metacognitive and strategy instruction in children**
  - **Source:** Dignath & Büttner (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Two separate meta-analyses covering 49 studies with primary school students and 35 studies with secondary school students, analyzing 357 effect sizes altogether, found an average effect size of 0.69 for self-regulated learning interventions. *(Dignath & Büttner, 2008, *Metacognition and Learning* 3(3):231–264, DOI 10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
      - At both school levels, effect sizes were higher when the training was conducted by researchers instead of by regular teachers. *(Dignath & Büttner, 2008, *Metacognition and Learning* 3(3):231–264, DOI 10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
      - Interventions attained higher effects in mathematics than in reading/writing or other subjects, and the theoretical background of the program and the type of strategy taught produced differential effects at the two school levels. *(Dignath & Büttner, 2008, *Metacognition and Learning* 3(3):231–264, DOI 10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This flagship child-population meta-analysis with an average effect of 0.69 is among the largest in Category 4 while resting entirely on primary and secondary school samples. It works better in mathematics than in verbal subjects, and it works better when researchers rather than ordinary teachers deliver it, which is a direct warning about fidelity loss at scale. Outcomes were measured across academic performance, strategy use, and motivation.
    - **Link to source:** [Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students. A meta-analysis on intervention studies at primary and secondary school level](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)
  - **Source:** de Boer, Donker, Kostons & van der Werf (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Moderator analyses showed that low-SES students benefited the most in the long term, and that interventions including the cognitive strategy 'rehearsal' had lower long-term effects than interventions without that component. *(de Boer, Donker, Kostons & van der Werf, 2018, *Educational Research Review* 24:98–115, DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2018.03.002)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
      - Subject domain, measurement instrument, duration, time between posttest and follow-up, and cooperation did not moderate the follow-up effect. *(de Boer, Donker, Kostons & van der Werf, 2018, *Educational Research Review* 24:98–115, DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2018.03.002)* `T2` `[meta-analysis]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The effect of metacognitive strategy instruction did not merely persist but grew slightly between posttest and follow-up, which demonstrates transferable capability rather than a temporarily boosted performance. The effect was insensitive to the length of the follow-up gap strengthens the durability reading. Rote-repetition strategies dilute the long-run benefit even when they help immediately.
    - **Link to source:** [Long-term effects of metacognitive strategy instruction on student academic performance: A meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.03.002)

### Category 5: What each reasoning battery actually demands, and which demands are teachable

- **Subcategory 5.1: Training spatial ability**
  - **Source:** Uttal, Meadow, Tipton, Hand, Alden, Warren & Newcombe (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 217 research studies, the average effect size (Hedges's g) for spatial training relative to control was 0.47 (SE = 0.04). *(Uttal et al., 2013, *Psychological Bulletin* 139(2):352-402, DOI 10.1037/a0028446)* `T2`
      - Training effects were stable and were not affected by delays between training and posttesting. *(Uttal et al., 2013, *Psychological Bulletin* 139(2):352-402, DOI 10.1037/a0028446)* `T2`
      - Training also transferred to other spatial tasks that were not directly trained. *(Uttal et al., 2013, *Psychological Bulletin* 139(2):352-402, DOI 10.1037/a0028446)* `T2`
      - Moderators examined included presence and type of control group, sex, age, and training type, organized around a two-dimensional typology of spatial skills crossing intrinsic versus extrinsic with static versus dynamic. *(Uttal et al., 2013, *Psychological Bulletin* 139(2):352-402, DOI 10.1037/a0028446)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Spatial skill moves about half a standard deviation with training, the gain does not decay over the delays studied, and it generalizes to untrained spatial tasks. Magnitude, durability, and transfer are precisely what is missing from the working-memory training data, and they are what make the nonverbal/figural battery the most defensibly trainable of the three.
    - **Link to source:** [The malleability of spatial skills: A meta-analysis of training studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028446)
  - **Source:** Gilligan, Thomas & Farran (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a sample of 250 eight-year-olds, spatial training targeting spatial scaling and mental rotation produced near transfer to the trained skills, intermediate transfer to untrained spatial skills, and far transfer to mathematics domains. *(Gilligan, Thomas & Farran, 2020, *Developmental Science* 23(4):e12909, DOI 10.1111/desc.12909)* `T2`
      - For most outcomes except geometry, implicit instruction (practice with feedback) was no less effective than explicit instruction (instructional videos). *(Gilligan, Thomas & Farran, 2020, *Developmental Science* 23(4):e12909, DOI 10.1111/desc.12909)* `T2`
      - No known prior studies had compared explicit versus implicit modes of spatial instruction. *(Gilligan, Thomas & Farran, 2020, *Developmental Science* 23(4):e12909, DOI 10.1111/desc.12909)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the cleanest child-age causal demonstration while still existing in the target age band. Well-designed practice with feedback matched video explanation on nearly everything, so the expensive component (explicit teaching) is not always necessary. Geometry was the exception where explicit instruction mattered, which suggests explicit teaching earns its keep where the outcome depends on named conventions rather than pure visualization.
    - **Link to source:** [First demonstration of effective spatial training for near and far transfer at 8 years](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12909)

- **Subcategory 5.2: Coachability across content types**
  - **Source:** Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A meta-analysis of 50 studies of practice effects on cognitive ability tests, covering 107 samples and 134,436 participants, found an adjusted overall effect size of .26. *(Hausknecht et al., 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(2):373-385, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373)* `T2`
      - Effects were larger when practice was accompanied by test coaching and when identical forms were used. *(Hausknecht et al., 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(2):373-385, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373)* `T2`
      - As many as 25-50% of applicants in organizational and educational settings are retested with measures of cognitive ability. *(Hausknecht et al., 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(2):373-385, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Mere retesting buys about 0.25 SD, and it is roughly half the size of the spatial-training effect. Coaching also adds to bare practice, and identical forms inflate gains, meaning a preparation program that reuses the same items is measuring memorization. The paper explicitly flags the unresolved question of what retest gains do to the validity of inferences drawn from scores.
    - **Link to source:** [Retesting in selection: A meta-analysis of coaching and practice effects](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373)
  - **Source:** Lohman, Korb & Lakin (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Among 1,198 elementary children (approximately 40% English-language learners) administered three nonverbal reasoning tests, ELL children scored .5 to .67 standard deviations lower than non-ELL children on all three. *(Lohman, Korb & Lakin, 2008, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 52(4):275-296, DOI 10.1177/0016986208321808)* `T2`
      - None of the nonverbal tests predicted achievement for ELL students very well. *(Lohman, Korb & Lakin, 2008, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 52(4):275-296, DOI 10.1177/0016986208321808)* `T2`
      - The standard error of measurement was twice as large for one of the nonverbal tests as for the other two. *(Lohman, Korb & Lakin, 2008, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 52(4):275-296, DOI 10.1177/0016986208321808)* `T2`
      - Norming errors caused 2/3 nonverbal tests to substantially overestimate the number of high-scoring children when national norms were used. *(Lohman, Korb & Lakin, 2008, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 52(4):275-296, DOI 10.1177/0016986208321808)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This rebuts the claim that a figural battery is knowledge-free or culture-fair. Children still differing in language exposure differed by half to two-thirds of a standard deviation on purely figural items. The includes everything else that accumulates with schooling and environment, such as familiarity with the format, with test-taking conventions, and with the abstract-pattern conventions the items assume. For a trainer, this is the clearest signal that figural items carry acquirable content, which is exactly why they are trainable.
    - **Link to source:** [Identifying Academically Gifted English-Language Learners Using Nonverbal Tests](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986208321808)

- **Subcategory 5.3: Strategy use on matrix items — constructive matching versus response elimination**
  - **Source:** Gonthier & Roulin (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Two strategies are distinguished on matrix items: constructive matching, in which the solver mentally constructs the answer before consulting the options, and response elimination, in which the solver discards candidate answers; constructive matching is effective but costly for complex problems, and response elimination is less accurate. *(Gonthier & Roulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(3):564-579, DOI 10.1037/xge0000660)* `T2`
      - Across two samples of 100 participants each on Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices, participants tended to shift to the less costly response elimination strategy as task difficulty increased. *(Gonthier & Roulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(3):564-579, DOI 10.1037/xge0000660)* `T2`
      - This variability in strategy use predicted 78% of item-to-item variance in accuracy. *(Gonthier & Roulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(3):564-579, DOI 10.1037/xge0000660)* `T2`
      - Working memory capacity moderated the shift, so only participants with both high working memory capacity and high need for cognition continued using constructive matching in the later, harder part of the task. *(Gonthier & Roulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(3):564-579, DOI 10.1037/xge0000660)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Which strategy the solver is using explains the overwhelming majority of item-to-item accuracy variation. Strategy is teachable behaviour, so this identifies the highest-leverage instructional target in the entire nonverbal battery. Persistence with constructive matching depends on both capacity and motivation, so instruction has to build stamina and disposition beyond teaching the method once.
    - **Link to source:** [Intraindividual strategy shifts in Raven's matrices](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000660)
  - **Source:** Gonthier & Thomassin (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Inducing all participants to use an effective strategy on Raven's matrices decreased the correlation between working memory capacity and performance (Experiment 1, N = 250). *(Gonthier & Thomassin, 2015, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 144(5):916-924, DOI 10.1037/xge0000101)* `T2`
      - A self-report strategy-use measure fully mediated the relationship between working memory capacity and performance on the matrices task (Experiment 2, N = 93). *(Gonthier & Thomassin, 2015, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 144(5):916-924, DOI 10.1037/xge0000101)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that individual differences in strategic behavior drive the predictive utility of working memory. *(Gonthier & Thomassin, 2015, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 144(5):916-924, DOI 10.1037/xge0000101)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Strategy was directly manipulated, and doing so shrank the contribution of a supposedly fixed capacity. Teaching the strategy partially substitutes for having the working memory capacity by leveling rather than merely lifting. This is the strongest available evidence that strategy instruction on figural matrices changes performance, and it should be paired with the caution that Raven's-style score gains may not reflect general reasoning gains.
    - **Link to source:** [Strategy use fully mediates the relationship between working memory capacity and performance on Raven's matrices](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000101)

### Category 6: Intellectual property, test security, professional ethics, and the equity boundary around test preparation

- **Subcategory 6.1: Copyright in test items**
  - **Source:** Educational Testing Service v. Katzman, 793 F.2d 533 (3d Cir. 1986)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The Third Circuit held that individual standardized-test questions are "original works of authorship" under 17 U.S.C. § 102(a) and are therefore copyrightable in themselves. *(Educational Testing Service v. Katzman, 793 F.2d 533, 3d Cir. 1986, ¶31, https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/793/793.F2d.533.85-5613.html)* `T2`
      - Registering a whole test form as a "compilation" does not narrow the copyright to the arrangement: the court held that "if the questions are copyrightable, then ETS' registrations of the tests as compilations covered the questions." *(same, ¶30)* `T2`
      - The court rejected the merger/idea-expression defence, holding that a publisher "cannot appropriate concepts such as rules of punctuation, analogies, vocabulary or other fundamental elements" but can copyright its own questions testing them, and that others have "ample latitude and opportunity to frame noninfringing questions testing the same subjects." *(same, ¶34)* `T2`
      - On the item-TYPE question the court stated directly: "We are not convinced that ETS' copyright in the text of a question precludes a coaching school from testing the same concept in the same order, as long as it does not use the same or substantially similar language." *(same, ¶52)* `T2`
      - Substituting different numeric values into an otherwize identical question was held to be copying, not independent authorship — the court's worked example shows a Review item differing from the source item only in the variable used. *(same, ¶¶43–45)* `T2`
      - Fair use was rejected on all four statutory factors, with the court holding that "the unique nature of secure tests means that any use is destructive of ETS' rights" and that the market-effect factor was "dispositively in favor of ETS." *(same, ¶¶64–67)* `T2`
      - The court nonetheless *narrowed* the injunction, excising the words "adapting" and "or any other information" because a ban on using "information obtained directly from" the tests "is broader than the scope of the copyright laws which ... do not protect ideas but only the expression of such ideas." *(same, ¶¶77, 88)* `T2`
      - Secure tests may be registered under a special reduced-deposit procedure (the court cites 37 C.F.R. § 202.20(b)(4) and (c)(2)(vi) as of 1985), under which the registrant deposits only a portion or description sufficient to identify the test rather than the test itself. *(same, ¶22; regulation text as quoted by the court — current CFR numbering should be re-verified)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Katzman is the closest thing to a controlling map of the boundary for anyone writing preparation material. It establishes that live items are individually copyrightable expression, that verbatim or near-verbatim reproduction (including trivial renumbering) infringes, and that fair use is a very weak defence for a commercial preparation product because reproduction destroys the very secrecy the work depends on. Crucially, the same opinion draws the other half of the line: the copyright reaches the *language* of the item, not the concept it tests or the order in which concepts appear, and the appellate court struck injunction language that would have barred use of mere "information" about the tests. Describing an item type, and authoring original items that test the same construct, is the space the court expressly left open.
    - **Link to source:** [Educational Testing Service v. Katzman, 793 F.2d 533 (3d Cir. 1986)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/793/793.F2d.533.85-5613.html)

- **Subcategory 6.2: What the AERA/APA/NCME Standards (2014) actually say about preparation and security**
  - **Source:** American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association & National Council on Measurement in Education (2014), *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The Standards state the item-authorship rule for third-party preparation publishers directly: "Outside groups that develop items for test preparation should base those items on publicly disclosed information and not on information that has been inappropriately shared by test takers." *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing, Ch. 8 "The Rights and Responsibilities of Test Takers," Background, p. 132)* `T2`
      - Standard 12.7 requires that "test users should take steps to prevent test preparation activities and distribution of materials to students that may adversely affect the validity of test score inferences." *(same, Standard 12.7, p. 197)* `T2`
      - The comment to Standard 12.7 supplies the operative test for judging preparation material: appropriateness "can be evaluated, for example, by determining the extent to which they reflect the specific test items and by considering the extent to which test scores may be artificially raized as a result, without increasing students' level of genuine achievement," and it names "excessive teaching of items that are equivalent to those on the test" as the paradigm abuse. *(same, comment to Standard 12.7, p. 197)* `T2`
      - Standard 12.8's comment endorses the *legitimate* half of preparation, saying students, parents and staff "should be informed of the domains on which the students will be tested, the nature of the item types, and the criteria for determining mastery," and that evidence should be provided "that students have had the opportunity to become familiar with the mode of administration and item formats used in testing." *(same, comment to Standard 12.8, p. 197)* `T2`
      - Standard 6.5 requires that "test takers should be provided appropriate instructions, practice, and other support necessary to reduce construct-irrelevant variance." *(same, Standard 6.5, p. 116)* `T2`
      - Standard 6.7 states that "test users have the responsibility of protecting the security of test materials at all times," extending to access "before the test administration, and afterwards as well, if any part of the test will be reused at a later time." *(same, Standard 6.7 and comment, p. 117)* `T2`
      - Standard 9.21 states that "test users have the responsibility to protect the security of tests, including that of previous editions," grounding this in "validity of inferences drawn, protection of intellectual property rights, and the costs associated with developing tests." *(same, Standard 9.21 and comment, p. 147)* `T2`
      - Standard 1.7 imposes a burden on anyone claiming coaching-resistance: "If test performance, or a decision made therefrom, is claimed to be essentially unaffected by practice and coaching, then the propensity for test performance to change with these forms of instruction should be documented," and its comment says interpretive materials should "provide practical guidance about the value of test preparation activities, including coaching." *(same, Standard 1.7 and comment, p. 24)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Standards draw a line that maps closely onto the copyright line but is stated in validity terms rather than property terms. Familiarising a test taker with item formats, response modes, directions and the domains assessed is not merely permitted — Standards 6.5 and 12.8 affirmatively require it, because unfamiliarity is construct-irrelevant variance that damages validity. What is prohibited is preparation that "reflect[s] the specific test items" closely enough to raize scores "without increasing students' level of genuine achievement," and item development for preparation products is expressly required to rest on publicly disclosed information only. Standard 1.7 additionally puts an evidentiary burden on the *publisher* side of any claim that a test is coaching-proof.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014)](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)
