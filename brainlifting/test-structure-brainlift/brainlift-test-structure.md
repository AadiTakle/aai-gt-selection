## Purpose

### Purpose

How the **structure** of a gifted/IQ test — its adaptivity, retake policy, item ordering and presentation, session design, and the cognitive-load / learning-science of the test *experience* — shapes what the test actually measures. The goal is an original, defensible position on how to structure a K–8 cognitive screen so the delivered experience elicits genuine ability rather than test-taking artifacts. It argues from external evidence and general principles only, and is written to be read with no knowledge of any specific product or codebase. *(DOK 1–2 are AI-assisted + citation-verified; DOK 3–4 are the author's to own. Companion: `source-register-annotated.md`.)*

### In Scope

The structure / sequencing / delivered-experience design of a K–8 automated, adaptive, text-only cognitive screen, and its learning-science grounding:

- Adaptive **structure & routing** (CAT vs MST vs hybrid; item-selection, stopping rules, content balancing, exposure control).
- **Item ordering, position & context effects** (easy→hard vs random vs adaptive; warm-up placement; within-session practice/fatigue).
- **Retakes / repeated administration** (test–retest reliability, practice effects, reliable change, occasions, spacing, alternate/rotating forms, retest policy for a decision).
- **Presentation & interaction** (text-only delivery; one-at-a-time vs review; response formats; device/touch; instructions/self-teaching demos + warm-ups).
- **Cognitive load & working memory** in test design (intrinsic/extraneous/germane load; WM limits by age; how load interacts with the above).
- **Session design & developmental fit** (length, breaks, fatigue, attention by band K-1/2-3/4-5/6-8).
- **Motivation, effort & engagement structure** (test anxiety, flow, rapid-guessing/effort detection, feedback timing/type, reward crowding-out).
- **Fairness & access in structure**, and **auditability/reproducibility of an adaptive structure** (locked, deterministic routing/stopping/ordering).

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

- **Spiky POV 1 - A gifted screener should measure two different constructs in two different test structures.**
   - **Elaboration:** There are two things our test should understand and measure: "What can this applicant already do?" (standing) and "How fast can they learn under difficulty" (learning-rate/timeback readiness). Unfortunately, these two measurements require different test structures to be adequately measured (Insight 1). Standing is most cleanly read when questions are asked below a student's skill/knowledge limit, while learning rate only becomes truly observable above that limit (5.8, GA 4.8). Therefore, a single test that tries to measure both will not be able to measure either one accurately enough for the high-stakes gate that we are measuring for. Instead, a MST that covers the standing in a first stage and then learning-rate in a second difficulty-scaled stage will provide more accurate results. The accuracy stage will be adaptive to identify where the student's limits are in each of the core reasoning areas we are looking at as well as their ability to accurately answer questions below that limit (Insight 2). Then, the learning-rate stage will use that information to scale the difficulty of its test past the student's limit so they actually struggle to answer the proposed questions. Then, through this struggle, students will demonstrate that they are able to understand their mistakes and correct/improve from them (Insight 3). This MST design also needs to account for fatigue and effort loss over the course of the test, so incorporating a break between phases and tracking real time response time data to account for engagement dropoff will make the test more effective (Insight 6). Supporting retakes, however, is a large departure from this core structure and is best left as a future extension, since it does not have a large impact on the outcome or accuracy of the exam itself. Instead, we should develop our structure to allow retakes to be folded in later. This primarily includes sizing and calibrating the item bank for rotation from the start. Then, retakes can be added as an extension rather than a complete redesign (Insight 7).
- **Spiky POV 2 - The student should only be "struggling" during the learning-rate stage, since any difficulty during the accuracy stage will degrade results.**
   - **Elaboration:** Desirable difficulties are only evidence-backed for the durable learning measured in the learning-rate stage (5.8, Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). In the accuracy stage, desirable difficulty leads to unreliable and unpredictable performance, especially without repetition/immediate feedback. It additionally destroys any diagnostics for those difficult questions since the student is no longer engaging with the question in a meaningful and thoughtful process, often leading to frustration and rapid-guessing and unnecessary cognitive load (Insight 2, 6.2). Thus, it should be avoided during this stage of the test.
- **Spiky POV 3 - Effort and engagement telemetry data should be used as validations and filters for the assessment's determined score/decision. They should not be used to gate/grade the child.**
   - **Elaboration:** Though timeback's model is most effective with kids demonstrating high engagement and effort, these signals are not correlated with the more important accuracy and learning-rate measurements. Effort is near-zero correlated with ability (7.4) and the telemetry data can encode device/motor/timeback familiarity variance worst for young applicants (4.1, 4.4, 6.7, GA 6.6/7.5). Instead, these metrics should be used to detect and prevent against biases by detecting disengagement, filtering rapid-guessing, and keeping effort variance out of the estimate (7.4, 7.5).
- **Spiky POV 4 - the accuracy and learning-rate metrics that are being gated on must be auditable. This is how the test's results can be verified and validated.**
   - **Elaboration:** Any metric we actually gate the admission decision on has to be auditable because that is how a borderline or rejected family's result can be reconstructed, defended, and shown to be rule-governed instead of arbitrary (Category 9; 8.2). However, the test needing to be auditable cannot choose the construct for us: accuracy is easy to make reproducible and fair, so it is tempting to gate on accuracy alone. However, that inverts our priorities since it optimizes defensibility over validity and turns our test back into a conventional ability screen, under-representing the learning-rate/timeback-readiness we actually care about (construct underrepresentation) (7.3, Insight 5). Therefore, the real work is bringing the learning-rate metric up to the same bar as accuracy and have it be deterministic, replayable, fair at the cut, and shown to add real predictive value against an outcome we trust (like later achievement growth) so it can legitimately help gate (Category 9, 8.2, GA 4.8). Learning-rate measurement is still unproven for high-stakes decisions (GA 4.8), so until it clears that bar, we should use a documented accuracy floor alongside a logged, provisional learning-rate signal on an explicit, timeboxed validation plan.

## Experts

> Chosen for productive tension — the disagreements between these camps are where the DOK 3 insights come from.

- **Expert 1 — Wim J. van der Linden and the adaptive-testing psychometricians (with David Weiss; Yan, von Davier & Lewis)**
   - **Who:** Psychometricians who formalized computerized adaptive testing (CAT), multistage testing (MST), item-selection, shadow-test content balancing, and exposure control.
   - **Focus:** Tuned adaptive routing measures more precisely with fewer items; the structure (selection rule, stopping rule, exposure control) is engineered, not incidental.
   - **Why Follow:** They define what an adaptive *structure* can and cannot do, and the machinery a classification screen would use. Pole: adaptivity + tuned routing is superior.
   - **Where:** van der Linden & Glas, *Elements of Adaptive Testing*; Yan, von Davier & Lewis, *Computerized Multistage Testing*.
- **Expert 2 — John Sweller and Cognitive Load Theory (with Paas, van Merriënboer)**
   - **Who:** Originators of Cognitive Load Theory — intrinsic/extraneous/germane load, element interactivity, and the split-attention, redundancy, and modality effects.
   - **Focus:** Performance degrades when extraneous load consumes the working memory needed for the target reasoning.
   - **Why Follow:** The core lens for presentation, session, and ordering design. Pole: minimize extraneous load.
   - **Where:** Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (1998/2019).
- **Expert 3 — Robert & Elizabeth Bjork (desirable difficulties)**
   - **Who:** Memory researchers behind "desirable difficulties" — conditions that slow acquisition but improve retention and transfer.
   - **Focus:** Some difficulty *improves* learning outcomes.
   - **Why Follow:** The counter-pole to CLT, and the sharpest tension in a *measurement* (not learning) context — does "desirable difficulty" even apply when you are measuring, not teaching?
   - **Where:** Bjork & Bjork (2011); Soderstrom & Bjork (2015).
- **Expert 4 — Steven Wise (rapid-guessing / response-time effort)**
   - **Who:** Measurement researcher on test-taking effort, rapid-guessing, and effort-moderated scoring.
   - **Focus:** Disengaged rapid guesses carry no signal and must be detected and filtered; effort is a precondition for valid measurement.
   - **Why Follow:** Grounds the engagement/effort structure and the response-time signals (with van der Linden's RT models).
   - **Where:** Wise & Kong (2005); Wise & DeMars (2006).
- **Expert 5 — Retest / practice-effect meta-analysts (Hausknecht et al.; Scharfen et al.; Lievens)**
   - **Who:** Researchers quantifying test–retest gains, practice effects by form type, and retest policy in high-stakes selection.
   - **Focus:** Repeated testing produces score gains that vary by form and interval and can be subgroup-differential.
   - **Why Follow:** The evidence base for a retake policy — occasions, spacing, alternate forms, reliable change.
   - **Where:** Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018); Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007).
- **Expert 6 — Item-position & context-effect researchers (Debeer & Janssen; Weirich et al.; Kingston & Dorans)**
   - **Who:** Psychometricians modeling how an item's serial position and surrounding context shift its difficulty and DIF.
   - **Focus:** Position is a construct-irrelevant factor that ordering choices can inject.
   - **Why Follow:** Grounds the item-ordering / warm-up design and the "adaptivity confounds position with difficulty" problem.
   - **Where:** Debeer & Janssen (2013); Weirich, Hecht, Penk, Roppelt & Böhme (2017).
- **Expert 7 — Test-anxiety & motivation researchers (von der Embse et al.; Deci, Koestner & Ryan)**
   - **Who:** Researchers on K–8 test anxiety and on extrinsic rewards crowding out intrinsic motivation.
   - **Focus:** Anxiety adds construct-irrelevant variance; tangible/performance rewards can distort effort, worse in children.
   - **Why Follow:** Grounds the anxiety-reducing, reward-free engagement structure.
   - **Where:** von der Embse et al. (2018); Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999).
- **Expert 8 — The testing Standards & Universal Design for Assessment (AERA/APA/NCME; Thompson, Johnstone & Thurlow)**
   - **Who:** The joint *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* and the Universal Design for Assessment framework.
   - **Focus:** Fairness, accessibility, comparability, and the construct-relevant vs construct-irrelevant line.
   - **Why Follow:** The rulebook for fairness/auditability of structure and the counter-pole to a text-only reading barrier.
   - **Where:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014) *Standards*; Thompson, Johnstone & Thurlow (2002), NCEO.

## DOK 3: Insights

- **Insight 1 - A student's standing and their learning rate are useful at distinctly different levels of understanding and difficulty.** A test's one-shot accuracy score will provide information about a student's standing and baseline capability, but will not tell us about their capability to learn from their mistakes, understand them, and improve. Learning rate encapsulates that concept by measuring a latent change through "improvement over exposure" (5.8; dynamic/test-teach-retest, GA 4.8). Additionally, applying the learning science concept of desirable difficulty has shown to raise later learning and growth but reduces short-term performance (designed for failure-first) (5.8). Since adaptable test designs introduce an element of desirable difficulty as the student's boundary is reached, it is clear that both standing and learning rate cannot and should not be measured together during the test. Standing is more useful for identifying the student's limits within the reasoning areas and learning rate is more suitable for desirable difficulty testing where the goal is to see how the student improves over multiple exposures (7.7). This insight immediately lends itself to an MST test design (1.1 MST routing). 

- **Insight 2 - When identifying a student's reasoning limits, don't ramp up to their limits, bracket around them.** The immediate instinct to measure a student's limits by pushing difficulty until they plateau does eventually give a secure answer, but it causes students to experience test anxiety as questions approach and exceed their ability limits, leading to rapid-guessing and reduced effort (6.2, 7.6, 7.1, 2.1). Instead, we should close in on the student's ability from both sides, which is best done by picking each question to shrink the remaining uncertainty as much as possible. This is what Bayesian criteria like minimum expected posterior variance (MEPV) are built for (1.6). Maximum-information selection does nearly the same thing more cheaply, and the comparisons find little separating them past the first several items (1.7, 1.8). Thus, what matters here is closing in from both sides, not the specific criterion used. Two things about the underlying math change how this should be built. The most informative question is the one sitting at the student's current estimate, not one at the edge of what they can do, since a question far from the student carries almost no information and a wide range will stay wide (1.6). Additionally since we are primarily using five-option questions and a guessing floor, the information-optimal question is one the student gets right about 65% of the time rather than 50% (1.10). Therefore, the question we want for measurement reasons is already easier than the naive version and much of the conflict between measuring well and causing test anxiety closes on its own. SPRT belongs in this design but not in this step since it is a stopping rule that tells us when the decision is secure, not which question to ask next (1.3). The bracketing is a separate choice about selection that happens to pair well with it.


- **Insight 2b - Aiming our questions at the gifted cut instead of at the student would decide faster, but it costs us the per-area estimate the learning-rate stage runs on.** Selecting questions at the decision point rather than at the student's own estimate reaches a secure classification in fewer questions, and the advantage is largest exactly where a selective cut sits, which is where ours sits (1.9). That makes it genuinely tempting. The problem is what it gives up. Aiming at the cut produces an ability estimate that is only precise near the cut and close to useless away from it (1.9), and our standing stage is not just deciding pass or fail, it has to locate the student's limits in each reasoning area so the learning-rate stage can scale its difficulty past them (Insight 1, SPOV 1). A number that is only trustworthy near the cut cannot do that job. Cut-aimed selection also serves every student the same opening questions since it is barely adapting to the person at all (1.9), which throws away the exposure protection that student-matched selection gives us for free and would burn our item bank faster. Therefore, we should aim at the student and accept the extra questions, and treat cut-aimed selection as the right tool for a different test than the one we are building.

- **Insight 3 - Despite being harmful for a student standing focused test, desirable difficulty will improve our ability to measure learning-rate once the student's limits are identified.** Because desirable difficulty reduces performance in the short term (5.8, Soderstrom & Bjork 2015), a standing measure cannot use it since it will worsen the accuracy of its estimates. In contrast, desirable difficulty is shown to improve durable learning, which makes it perfect for a learning-rate focused test and measurement (5.8, Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). Such a test can measure improvement-under-difficulty by providing desirable difficulty to identify how well the student adapts and improves over multiple iterations. Thus, load and difficulty is minimized during the standing test and the improvement is mandated as part of the learning-rate test (5.2, 5.6).   

- **Insight 4 - Effort telemetry data is primarily useful for score reinforcement, but cannot determine admission on its own.** Response-time-based effort is near-zero correlated with ability (7.4, Wise & Kong 2005), so though it is useful for identifying engagement, it is not useful for identifying giftedness. What has support is using the data for effort-moderated scoring/filtering that's supplementary to the methods of determining admission (7.4, 7.5). Using it as a primary metric is ill-advised since it doesn't validate gifted kids and encodes device/motor/timeback familiarity variance the worst for younger kids (GA 6.6/7.5; 4.1, 4.4, 6.7). Thus, these metrics should be kept as secondary, supporting telemetry metrics that contribute as a final filter at the admissions cut but not as a metric that determines final admission.

- **Insight 5 - Every signal that carries a high-stakes decision must be auditable and traceable. This includes the accuracy, telemetry, and learning-rate signals.** Since we are attempting to understand and quantify how fast a child is able to learn and adapt, we need to gate on multiple signals to ensure accuracy on this unconventional metric and prove validity. Currently, the two formal threats to validity are construct-irrelevant variance and construct underrepresentation (AERA/APA/NCME 2014, 7.3). Gating on accuracy provides a great floor, finding the initial group of applicants that has the skills needed to excel, but provides no information about their learning rate. For that, we would need to gate on a learning-rate signal. Unfortunately, learning-rate signals are difficult to audit as an IRT accuracy classification is only lockable, seedable, replayable, and DIF-auditable at an accuracy cut (Category 9, 8.2), making it the wrong construct for these signals. Additionally, learning-rate signals can carry construct-irrelevant device/familiarity variance (GA 6.6, 4.4). Both of these are serious threats to the validity of learning-rate signals, so a good learning-rate gate will need to engineer an auditable and traceable learning-rate measure up to the same decision-grade bar as the IRT accuracy classification (deterministic/replayable, fair at the cut, and shown to add incremental validity against an external outcome such as later achievement growth).

- **Insight 6 - Because the learning-rate stage needs both a student's known limits and full effort to be an effective test, our structure needs to ensure students provide full effort on the test.** Effort and data quality decline with position and accumulated load (2.2, 6.2). Additionally, the learning-rate stage is required to run after the accuracy/standing stage since it needs the student's difficulty limits to provide effective results, placing it directly where a student is likely to provide the least amount of effort. Thus the assessment will need to be designed to account for this. With attention plateauing around age 10 (6.4) and a break between tests recovering more than a hour's worth of fatigue, a two-stage design will only work if the segments are short, and a break is provided between segments, and response time effort data is collected in real time to identify when engagement reduces (6.2, 6.3). This metric will perform better than the generic fixed clock for different ages (6.5). Because of this, effort-protection has become a core element of the test's design, as it is required to ensure that the test's results are accurate and valid.

- **Insight 7 - A retake policy will force the standing stage onto a large, rotating item bank and force us to score a retake as a change in classification.** Studies have shown that repeated testing reliably raises scores slightly, but doesn't result in a real ability gain (d ~ .26, 3.2), with the gain doubling if an identical test form is used (3.3). If allowing for retakes, quizzes must expand the question banks and rotate different questions, meaning that the design decision is apparent from the first administration of the test (Cat 1, Cat 8). Students who retake tests over multiple years will naturally improve their scores in a manufactured manner, producing false-positives above the cut (3.7). Seeing as these individuals are close to the cutoff (3.9), a rudimentary structure of taking the best of their attempts invalidates the results. The solution is to compare the consistency between the years with a Reliable Change Index, not banking on the student's high test score (3.8, 3.10). Retakes affect both stages of the test: the standing stage must take into account form-recall and regression risk and re-administration must factor in the memory of "learn-under-struggle" tasks. A learning-rate retake needs new yet parallel questions, solidifying the need for a rotating set of questions from an immense bank.

### Overall Summary

This tree maps how the *structure* of a K–8 adaptive cognitive screen shapes what it measures, across nine categories: adaptive routing, item ordering, retakes, presentation, cognitive load, session design, motivation and effort, fairness, and auditability. The recurring finding is that most structural levers — adaptivity, ordering, retakes, exposure control, and a text-only reading gate — improve one property (efficiency, security, engagement, or reproducibility) at a measurable cost to another (comparability, fairness, effort, or construct validity), and almost all of the evidence comes from adult or non-gifted-tail settings, so the magnitudes are design mechanisms to reason from, not calibrated constants for a top-percentile K–8 cut.

### Category 1: Adaptive structure & routing

- **Subcategory 1.1: Three ways to build an adaptive test — question by question, block by block, or a controlled hybrid**
  - **Source:** van der Linden & Glas (2010); Weiss (1982); Yan, von Davier & Lewis (2014); Luecht & Nungester (1998)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Computer-adaptive testing (CAT)**, in its item-level form, picks and scores one question at a time: after every answer it re-estimates the child's ability and chooses the next question against that running estimate. It is a **mature** method, but it only works on top of a large bank of questions whose difficulty has already been measured on a shared scale — the **item response theory (IRT)** calibration that places children and questions on one common yardstick — plus controls that stop any question being over-used and leaking. `T2` **Conflict of Interest:** Weiss and Thompson are principals of commercial computer-adaptive-testing vendors. The measurement-maturity claim itself is owned by **GA 4.1**.
      - **Multistage testing (MST)** does the same job in blocks. It pre-assembles fixed **modules** — short, fixed sets of questions — into **panels**, and routes a child to an easier or harder next module after each block rather than after each individual question. It is a **mature** method treated as its own operating model. `T2` — the maturity claim is owned by **GA 4.2**.
      - **Computer-Adaptive Sequential Testing (CAST)** is a structured version of multistage testing that "incorporates both adaptive testing methods with automated test assembly to allow test developers to maintain a greater degree of control over the production, quality assurance, and administration" of computerized tests, and "retains much of the efficiency of traditional CAT" while being adaptable to computer **mastery** testing (CMT) — a test whose output is a pass/fail call rather than a score. `T1` The demonstration is on a medical-licensure example, so the evidence comes from an adjacent setting rather than a school one.
      - MST and CAST families differ in practice by **number and length of stages/testlets** — a testlet being a small bundle of questions administered together — and by the statistical and qualitative targets set for those stages and testlets. Named variants include Computerized Mastery Testing (CMT), CAST, Multiple-Form Structures (MFS), and Bundled MST (BMAT). *(NCME Instructional Module on Multistage Testing, Hendrickson; citing Lewis & Sheehan 1990, Luecht & Nungester 1998, Armstrong et al. 2004, Luecht 2003.)* `T4` This is a practitioner-facing instructional module rather than a primary study.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Adaptivity is not one switch but a family of separately configurable choices: the architecture covered here, the question-selection rule (1.2), the stopping rule (1.3), content balancing (1.4), and exposure control (1.5). Each can be set independently of the others. On architecture specifically, item-level CAT, multistage testing, and the hybrid CAST are three mature options that trade per-question efficiency against the ability to inspect and sign off on the assembled test before anyone sits it.

- **Subcategory 1.2: How the test decides which question to ask next**
  - **Source:** Lord (1980); Chang & Ying (1996, 1999); van der Linden (1998); Chang, Qian & Ying (2001)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The default rule in adaptive testing is **maximum Fisher information (MFI)**: at each step, pick the question carrying the most "information" at the child's current ability estimate, written **θ** ("theta", the child's position on the shared IRT scale). Information is simply precision — the more a question adds at a given ability level, the narrower the error bar on the score there. The canonical statement is Lord's 1980 book *Applications of Item Response Theory to Practical Testing Problems*. `T2` The specific page remains unverified; it is a canonical book that was not directly resolved this session.
      - Maximum Fisher information "could be much less efficient than assumed if the estimators are not close to the true θ, especially at early stages … when the test length … is too short to provide an accurate estimate." The proposed alternative is a **Kullback–Leibler (global) information** criterion: instead of measuring a question's precision at the single point where the child is currently estimated to sit, it averages that question's power to tell children apart across a whole range of plausible ability values, using a moving average of that quantity. It reduced bias and mean squared error — the average squared distance between the estimated and the true ability — when the test was short or early (roughly m < 30). `T1`
      - **Bayesian item-selection criteria** built on the *true* posterior are proposed, including maximum predicted posterior expected information, and are recommended for short adaptive tests where the large-sample approximation behind Fisher information is least safe. The "posterior" here is the full updated probability distribution over the child's ability given every answer so far, rather than Owen's 1975 shortcut of standing in a normal curve for it. `T1` **Provenance note:** an earlier version of this entry carried a verbatim quotation ("…is the criterion elect for application in short adaptive tests") that could not be located in the paper's full text. The substance is restated as a paraphrase rather than dropped, and the wording should be re-checked against the article before it is quoted anywhere.
      - **a-stratification** splits the bank into layers by the **discrimination parameter (a)** — how sharply a question separates children just above from children just below its difficulty — and administers **low-a items in early stages and high-a items in later stages**, holding the sharpest questions back until the ability estimate is good enough to aim them properly. In simulation it reduced the skewness of item-exposure distributions "while efficiency was maintained in trait level estimation" and "achieved a lower average exposure rate than CATs based on Bayesian or information-based item selection and the Sympson–Hetter method." `T1`
      - A refinement, **a-stratification with b-blocking**, additionally requires the **difficulty parameter (b)** values to be spread evenly across the a-strata, so every layer covers the full difficulty range; it "improved control of item exposure rates and reduced mean squared errors" on a retired GRE bank. `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Picking the question that carries the most Fisher information at the child's current ability estimate is the default rule, but it is unreliable early in the test and whenever the estimate is far from the truth. That is what motivates the alternatives: Kullback–Leibler/global information, which averages discriminating power over a range instead of a point; true-posterior Bayesian criteria, which are best for extreme ability and short tests; and a-stratification, which saves the sharpest questions until the estimate can aim them.

- **Subcategory 1.3: When the test should stop — and why a pass/fail decision stops much sooner than a score**
  - **Source:** Wald (1947); Eggen (1999); Eggen & Straetmans (2000); Spray & Reckase (1996); Kingsbury & Weiss (1983); Thompson (2009); Bartroff, Finkelman & Lai (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Adaptive tests generally stop in one of two ways: **fixed-length**, meaning stop after a set number of questions, or **fixed-precision / variable-length**, meaning keep going until the standard error of θ — the width of the error bar on the ability estimate — falls below a target. These are treated as the two standard stopping families. `T2` **Conflict of Interest:** Thompson is vendor-affiliated.
      - **Wald's (1947) Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT)**, from his book *Sequential Analysis* and applied to testing by Eggen (1999) below, can be run as an adaptive test that **classifies** children into categories instead of scoring them. It pits two hypotheses against each other around a cut score θ₀ — H₀: θ ≤ θ₀−δ versus H₁: θ ≥ θ₀+δ — leaving an **indifference region** of half-width δ straddling the cut, inside which either call is acceptable. It stops as soon as the running tally of evidence (the log-likelihood ratio, a measure of how much more consistent the answers are with one hypothesis than the other) crosses the A/B boundaries set by the error rates you are willing to tolerate. `T2`
      - For classifying into categories, **item selection using Kullback–Leibler information "performed better than or as well as" Fisher-information-based selection** with the SPRT, in 2- and 3-category simulations. `T1`
      - An adaptive test that sorts children into **three categories** — using either the SPRT hypothesis test or straight ability estimation — yielded "a reduction of at least 22% in the mean number of items … compared to an existing paper-and-pencil placement test," and "imposing constraints on the MI selection strategy does not negatively affect the quality," MI being maximum-information selection. `T1` The cuts studied were placement and pass-level cuts, so the evidence comes from an adjacent setting rather than a top-percentile gifted cut.
      - When matched on classification error rates, the **SPRT required fewer test items than a sequential Bayes procedure** to achieve the same classification accuracy. `T1` The setting is adjacent — licensure, certification and placement rather than gifted selection.
      - In a **confidence-interval / adaptive-mastery** stopping rule, an error band is drawn around θ and compared with the cut, and the test stops once that band sits clearly on one side. A comparison found the **SPRT produced greater average test-length reductions, but adaptive mastery testing (AMT, the confidence-interval approach) "resulted both in more valid mastery decisions and in more balanced error rates," giving "the best combination of test length and validity."** The finding is corroborated in Kingsbury & Weiss, 1980, Research Report 80-4, University of Minnesota. `T2` **Conflict of Interest:** Weiss is vendor-affiliated. The 1983 chapter's page range remains unverified, as it was not directly resolved; the 1980 report was verified.
      - A review of **item selection in computerized classification testing (CCT)** — adaptive testing whose output is a category rather than a score — concludes there is "no conclusive evidence on the substantial superiority of a single method" (several methods "assess items very similarly … and will usually select the same item"); crucially, "the efficiency of item selection approaches depend on the termination criteria," and "item selection at the cut score, which seems conceptually appropriate for CCT, is not always the most efficient option." `T1` **Conflict of Interest:** the author is affiliated with a CAT/CCT vendor (Assessment Systems Corporation).
      - **Sequential generalized likelihood ratio (GLR)** tests extend asymptotic-optimality theory to "sequentially generated experiments … of particular interest in computerized adaptive testing," and the resulting adaptive mastery tests are "asymptotically optimal and … provide substantial improvements over currently used sequential and fixed length tests." The paper flags that the error rates of the standard SPRT with adaptively selected items "are often substantially inflated" — adaptive selection means the questions are not drawn independently from one fixed distribution, which is the assumption the textbook error rates rest on. `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Because this screener makes a single classification rather than producing a general-purpose score, the relevant stopping rules are the classification family — the sequential probability ratio test, the confidence-interval/adaptive-mastery rule, and the generalized likelihood ratio test. These end as soon as the cut decision is statistically secure and are much shorter than fixed-length estimation. The catch is that textbook SPRT error rates inflate once the questions are being chosen adaptively.

- **Subcategory 1.4: Making an adaptive test still cover the right mix of content**
  - **Source:** Kingsbury & Zara (1989, 1991); van der Linden & Reese (1998); Leung, Chang & Hau (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Constrained CAT (C-CAT)** bolts content control onto a classical selection rule: the next question is drawn from whichever content area is **currently running farthest below its target percentage** of the test, "while maintaining the high measurement precision and short test length" of adaptive testing. `T1`
      - Enforcing a content blueprint has a **length cost**: constrained CAT "requires an increase of 5% to 11% in test length over the traditional adaptive test to reach the same error level," while a testlet-based approach "requires an increase of 43% to 104%." `T1`
      - The **shadow-test approach** re-assembles, before every single question, a complete full-length "shadow" test by linear programming — an optimiser that maximizes information at the current θ, **fixes the already-administered items, and satisfies the entire constraint set** at once. The most informative still-unused question from that shadow test is the one actually administered, so the adaptive test automatically meets every blueprint rule while staying optimal at each θ. On a 753-item LSAT bank, "the θ estimator for adaptive tests of realistic lengths did not suffer any loss of efficiency from the presence of 433 constraints." The method also appears in van der Linden (2000). `T1` The evidence comes from an adjacent setting: an adult high-stakes LSAT bank.
      - Comparative work on content-balancing methods — constrained CAT, modified constrained CAT, and a modified multinomial rule — reports "no systematic effect of content balancing method in measurement efficiency and pool utilization," though the multinomial variant "consistently over-expose[s] fewer items." *(Leung, Chang & Hau, 2003, "Computerized Adaptive Testing: A Comparison of Three Content Balancing Methods," *Journal of Technology, Learning, and Assessment* 2(5).)* `T4` A peer-edited e-journal, used as corroboration only.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Content constraints — constrained CAT, or full shadow-test assembly — force the routing to honour a blueprint so no child ends up with a test made of one question type, and they keep the resulting form auditable. The bill is roughly 5–11% more questions.

- **Subcategory 1.5: Stopping the same questions being seen too often — and what that security costs in precision**
  - **Source:** Sympson & Hetter (1985); Stocking & Lewis (1995, 1998); Revuelta & Ponsoda (1998); van der Linden & Veldkamp (2004); Chang & Ansley (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Exposure control** is the machinery that keeps any one question from being administered so often that it leaks. The foundational probabilistic method is the **Sympson–Hetter (SH) procedure**: once a question has been selected, it is only actually shown with a certain acceptance probability, tuned so that no question reaches more than a target proportion of a reference population. *(Sympson & Hetter, 1985, "Controlling item-exposure rates in computerized adaptive tests," Proceedings of the 27th Annual Meeting of the Military Testing Association, pp. 973–977, San Diego: Navy Personnel R&D Center; documented in Wainer et al., 2000, *Computerized Adaptive Testing: A Primer*.)* `T2` **Conflict of Interest:** military and vendor origin. The original proceedings remain unverified — they were not directly resolved — though the content is corroborated by Stocking & Lewis (1995) and by downstream T1 papers.
      - The **Stocking–Lewis multinomial** method generalizes Sympson–Hetter to control overall exposure, including for sets of questions administered together, but its authors note it "do[es] not have all the features one might eventually require … the overall exposure rate of an item is controlled, [but] its exposure conditional on ability is not." `T3` **Conflict of Interest:** written in an ETS operational-testing context.
      - **Exposure conditional on ability** is the loophole that leaves. A question can have a low *overall* exposure rate and still be administered to "100% of the high[-ability]" test-takers, because the routing sends everyone at the top of the scale to it. A method is presented to control the exposure rate of items **conditional on ability level** in continuous testing. `T1`
      - The **Progressive** method starts out choosing questions largely at random and steadily shifts the weight toward information as the test proceeds; the **Restricted** method simply forbids any item from exceeding a predetermined exposure proportion. The Restricted method "reduce[d] maximum exposure rates," the Progressive method "reduced the number of unused items," both "did well regarding precision," and a **combined Progressive-Restricted** method "may be useful to control item exposure without a serious decrease in test precision." `T1`
      - The **shadow-test / item-eligibility** method folds the exposure limit into the shadow-test assembly itself (1.4), and **a-stratification** (1.2) is itself categorized as a *stratification* exposure-control strategy, alongside Sympson–Hetter (conditional selection) and Progressive (randomization). *(Taxonomy per Georgiadou, Triantafillou & Economides, 2007, *Journal of Technology, Learning, and Assessment* 5(8).)* `T1` (van der Linden & Veldkamp) / `T4` (Georgiadou review)
      - Independent comparisons report a "clear and logical trade-off between item exposure control and measurement precision," with **no single method possessing all desired characteristics**; the Stocking–Lewis conditional multinomial and the Davey–Parshall methods were judged most promising overall. `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Every exposure-control method buys question security by giving up some measurement precision, no method dominates the rest, and the precision loss is largest at extreme ability — exactly where a top-percentile cut sits.

- **Subcategory 1.6: Choosing the question that will leave the least uncertainty *after* it is answered**
  - **Source:** Owen (1975); van der Linden (1998)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Owen (1975)** started the Bayesian line of question selection. Working with a three-parameter **normal-ogive** model — an S-shaped curve linking ability to the chance of a correct answer, the close cousin of the logistic models used today — he replaced the true posterior with **a normal approximation having the same mean and variance**, selected item *k* to satisfy **|bₖ − E(θ | u₁,…,uₖ₋₁)| < δ** for a small δ, and **stopped as soon as the posterior variance fell below a prespecified threshold**. In plain terms: pick a question whose difficulty *b* lands within δ of the current average of the ability distribution — the **expected a posteriori (EAP)** estimate — and stop once the remaining uncertainty, measured as the variance of that distribution, is small enough. `T1` The 1975 original remains unverified; it was not directly resolved this session, and the description is taken from van der Linden's (1998) account.
      - **Owen also proposed the stronger criterion in the same paper** — "minimization of the **preposterior risk under a quadratic loss function**," which "selects the item that **minimizes the expected posterior variance**" — but it was "computationally … more involved" than the δ-rule, "and for this reason the latter became widely popular as Owen's procedure of adaptive testing." `T1`
      - **Minimum expected posterior variance (MEPV), formally.** To select the *k*th item, work out how much uncertainty about θ would be left after each possible answer, weight each of those by how likely that answer is — its **posterior predictive probability** — and take the item that minimizes the total. Writing **u** for the responses already given to the first *k*−1 items: **iₖ = minⱼ { p(Uⱼ=0 | u)·Var(θ | u, Uⱼ=0) + p(Uⱼ=1 | u)·Var(θ | u, Uⱼ=1) }**. It is thus a **preposterior** rule: it scores a question by the uncertainty that will remain *after* the answer, not by information at a point. `T1`
      - **Why the posterior variance rather than the information.** "Though the use of information measures for item selection is a well-established practice in IRT, **the reciprocal of the information measure is only a large-sample approximation to the true variance of the posterior**" — the familiar shortcut of treating 1 ÷ information as the uncertainty only holds once enough answers have piled up. MEPV is therefore "**a small-sample alternative**": it is specifically the criterion that stays correct while the ability estimate is still poor. `T1`
      - MEPV sits in a family of true-posterior criteria proposed in the same paper — **maximum posterior-weighted information (MPWI)**, **maximum expected information (MEI)**, MEPV, and **maximum expected posterior-weighted information (MEPWI)** — of which MEI, MEPV and MEPWI are the ones that use the **posterior predictive distribution of the next response**, meaning they look ahead to what the child is likely to answer. `T1`
      - **The criterion self-destructs as the test lengthens.** Asymptotically — that is, as the test gets long — the Bayesian criteria "lead to the selection of the same items as the maximum-information criterion," and for MEPV specifically "both posterior variances converge to the reciprocal of Fisher's information"; hence "the choice of a Bayesian item selection criterion in adaptive testing **is not expected to lead to improved ability estimation … for long tests**." `T1`
      - **Computation is a real cost, not a rounding error.** The four Bayesian criteria each required **1.0–1.5 seconds per item selection** on the study's hardware (a Pentium 133 PC); MEPV requires re-estimating the full posterior twice per candidate item at every step. `T1` The hardware is dated, so the transferable fact is the per-item cost relative to maximum Fisher information, not the seconds.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** MEPV picks the item that minimizes the uncertainty expected *after* the child answers it — an idea originating with Owen (1975) and formalised by van der Linden (1998). Its stated justification is precisely that Fisher information is only a large-sample approximation to that uncertainty, so MEPV is the small-sample criterion that stays correct early in the test and provably converges to plain maximum-information selection late in it.

- **Subcategory 1.7: Why aiming every question at the current best-guess ability goes wrong at the start of a test**
  - **Source:** Veerkamp & Berger (1997); van der Linden (1998); Chang & Ying (1996); Rulison & Loken (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The whole weighted family exists because the point estimate is untrustworthy early: "as long as the posterior distribution of the estimator has not yet converged to a single point, it may be **suboptimal to select an item with maximum information at a point estimate ignoring values of the ability parameter with substantial likelihood in its neighborhood**." Put plainly, while the child's ability could still credibly be anywhere across a wide band, aiming every question at the single best-guess number throws away the rest of the band. `T1`
      - **Veerkamp & Berger (1997)** proposed criteria that "take into account **the uncertainty of the ability estimates**" and gave "**a general weighted information criterion of which the usual maximum information criterion and the proposed alternative criteria are special cases**" — one formula with a weighting function, out of which the older rule falls as a single setting. Their simulation found "the **likelihood weighted information** criterion is a good alternative to the maximum information criterion," and that so is "a maximum information criterion with the maximum likelihood estimator of ability **replaced by the Bayesian expected a posteriori estimator**" — that is, keep the old rule but aim it at the average of the ability distribution rather than at the single value that makes the answers given most probable. `T1`
      - **Each weighting choice has its own failure mode.** For the **interval information** criterion under the 2-PL — a model that estimates each question's difficulty and its sharpness — "the integral is known to approach the value of the discrimination parameter aⱼ" as the interval widens, so "item selection becomes **independent of the value of the item difficulty parameter bⱼ** — a phenomenon contrary to the well-known importance of the difficulty parameter." Widen the window far enough and the rule stops caring how hard a question is and simply grabs the sharpest one. **Likelihood weighting** improves on this, "but for the first few items the likelihood function is still relatively flat and item selection **may capitalize again on large values of aⱼ independent of the value of bⱼ**"; posterior weighting is described as "the only way out." `T1` — the "capitalize on high-a items" failure is the same mechanism a-stratification counteracts (**1.2**) and the reason exposure skews (**1.5**).
      - **Kullback–Leibler / global information** (Chang & Ying, 1996) belongs to this family as the non-Fisher way of averaging discriminating power over a region rather than at a point; its derivation and short-test result are owned by **1.2** and are not re-derived here. `T1` — cross-ref **1.2**.
      - **The concrete damage from early mis-targeting, at the top of the scale.** In a CAT, "**early mistakes by high-ability students can lead to considerable underestimation, even in tests with 45 items**," and the error is **asymmetric**: "the opposite response pattern, where low-ability students start with lucky guesses, leads to **much less bias**." Using a four-parameter model (Barton & Lord), which allows even a very able child to miss an easy question, plus "**a less informative prior** can lower bias and root mean square error … for high-ability students with a poor start" — the prior being the assumption the test starts with about where a child's ability probably lies. The four-parameter model "slightly outperform[ed] a CAT in which less discriminating items are initially used." `T1` This is a simulation, and it lands directly on the high-ability tail.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Likelihood-weighted, posterior-weighted and Kullback–Leibler criteria all exist because maximum-information selection trusts a point estimate that is unreliable early in the test. The cost of getting that early window wrong is asymmetric and largest for high-ability children, who can still be substantially underestimated 45 questions later.

- **Subcategory 1.8: Does the more sophisticated selection mathematics actually pay off in practice?**
  - **Source:** van der Linden (1998); Chen, Ankenmann & Chang (2000); Choi & Swartz (2009); Thompson (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The originating simulation is favourable to MEPV.** On a 300-item 2-PL pool with sharpness and difficulty drawn as a ~ U(0.5, 1.5), b ~ U(−4, 4), test lengths n = 5/10/20/30, and 250 replications per θ: "for all test lengths **the maximum information criterion had the worst MSE function**, followed by the maximum posterior-weighted information criterion"; "**the differences among the MSE functions for these three criteria [MEI, MEPV, MEPWI] were always negligible**"; and "**even after 30 items**, the MSE functions for [the] first two and last three criteria still **differed by a factor equal to 2**." MSE is mean squared error, the average squared distance between the estimated and the true ability, so lower is better. `T1`
      - **A design caveat on that comparison.** In the same study the maximum-information arm used "θ ~ U(−4.0, 4.0) as prior" — a flat prior that, combined with MAP estimation (taking the peak of the ability distribution as the score), "yields an MLE for θ", the plain maximum-likelihood estimate — while the four Bayesian arms used an **informative empirical normal prior** derived from a background variable. Criterion and prior are therefore **not fully separated** in the reported gap. `T1` That confound is a reasoned extension of the published finding, not something the source states directly: it is read off the stated design rather than claimed by the author.
      - **Independent replication finds the advantage small and short-lived.** Comparing five rules — Fisher information (FI), Fisher interval information, Fisher information with a posterior, KL, and KL with a posterior — the four alternatives "**performed marginally better than FI at the early stages of CAT for θ = −3 and −2**," but "**for tests longer than 10 items, there appeared to be no precision advantage for any of the selection rules**." `T1`
      - **A systematic six-criterion comparison finds no practical benefit.** "The results showed **no clear benefit from more sophisticated selection criteria**," and MEPWI — "previously believed to be superior" — was shown to be **mathematically equivalent to the simpler MPWI**. The authors conclude that "more complex and computing-intensive item selection procedures … with theoretical advantages **do not seem to provide practical benefits over the standard maximum information criterion in conjunction with the EAP**," and that "**the MFI using the EAP theta estimation was very competitive** (although the MEPV method would be preferred from the Bayesian perspective)." `T1` The evidence comes from an adjacent kind of test: polytomous graded-response banks of 30–60 items — questions scored in several ordered steps rather than simply right or wrong — measuring health and psychology outcomes, not a right/wrong K–8 reasoning bank.
      - **What the gain is actually attributable to.** The same authors argue that "**selecting an item based on summarizing the information for a range of theta values** (i.e., using a weight function such as the posterior distribution) as opposed to the information at a single point … **might be all that is necessary to achieve the performance gain**, and predicting the next observation using the predictive posterior distribution **may not be necessary**" — consistent with Veerkamp & Berger's finding that maximum Fisher information evaluated at the EAP performs like the weighted criteria. *(Veerkamp & Berger, 1997, DOI 10.3102/10769986022002203.)* `T1`
      - Reviews of the classification case reach the same verdict — "**no conclusive evidence on the substantial superiority of a single method**," with several methods that "assess items very similarly … and will usually select the same item." `T1` **Conflict of Interest:** the author is vendor-affiliated. Full treatment in **1.3**.
      - These comparisons are all run **unconstrained**, and no study establishing how the ranking survives once real operational constraints are switched on was located. The standing caution in this literature is that differences between selection procedures "will probably be smaller" once exposure control and complex content balancing are added. *(Spray & Reckase, 1994, ED372078.)* `T4` A conference paper. The interaction with exposure control belongs to **1.5**; the only claim extracted here is that it **shrinks** criterion differences.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** MEPV's advantage over maximum Fisher information is real but modest, and concentrated where the ability estimate is poor. The originating simulation reports a factor-of-two gap in mean squared error, with prior and criterion partly confounded, while independent replications find no precision advantage past about ten questions and no practical benefit over maximum information evaluated at the EAP — all at a real per-item computational cost.

- **Subcategory 1.9: Aiming the questions at the cutoff instead of at the child**
  - **Source:** Spray & Reckase (1994); Huebner (2012); Weissman (2007); Eggen (1999); Thompson (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The two objectives are formally different problems.** Adaptive tests "seek to **estimate an examinee's ability parameter as accurately and precisely as possible, as opposed to categorizing the examinee**," and "the difference in the aims … lead to differences in how the two types of tests are delivered" — in both the **item-selection rule** and the **termination rule**. `T4` An open-access peer-reviewed e-journal, used as corroboration.
      - **The core result: when the objective is a decision, the best question sits at the cut, not at the child.** "Results … provide very clear evidence that **selecting items to maximize information at the decision point results in shorter average test lengths than selecting items to maximize information either at true ability (which is impossible) or the most recent ability estimate**." `T4` A conference paper; the peer-reviewed companion comparing the SPRT to sequential Bayes is Spray & Reckase (1996), in **1.3**.
      - **A worked Rasch illustration of why** — the Rasch model being the simplest scoring model, estimating difficulty alone and treating every question as equally sharp. With the cut at θ₀ = 0.0 and an examinee at θ = 1.0, testing H₀ at α = .05 needs **n = 3.78 items** when items are selected at the cut (b = 0.0, where the examinee's success probability is .73 against a null of .50) but **n = 4.26 items** when items are selected at the examinee's own ability (b = 1.0, where success is .50 against a null of .27) — matching the examinee **flattens the very contrast the decision rests on**. `T4`
      - **The gap widens exactly where a selective cut sits.** "When the **decision points are high relative to the prior distribution, or for high ability examinees**, the differences in average test length for the alternative item selection methods can be **quite dramatic** … This finding may be quite important when certification or licensure standards are fairly high, **or selection criteria are rigorous**." `T4` The evidence comes from an adjacent setting: a college-placement item pool with pass/fail cuts, not a top-percentile K–8 cut.
      - **The documented exception runs through the guessing floor.** The advantage of cut-based selection reverses "for very low ability examinees **when a model with nonzero lower asymptotes is used**" — a lower asymptote being the score floor a child can reach by guessing alone. Below about **θ = −1.5**, "selecting at the estimated ability may save one or two items," because cut-targeted items are so hard for those examinees that "the noise induced by the c parameter has a prominent effect," c being that guessing floor. `T4` — see **1.10**.
      - **What cut-based selection costs.** It is barely adaptive to the person: "**all examinees start with the same items** (unless some sort of exposure control is used) and **the only adaptation that takes place is in the length of the test**." Consequently it forfeits the **inherent exposure control** that ability-matched selection provides — under ability matching "it is likely that a given pair of examinees will not have seen the same exact set of items," whereas under cut-based selection "it is **guaranteed that every examinee will see the most informative items in the pool**," so "the problem of item overexposure is **exacerbated**." `T4` — exposure *mechanics* owned by **1.5**.
      - **What estimation-based selection costs.** It buys a θ estimate that is good across the whole scale, at the price of more items to reach the same decision accuracy (above); conversely, cut-based selection concentrates precision at the cut, so the resulting θ is **not a general-purpose ability score away from the cut**. The two objectives are traded off, not jointly optimised. This last step is a reasoned extension of the published finding rather than something the source states directly: it follows from Spray & Reckase (1994) plus the definition of the information function, and no source quantifying the off-cut precision loss for a top-percentile screener was located.
      - **Inside the classification problem the criterion ranking changes again.** For a four-category adaptive classification test, "**mutual information (MI) item selection classifies the highest proportion of examinees correctly and yields the shortest test lengths**. The next best performance is observed for **FIP** [posterior-weighted Fisher] item selection, followed by **FI**." `T1` — consistent with Kullback–Leibler selection performing "better than or as well as" Fisher-based selection under the SPRT (Eggen, 1999, **1.3**).
      - **Counterweight.** Cut-based selection is not automatically best: "the efficiency of item selection approaches **depend on the termination criteria**," and "**item selection at the cut score, which seems conceptually appropriate for CCT, is not always the most efficient option**." `T1` **Conflict of Interest:** the author is vendor-affiliated. Full treatment in **1.3**.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** When the objective is a classification at a cut rather than a score, the optimal item is located at the cut and not at the examinee — an advantage that is largest precisely for high, selective cuts. But cut-based selection buys that shorter test by giving up person-level adaptation, the inherent exposure control that ability matching provides, and precision in the ability estimate away from the cut.

- **Subcategory 1.10: Because children can guess, the most informative question is one they get right about 65% of the time**
  - **Source:** Birnbaum (1968) / Lord (1980); Magis (2013); Eggen & Verschoor (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Under the 1-PL and 2-PL, the most informative item is the coin-flip item.** Neither model has a guessing term: the 1-PL (Rasch) estimates only each question's difficulty, and the 2-PL adds each question's sharpness. With the guessing floor c = 0, the item information function — the curve showing how much precision a question contributes at each ability level — "is maximized whenever x = 0.5, implying that θ = b is the optimal ability level," meaning the best question is the one the child has an even chance of answering. `T1`
      - **Under a non-zero lower asymptote it is not.** For the 3-PL — which adds a guessing floor c on top of difficulty and sharpness — maximising information in P-space requires the roots of **p(x) = −2x² + x + c**, which "are equal to **0.25 ± √(1+8c)/4**"; the relevant (positive) root "**belongs to (0.5; 1)**", so the optimum sits above a coin flip. Equivalently, the optimal success probability is **P\* = (1 + √(1 + 8c)) / 4**, and the optimal ability is **θ\* = b + (1/a)·log[(1 + √(1 + 8c)) / 2]**. `T1`
      - **The derivation is Birnbaum's, restated by Lord.** "With the 3PL model, the solution was provided by **Birnbaum (1968; see also Lord, 1980)**"; Magis's algebra "corresponds exactly to the result provided by Lord (1980)." The two originals are Birnbaum (1968), in Lord & Novick, *Statistical Theories of Mental Test Scores*, and Lord (1980), *Applications of Item Response Theory to Practical Testing Problems*. `T1` (Magis) / `T2` (Birnbaum; Lord) The Birnbaum and Lord pages remain unverified, as they were not directly resolved this session; the derivation is independently reproduced in Magis (2013).
      - **What it means for five-option items.** At **c = 0.20** — a five-option item guessed at chance — **P\* ≈ 0.653**, so the information-maximising item is one the child gets right about **65%** of the time, not 50%, and the optimal difficulty sits **≈ 0.27/a logits below** the child's ability, a logit being one unit on the θ scale. For four options (c = 0.25), **P\* ≈ 0.683**. `T1` These are arithmetic from the cited formula P\* = (1 + √(1 + 8c))/4, not a separately reported statistic.
      - **Caveats on transferring the number.** Estimated c is frequently **below** the reciprocal of the number of options, so 0.653 is an upper anchor rather than a design constant. And if an **upper asymptote** is modelled — a ceiling below 1.0 reflecting that even an able child sometimes slips through inattention — the optimum moves again and no longer has the closed 3-PL form. `T1` No source calibrating c for a K–8 text-only reasoning bank was located.
      - **The 3-PL case is explicitly flagged as open in the adjacent literature.** Work on selecting easier or harder items notes that its methods "are **symmetric around the p-50 points** … only true for the 1pl and 2pl model. Knowing that **the symmetry disappears**, it is worthwhile investigating the application of the selection method if the 3pl model, including a guessing parameter, is used." `T1` — see **1.12**.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Under a model with a guessing floor the information-maximising item is not the coin-flip item: the optimum sits at P\* = (1 + √(1 + 8c))/4, which for five-option items is about 65% success. So part of the gap between "information-optimal" and "child-tolerable" difficulty closes for free, and the symmetric easier/harder machinery built for 1-PL and 2-PL banks no longer applies unchanged.

- **Subcategory 1.11: Does a test that keeps a child at 50% correct demotivate them? The evidence splits**
  - **Source:** Eggen & Verschoor (2006); Ortner, Weißkopf & Koch (2014); Ortner & Caspers (2011); Asseburg & Frey (2013); Martin & Lazendic (2018); Akhtar et al. (2023); Frey, Liu, Fink & König (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The premise is quantified, not rhetorical.** "**Maximum information item selection in CATs using an item bank that is calibrated with the one or the two-parameter logistic model results in each individual answering about 50% of the items correctly.**" `T1`
      - **Psychometricians themselves raise the child-specific worry.** CAT tests "can be perceived as very difficult for each individual student and this could have possible negative side effects, for example, **enhanced test anxiety and, consequently, possible lower test performance**. This could **especially be the case for tests which are administered in primary and secondary education**, where, traditionally, tests are constructed in such a way that the average student has … a somewhat **higher probability (60 or 70%)** of correctly answering the items." A report version of this work appears as Cito Measurement and Research Department Report 2004-2. `T1` — an argued rationale, not an empirical finding.
      - **Against — adaptive delivery lowered motivation in adolescents.** In 174 students aged 15–21 given either a CAT or a fixed-item version (FIT) of the same matrices test, "**more situational fear of failure and less subjective probability of success were reported using CAT compared to FIT**"; self-reported **flow did not differ**; and "as average reported motivation was lower during CAT, **results contradict assumptions of enhanced motivation during CAT**." `T1` The sample is an adjacent population — ages 15–21 in German secondary schools. The publication year also remains unverified: Crossref indexes the issue as 2014, while many records cite the 2013 online-first version.
      - **Against — the harm is concentrated in anxious test-takers, and is partly an explanation problem.** Testing the hypothesis "that tests containing mainly **items with medium probabilities of being solved** would have negative effects on test performance for testtakers high in test anxiety," a study of 110 students aged 16–20 found "a **significant interaction of test anxiety and test mode**": the effect of test mode on score "was **stronger for students with higher scores on test anxiety**." Notably, "**getting information about CAT led to significantly better results than receiving standard test instructions**" — simply explaining to the test-taker how an adaptive test works helped. `T1` Again an adjacent population: ages 16–20, n = 110.
      - **Against, with a twist — the effort/difficulty relation is linear, not an inverted U.** In **N = 9,452** German ninth-graders (PISA 2006 mathematics), "**ability-difficulty fit was positively linear related with effort and boredom/daydreaming**," and "overall, mean item difficulty exceeded individual ability," so "**low ability students may not show maximum performance** in a sequential achievement test." The same easing that raises effort also raises boredom; no sweet spot at 50% is observed. `T1` An adjacent setting: ninth-graders taking a fixed sequential test, not an adaptive one.
      - **For — the largest school-age study found no motivational penalty and a precision gain.** Among **N = 12,736** Australian students in **years 3, 5, 7 and 9**, adaptive (multistage) delivery "generated **lower achievement error rates (i.e., higher measurement precision)**," with positive motivation, engagement and subjective-experience effects "relatively greater for **females and older students**"; the authors state the findings "**counter claims that computer-adaptive testing reduces students' test-relevant motivation, engagement, and subjective experience**." `T1` This study is K–8 relevant, covering years 3–9, but it is still adjacent to the question at hand: it contrasts multistage routing with a fixed order and never manipulates the success-rate target.
      - **For — meta-analysis finds no overall motivational effect of adaptivity either way.** Across 11 direct CAT-vs-fixed comparisons, "**no overall effect of test type on anxiety and motivation** … (k = 11, g+ = 0.06, p = .28)" — k being the number of studies pooled and g+ the pooled effect size, where 0 means no difference. Only "two of the four studies on motivation and four of nine studies on anxiety … supported the benefits of CAT, while one of them showed the opposite result: a decrease in motivation under CAT." `T1`
      - **Both at once — but item difficulty is a significant moderator.** A larger meta-analysis (**27 studies, 190 effect sizes**) found "the overall effect of CAT was **not significant for motivation and negative emotion** and significant for positive emotion after publication bias correction," while moderator analysis found a significant effect of "**average difficulty of presented items**" on **negative emotion**; the authors recommend that CAT configurations "avoid speededness, **present easy items**, allow for response revision, and provide feedback." `T1`
      - No study manipulating the **target success probability** and measuring effort, rapid-guessing or anxiety in a **K–8 gifted upper-tail** screener was located. Most of the child-age evidence contrasts *adaptive vs fixed delivery*, which is not the same manipulation; the one located study that varies the success target directly in school-age children is on a general middle-school mathematics assessment (**1.12**), not on a top-percentile reasoning screen.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The evidence does not resolve the tension: adolescents report more fear of failure and lower success expectancy under adaptive delivery, and the harm is worst for anxious test-takers, yet the largest school-age study and two meta-analyses find no overall motivational penalty from adaptivity. Both meta-analyses do agree that the average difficulty of the items presented is one of the few moderators that genuinely matters.

- **Subcategory 1.12: Making the test deliberately easier — and exactly what that costs in precision**
  - **Source:** Bergstrom, Lunz & Gershon (1992); Eggen & Verschoor (2006); Ling, Attali, Finn & Stone (2017); Akhtar et al. (2023); Pitkin & Vispoel (2001)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The mechanism.** Rather than selecting maximum information *at* the current ability estimate, select the item with maximum information at a **shifted** ability level — aim a little below the child so more answers come out right. Matching instead on an item's **probability points** (the p-60 or p-70 point, meaning the ability at which a child would have a 60% or 70% chance of getting it right) also works, but "yields good results **only with the one-parameter logistic model and not with the two-parameter** logistic model," because it ignores the height of the information function, which is how much precision the item actually contributes. The 1-PL precursor is Bergstrom, Lunz & Gershon (1992). `T1` That 1992 paper is cited here as Eggen & Verschoor describe it; the original was not directly resolved this session, so it remains unverified.
      - **The price, in numbers.** In a large 2-PL bank with a 40-item test, mean standard error and mean percent-correct were: **max-information 0.083 at 50.1%**, **p-60 0.085 at 60.1%**, **p-70 0.091 at 70.2%**, **p-80 0.106 at 79.7%**, **p-90 0.143 at 88.9%**, against **random selection 0.145 at 50.4%**. Expressed as items needed to match the precision of a 30-item random test: **11 / 12 / 13 / 17 / 29** respectively. `T1` The table values were read from the openly available Cito Measurement and Research Department Report 2004-2 preprint; the published table numbering remains unverified, as it was not directly resolved this session.
      - **The authors' own verdict.** "**For practical purposes, item selection, aiming at percentages correct of 60 or 70 (or 40 or 30), seems to be possible without a large loss in precision**," whereas "extreme values of the success probabilities are combined with a considerable loss in precision." `T1`
      - **Exposure control makes the compromise more expensive and less accurate.** Adding Sympson–Hetter exposure control at a maximum exposure rate of 0.3 cost "on average … **2 or 3 items**," and "**the discrepancy between the desired and the achieved percentages correct is larger when exposure control is applied**." `T1` — exposure mechanics owned by **1.5**; the point retained here is that exposure control **constrains how precisely a success-rate target can be hit at all**.
      - **The compromise has been tested on children, and it worked — motivationally.** Middle-school students took a mathematics assessment under **fixed, adaptive, or easier-adaptive** conditions: "**the easier adaptive test resulted in higher engagement and lower anxiety than either the adaptive or fixed-item tests**," and "these effects were **not related to ability level**." `T1` This one is K–8 relevant, being middle school. **Conflict of Interest:** all four authors are at Educational Testing Service.
      - **But the motivational gain did not convert into a measurement gain.** In the same study, "results showed **little evidence for test type effects**" and "**no significant differences in performance were found across test types**"; the authors raise "the possibility that test experiences in adaptive tests may **not in practice be significantly different** than in fixed-item tests." `T1`
      - **Meta-analytically, "easier CAT" is the one configuration with a positive effect.** Against an overall null for CAT vs fixed-item testing, "**easier CAT had [a] positive effect compared with FIT (k = 2, g+ = .22, p < .001)**" — and the reviewers note this rests on the small number of studies that compared an easier CAT with a regular CAT. `T1` The evidence base is thin: only k = 2 studies.
      - **A related lever, and its trade.** Letting examinees choose the difficulty themselves (self-adapted testing) "yielded proficiency estimates that were **0.12 standard deviation units higher** and post-test anxiety levels that were **0.19 standard deviation units lower** than those yielded by CATs," after controlling for measurement error — but self-adapted tests are **less precise and less efficient** than CATs. `T1` An adjacent population: mostly college samples.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Targeting a higher success probability is a solved engineering problem with a measured price — about 60–70% correct costs on the order of one or two extra items, and above that the precision loss climbs steeply — and in middle-schoolers an easier adaptive test did raise engagement and lower anxiety, but it produced no better scores, so the compromise is currently evidenced as an experience improvement rather than a measurement one.

### Category 2: Item ordering, position & context effects

- **Subcategory 2.1: Putting the hard questions first can depress scores and raise stress — but the finding does not always replicate**
  - **Source:** Hambleton & Traub (1974); Tippets & Benson (1989); Plake et al. (1981); Leary & Dorans (1985); Davey & Lee (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Hambleton & Traub (1974) reported that on a mathematics test the mean number of correct answers was **significantly lower** when items were arranged difficult-to-easy than when arranged easy-to-difficult, and that item order **affected the amount of stress** (measured physiologically) generated during the test. The sample is not specified as K–8, so this is evidence from an adjacent population rather than from the age group in question. [T1]
      - Tippets & Benson (1989) found, using an actual classroom exam with 128 graduate students, **significant effects of item arrangement on state test anxiety** — anxiety felt in the moment, while the test is happening. Anxiety was highest under the hard-to-easy arrangement in one course and under the random arrangement in the other. The authors state item arrangement "may introduce a source of variance unrelated to content, thereby reducing the validity of achievement tests." These were graduate students, an adjacent population rather than K–8 children. [T1]
      - Plake et al. (1981) found, on a 48-item college mathematics exam assembled in three orderings (easy-hard, uniform, random) and given with or without telling students which arrangement they had, **no significant effect** of item order, knowledge of arrangement, or anxiety level on number-right or elimination scores (two ways of scoring the same answers), tested by MANOVA and MANCOVA — statistical procedures for group differences across several outcome measures at once — at α = .05, the conventional 5% significance threshold. The authors note the study may have had inadequate power to detect a real effect. The population is undergraduates, adjacent rather than K–8, and the exact co-author list remains unverified: ERIC lists only "Plake, B. S.; And Others". [T1]
      - Leary & Dorans (1985), reviewing the item-arrangement and context literature, concluded that it "has produced evidence of context effects, but has **not demonstrated that the effects are so strong as to invalidate** test theory or practice that is dependent on an assumption of item parameter invariance." Invariance here means that a question's difficulty statistics stay put no matter where in the test it appears. **Conflict of Interest:** both authors work for the Educational Testing Service (ETS), the organisation that produces the GRE. [T1 review]
      - Davey & Lee (2011), as summarized by Ma & Harris (2024), reported that item-position effects influenced by **test speededness** — how far it is the time limit rather than the difficulty that stops people finishing — **are a complicated function of test length, test difficulty, and time limits**. **Conflict of Interest:** the authors are at ETS. [T3 report]
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The literature keeps two consequences of item order strictly apart: what ordering does to total scores and to the child's experience, which is this subcategory, and what it does to the questions' own difficulty statistics, which is 2.2. On the first, the evidence is inconsistent — significant on speeded tests, null on power tests — and context effects are real but generally not strong enough to invalidate item-parameter invariance.

- **Subcategory 2.2: The same question gets harder — and occasionally easier — the later it appears in the test**
  - **Source:** Christiansen & Janssen (2020); Albano et al. (2019); Albano (2013); Davis & Ferdous (2005); Weirich et al. (2017); Debeer et al. (2014); Wu et al. (2019); Nagy et al. (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Christiansen & Janssen (2020) summarize that two opposite effects of item position on difficulty are observed: item difficulty may **increase** toward the end (a "fatigue" effect) or **decrease** toward the end (a "practice" effect). The sample is 15-year-olds taking foreign-language tests, an adjacent population rather than K–8. [T1]
      - Albano et al. (2019) fit multilevel item-response models — models that place children and questions on a single shared difficulty scale, whose units are called *logits* — to an **early-literacy** measure and found statistically significant **increases in difficulty for items appearing later** in a 20-item form; the estimated linear change was **.024 logits per one-position increase**, i.e., a predicted **.46-logit** increase from the start to the end of the form. This one is measured on children in early education. [T1]
      - Albano (2013) modeled item position as a continuous variable using real data from a **K-12 reading achievement test** administered to over **90,000 students** (pilot items placed in random positions) and GRE pilot sections (~1,800 examinees), estimating position effects as **slopes (change in item difficulty per shift in position)**. The K-12 reading data make this directly relevant to K–8. [T1]
      - Davis & Ferdous (2005) compared item difficulties for the same items in field versus live administration — using both p-values (the share of students answering correctly) and b-parameters (the difficulty estimate on the shared scale) — and reported **declines in student performance as items dropped to later positions**; the item-position/difficulty relationship was statistically significant for Grade 5 Reading (Analysis 1), and correlation analyses were significant for all tested grade/subject combinations except Grade 3 Reading. The grades tested are K–8 grades. [T4 conference/technical]
      - Weirich et al. (2017) found, over a 60-minute large-scale assessment, a **substantial linear position effect (item difficulty increased across the test)** that **varied considerably across persons**; measured test-taking effort **diminished considerably across the test**, and only the **change** in effort (not initial effort) moderated the position effect. The setting is a German large-scale assessment, adjacent rather than K–8. [T1]
      - Debeer et al. (2014) analyzed PISA 2009 reading (N = 467,819; 65 countries) — PISA being the international assessment sat by 15-year-olds — and found a **consistent decrease in examinee effort across the test** ("persistence"), with individual differences within and between schools, **more pronounced in lower-performing countries**, concluding it is "important to model and control examinee effort in low-stakes assessments." Age 15 is an adjacent population, not K–8. [T1]
      - Wu et al. (2019) analyzed PISA 2006/2009/2012 and found **individual differences in position effects (persistence)**; **girls sustained performance better than boys**, and students reporting greater reading enjoyment showed **less performance decline** (PISA 2009 reading). Age 15 again, adjacent rather than K–8. [T1]
      - Nagy et al. (2018) modeled a reading-comprehension test with **random position effects on item difficulty and fixed position effects on item discrimination** — discrimination being how sharply a question separates stronger students from weaker ones — finding **gradually increasing difficulties and decreasing discriminations** toward the test end; variation in position effects related to students' **decoding speed and reading enjoyment**. This is secondary-school reading comprehension, an adjacent population. [T1]
      - Christiansen & Janssen (2020) found, in the 2012 European Survey of Language Competences, **consistent item-position effects for listening comprehension but not for reading comprehension** across languages; for a large subset of items difficulty **decreased** with later position (practice effect). Again 15-year-olds, adjacent rather than K–8. [T1]
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Where a question sits in the test measurably changes how hard it is. The effect is robustly documented and usually runs one way — questions get harder later on, as effort and attention drain away — but it sometimes runs the other way, as children get the hang of an unfamiliar format. It is bigger for some children than others, and in the one early-education study it ran at about **.024 logits per position**, accumulating to roughly half a logit across a 20-item form.

- **Subcategory 2.3: Where a question sits changes how hard it is — and not equally for every child**
  - **Source:** Debeer & Janssen (2013); Bulut, Guo & Gierl (2017); Bulut, Lei & Guo (2018); AERA/APA/NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Debeer & Janssen (2013) showed that with a rotated booklet design — different children get the same questions in different orders — item difficulty and item position can be disentangled, and that item-position effects can be treated as **an instance of differential item functioning (DIF)**: the statistical check for whether a particular question is harder for one group than another *among children of equal ability*, with the groups here defined by which booklet, and therefore which position, a child received. A **linear trend** can summarize the DIF parameters across positions. [T1]
      - Bulut, Guo & Gierl (2017) introduced a **structural-equation-modeling (SEM)** approach — a general statistical framework linking observed answers to underlying abilities, usable here because item response theory, the scoring maths that places children and questions on one shared difficulty scale, is mathematically equivalent to a factor analysis of right/wrong data — capable of estimating **form, passage-position, and item-position effects**, demonstrated on a large-scale reading assessment. The middle author's name remains unverified: the journal's own citation string renders it as "Quo", while the author's other records list "Qi Guo". [T1]
      - Bulut, Lei & Guo (2018) applied the SEM approach to a computer-based **alternate assessment across grade bands 3–5, 6–8, and high school** and found the difficulty of some field-test items **differed depending on position**, and that overall field-test-task difficulty in **grade band 6–8 increased** as students reached it in later positions. Grade bands 3–5 and 6–8 put this inside the K–8 range. [T1]
      - The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014) treat **construct-irrelevant variance** — score variance attributable to factors extraneous to the target construct, meaning score movement caused by something other than the ability the test is meant to measure — as a threat to validity, and treat **standardized administration conditions** as a basis for score comparability and fairness. [T2 canonical]
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Position effects are formally treated as construct-irrelevant variance — score movement that has nothing to do with the ability being measured — and they can be modelled as a form of differential item functioning whenever different children receive different item sequences. That reframes ordering from a scheduling detail into a fairness question with an established statistical test attached.

- **Subcategory 2.5: A question's neighbours change how that question behaves**
  - **Source:** Leary & Dorans (1985); Kingston & Dorans (1984); Wainer & Kiely (1987); Albano et al. (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Leary & Dorans (1985) define an item's context by the features, content, and quantity of adjacent items, and note that when context changes, an item's **psychometric properties (difficulty, discrimination) can change** — how hard it is and how sharply it separates stronger children from weaker ones — biasing the estimates of those properties. **Conflict of Interest:** both authors are at ETS. [T1 review]
      - Kingston & Dorans (1984) note that a within-test context effect implies that any equating method using item data — equating being the work of putting two forms on genuinely the same scale — should administer items in the **same position** across old and new forms, because a chain of equatings could otherwise **drift** steadily away from the truth through accumulated systematic bias. **Conflict of Interest:** the authors are at ETS. [T1]
      - Wainer & Kiely (1987) introduced the **testlet** — "a group of items related to a single content area that is developed as a unit" — proposing it to alleviate **context effects, item-difficulty ordering, and content balancing** in computerized adaptive testing, so that each item "carries its own context with it." **Conflict of Interest:** the idea originated at ETS (report RR-86-33). [T2 canonical]
      - Albano et al. (2020) compared **blocked vs interleaved** task ordering — all of one task type together, versus mixing the types up — across four alphabet-knowledge task types in an early-childhood assessment. Interleaving (varying item context) had a **negligible impact on mean performance** but produced **stronger internal-consistency reliability** (answers hanging together more coherently) **and improved item discrimination**, plus **longer mean response time** (blocked 117.93 s vs interleaved 132.79 s; t95 = −2.67, p = 0.009; d = 0.51 — an effect size *d* states a difference in standard deviations, the typical spread of scores across a population, so 0.51 is a moderate difference). This one is measured on young children. [T1]
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** What sits next to a question can shift that question's own difficulty and discrimination, which is exactly the problem the self-contained testlet was invented to solve. Mixing standalone task types together rather than grouping them is a different story: in an early-childhood measure it was psychometrically neutral-to-beneficial, costing some extra time but buying more coherent, better-discriminating scores.

- **Subcategory 2.6: Children warm up to unfamiliar question types — but nobody has tested a dedicated warm-up section for this age group**
  - **Source:** Wing (1980); Christiansen & Janssen (2020); Albano et al. (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Wing (1980) documented **practice effects with traditional mental-test items** — performance changes attributable to prior exposure or practice. The sample is adults, an adjacent population rather than K–8. [T1]
      - Christiansen & Janssen (2020) attribute the observed **decrease in difficulty at later positions** (for many items) to a **practice effect**, i.e., test-takers becoming more familiar with the task over the test. Again 15-year-olds, adjacent rather than K–8. [T1]
      - Albano et al. (2020) note in discussion that **initially unfamiliar / novel item types may become easier as test-takers "warm up" to them**, a mechanism producing decreasing difficulty across position. This one is measured on young children. [T1]
      - No study isolating the **causal effect of dedicated (unscored) warm-up or practice items on subsequent scored-item difficulty, anxiety, or completion in a K–8 on-screen adaptive screener** was located. The warm-up rationale here is a reasoned extension of the practice-effect direction and the item-unfamiliarity findings, not something any located source states directly.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Difficulty falling at later positions for novel item types is attributed to a warm-up or practice effect — children getting the hang of the format. But no study isolating the causal effect of dedicated unscored warm-up items in a K–8 adaptive screener was located, so the case for building a warm-up section rests on extension from adjacent findings rather than on direct evidence.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1007/s11092-020-09335-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11092-020-09335-7) | [https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2020.00133](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2020.00133)
- **Subcategory 2.7: In an adaptive test, ignoring position effects biases the child's ability estimate**
  - **Source:** Kingston & Dorans (1984); Albano et al. (2019); Davey & Lee (2011); Ma & Harris (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kingston & Dorans (1984) state that for computerized adaptive testing to be practical, **item pools that are unaffected by the order of administration are required**, and "the assumption of no location effect needs to be tested." **Conflict of Interest:** the authors are at ETS. [T1]
      - Albano et al. (2019) ran a simulation of item-position effects within **item-level computerized adaptive testing** and found that ignoring position effects can introduce **non-negligible bias in person-ability estimation** — the test settles on the wrong estimate of how able the child is. The context is early education, inside the K–8 range. [T1]
      - Davey & Lee (2011) reported item-position effects in GRE **multistage** data — a multistage test adapts in blocks of questions rather than after every single question — drawn from scrambled linear forms and the revised GRE, and found that **pretesting items in random locations** through the test can effectively mitigate position effects, at least when speededness effects are present. **Conflict of Interest:** the authors are at ETS. [T3 report]
      - Ma & Harris (2024) compared approaches to controlling item-position effects in computerized adaptive testing and found that adjusting via a **pretesting design** or a **pool design** produced **better ability-estimation accuracy** than no adjustment or item-level adjustment. [T1]
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Ignoring position effects biases the ability estimate an adaptive test produces, and the controls that actually work operate at the level of the whole question pool: pretest each question in several different positions, or calibrate the pool with position built in. Patching question by question after the fact performed worse than either.

### Category 3: Retakes & repeated administration

- **Subcategory 3.1: Three different things get called "reliability," and they are not interchangeable**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014), Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing, ch. 2
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Classical test theory recognizes three broad families of reliability coefficient: **(a) alternate-form**, a *coefficient of equivalence*, where two matched-but-different forms are given in independent sessions; **(b) test–retest**, a *coefficient of stability*, where the **same** form is given on two separate occasions; and **(c) internal-consistency**, computed from a single sitting by checking how coherently the questions hang together. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing, ch. 2, p. 37.)* `[T2]`
      - These coefficients are **not interchangeable** — "internal-consistency, alternate-form, and test–retest coefficients should not be considered equivalent, as each incorporates a unique definition of measurement error"; the error variances produced by item response theory are generally not equivalent to those from the other approaches either. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 2.6** + comment.)* `[T2]`
      - When a test–retest or alternate-form approach is used, **the interval between administrations must be reported**, and it is unacceptable to report a bare "reliability = .90" without the design behind it. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 2.19** comment.)* `[T2]`
      - The **reliability/precision of a difference (change) score can be much lower than the reliabilities of the two separate scores** it is computed from. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 2.4** comment.)* `[T2]`
      - Reliability is **lowest in the youngest children**: "preschool children tend to respond to test stimuli in a less consistent fashion than do older children." *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 2.11** comment.)* `[T2]`
      - Reliability must be reported **per grade/age band**; a coefficient pooled across grades with steadily rising means "will generally give a spuriously inflated impression of reliability/precision." *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 2.12**.)* `[T2]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Test–retest, alternate-form, and internal-consistency reliability are three different numbers measuring three different things, and they cannot be swapped for one another. The interval between sittings has to be reported alongside any of them, a change score is less trustworthy than either score it is built from, and reliability is at its worst in the youngest children — which is why it must be reported grade by grade rather than pooled into one flattering average.
    - **Link to source:** [https://www.testingstandards.net/](https://www.testingstandards.net/)
- **Subcategory 3.2: Taking the test again raises the score by a small, predictable amount**
  - **Source:** Hausknecht et al. (2007); Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018); Van Iddekinge & Arnold (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A meta-analysis of cognitive-ability retesting in **selection** pooled 50 studies, **107 samples, N = 134,436**, and found an **adjusted overall effect size d = .26** (uncorrected ≈ .24) — about a quarter of a standard deviation, which is a *small* gain by Cohen's benchmarks. Because cognitive ability is stable, factors other than true-ability change likely drive the gain. The setting is adult employment selection, adjacent to rather than the same as K–8 schooling. `[T1]`
      - An independent meta-analysis of cognitive-ability tests, broader than selection alone, covered **174 samples from 122 studies, 786 outcomes, N = 153,185**; retest effects were significant, with a mean **observed first-to-second standardized gain of SMCR ≈ 0.29** (bias-corrected standardized mean change, standardized on raw scores, and read roughly like Cohen's *d*; Table 1). `[T1]`
      - **25%–50% of applicants** in organizational and educational settings are retested with cognitive-ability measures. Adult applicant settings again, not K–8. `[T1]`
      - A recent review concludes retest scores tend to be **higher, more variable, more reliable, and to show somewhat stronger relations with criteria** (academic or job performance) than initial scores. This too comes from adult work settings rather than from children. `[T1]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Repeated administration is a decision-and-logistics policy as much as a measurement question: it reliably nudges scores up, and the size, fairness, and validity of that nudge depend on which form is used (3.3), how long the gap is (3.5), and how many attempts are allowed (3.4). The nudge itself is small and consistent — roughly a quarter of a standard deviation (d ≈ .26; SMCR ≈ .29) — and because ability is stable, most of it is not real improvement in ability.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373) | [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003) | [https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349)
- **Subcategory 3.3: Retaking the *same* form gives about twice the boost of retaking a different one**
  - **Source:** Hausknecht et al. (2007); Scharfen et al. (2018); Kulik, Kulik & Bangert (1984); AERA/APA/NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Identical forms produce roughly twice the gain of alternate forms**: corrected **d = 0.46 (identical) vs. 0.24 (alternate)**. That scores rise *even across non-identical forms* shows the gain is not purely item memorization. The data are from adult employment selection, an adjacent population. `[T1]`
      - Test-form **equivalence was a significant moderator** in the largest cognitive-ability meta-analysis (identical > alternate). Secondary summaries report the first-to-second gain as **SMCR ≈ 0.37 (identical) vs. ≈ 0.23 (alternate)**. This split remains unverified: it was not located verbatim in the primary tables reviewed, and comes from a secondary summary (d-nb.info/1270489011) citing Scharfen et al. `[T1]`
      - An earlier meta-analysis of **aptitude/achievement** tests — the closest population to K–8 students available in this literature — likewise found identical > alternate, **d ≈ 0.42 vs. 0.23**. These values remain unverified against the primary source: they are as summarized in Scharfen et al. (2018) and the secondary summary d-nb.info/1270489011. *(Kulik, Kulik & Bangert, 1984, *American Educational Research Journal*, 21(2), 435–447.)* `[T1]`
      - Mechanism for the identical-form advantage: on an identical retest, examinees **recall prior answers and errors, freeing working-memory capacity** — the limited mental workspace a person can hold things in — to attempt harder items. That is a test-specific strategy, inapplicable when alternate, differently-worded items are used. *(Cook & Campbell, 1979, as cited in Hausknecht et al., 2007; Scharfen et al., 2018, §1.3.2.)* `[T1]`
      - "Alternate form" is a term of art: forms **built to the same content and statistical specifications to measure the same construct**, with **equating** used to enhance cross-form comparability. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, ch. 5, footnote on alternate forms; glossary "parallel forms".)* `[T2]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Handing a child the identical form again produces roughly twice the gain of handing them a matched but different one (d ≈ 0.46 versus 0.24), because on an identical retest a child can recall their earlier answers and spend the freed mental capacity on the harder questions — a strategy that simply does not transfer to differently-worded items.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312021002435](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312021002435)
- **Subcategory 3.4: How big the retake boost is — front-loaded after the first attempt, and bigger the sooner it comes**
  - **Source:** Scharfen et al. (2018); Van Iddekinge & Arnold (2017); Hausknecht, Trevor & Farr (2002); Hausknecht et al. (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Score gains **plateau: no further significant score gains after the third administration**. `[T1]`
      - The relationship between number of retests and score change is **curvilinear** — scores improve from the initial test to the first retest and then stabilize, a pattern first noted on the U.S. Army Alpha in 1920. Adult work settings, adjacent rather than K–8. `[T1]`
      - A field study of **N = 4,726** law-enforcement candidates on **identical** tests found significant score increases **both** 1st→2nd **and** 2nd→3rd administration. Adult candidates, adjacent again. `[T1]`
      - **Shorter intervals produce larger gains; longer intervals produce smaller gains.** In the selection meta-analysis the median gap between attempts was only **20 days**. Adult selection data, adjacent rather than K–8. `[T1]`
      - The test–retest **interval was a significant moderator** with a **negative influence** on effect size; across primary studies the interval after which retest effects diminish ranged widely, from roughly **2 to 13 years**. *(Scharfen et al., 2018, §1.3.3.)* `[T1]`
      - Mechanism: item-specific memory — recalled strategies, rules, and answers — decays with time, so short intervals preserve the recall advantage that inflates identical-form retests. `[T1]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The retest gain is front-loaded and then flattens out: most of it arrives on the first retake, one field study still found a real increase on the third, and the meta-analytic evidence finds no further significant gain beyond that point. Its size also depends on timing — the shorter the gap between attempts, the bigger the gain, because the item-specific memory driving it fades with time. In the selection studies the typical gap was only 20 days, which sits at the high-gain end of the range, while the interval after which the effect washes out runs to years rather than months.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.87.2.243](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.87.2.243) | [https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373) | [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003)
- **Subcategory 3.6: A retest score can end up measuring something different from the first score**
  - **Source:** Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad (2007); Van Iddekinge & Arnold (2017); Scharfen et al. (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a high-stakes setting (**N = 941**), a latent-variable analysis — a statistical model of the underlying abilities behind the observed scores — found **no measurement bias at initial testing, but retesting induced both measurement and predictive bias**: the retest factor was **less saturated with g** (the general-ability factor common to all cognitive tests) **and more associated with memory**, and these changes **eliminated the test's criterion-related validity**, its ability to predict the outcome it was built to predict. This is an adjacent, non-K–8 setting. `[T1]`
      - There is direct evidence that **retesting can change the constructs test scores reflect**. Again from adult work settings rather than from children. `[T1]`
      - The Scharfen meta-analysis isolated "pure" retesting: it **excluded any sample with a systematic between-test activity** (coaching, training, or medical intervention) and restricted itself to **sample mean ages 12–70** — that is, **children under 12 were excluded** to avoid confounding retest gains with cognitive development. Consequently no practice-effect meta-analysis covering the K–8 / ages 5–11 range was located; this evidence base stops short of the target age. `[T1]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Retesting can change what the score actually measures. In one high-stakes sample it introduced both measurement and predictive bias, with the retest score drifting away from general ability and toward memory, and that drift wiped out the test's ability to predict anything useful. Compounding the problem, the main practice-effect meta-analysis deliberately excluded children under 12, so this whole body of evidence stops short of the age range that matters here.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672)
- **Subcategory 3.7: Part of any retest gain is a statistical illusion rather than real improvement *(cross-ref GA 2.2)***
  - **Source:** Van Iddekinge & Arnold (2017); companion GA 2.2 (Lohman & Korb, 2006; Warne, 2012)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across studies, the share of a retest gain attributable to **regression toward the mean** — the tendency of an unusually high or low score to land closer to average next time, because part of any extreme score reflects a good or bad day rather than lasting ability — **varies widely: ≈ 9%** (two Hausknecht primary studies), **25–30%** (Feinberg, medical credentialing), **44%** (Van Iddekinge, job-knowledge), **55%** (Raymond, certification), **≈ 60%** (Topp). These are adult work and credentialing settings, adjacent rather than K–8. `[T1]`
      - Regression effects are **larger when selection ratios are more lenient** — that is, when a larger share of applicants is accepted — because the applicants who initially failed and come back to retest are then a more extreme, lower-scoring group. Adult applicant settings again. `[T1]`
      - The specific instability of **extreme (gifted-tail) scorers on re-administration** — roughly half of top-3% scorers fall out of the top 3% the next year, and only about 35–40% remain top-3% from grade 3 to grade 8 — is owned by the companion brainlift **(GA 2.2; Lohman & Korb, 2006; Warne, 2012)** and is **not re-derived here**. `[T1]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A highly variable 9–60% of a measured retest gain is regression to the mean rather than genuine improvement, and the effect bites hardest on extreme scorers who come back to try again — which compounds the tail instability already documented at the gifted cut.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349)
- **Subcategory 3.8: Deciding whether one child's score really changed, rather than just wobbled**
  - **Source:** Jacobson & Truax (1991)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The **Reliable Change Index** asks whether the difference between one person's two scores is larger than measurement error alone would produce: **RC = (x₂ − x₁) / S_diff**, where **S_diff = √2 × SE** and **SE = SD × √(1 − r_xx)** — SE being the standard error of measurement, SD the standard deviation of scores, and r_xx the test's reliability. **|RC| > 1.96** means the change is unlikely (p < .05) to be due to measurement error alone. The statistic originated in clinical psychology, an adjacent field rather than educational selection, but it is domain-general for individual change. `[T2]`
      - Jacobson & Truax pair the Reliable Change Index with a second criterion — crossing a cutoff that separates two distributions — giving a **two-part definition of meaningful individual change**: reliable *and* threshold-crossing. Clinical origin again, adjacent to this setting. `[T2]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Whether an individual's score truly changed should be judged against a defensible threshold — the Reliable Change Index, |RC| > 1.96, paired with actually crossing a meaningful cutoff — rather than by eyeballing a raw point difference.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12)
- **Subcategory 3.9: Retakes do not help every group equally, so an unmanaged retake right can widen gaps**
  - **Source:** Schleicher, Van Iddekinge, Morgeson & Campion (2010)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across **written tests (N = 7,031)** and **performance tests (N = 2,060)**, **White applicants showed significantly larger retest gains than Black, Hispanic, and Asian applicants on all three written tests** (for example, the verbal-ability gain was d = **0.12** for White applicants against **0.04** Black, **0.06** Hispanic, and **0.04** Asian); **women and applicants under 40 gained more** than men and applicants 40 and over; and the differential White advantage was **larger on the written tests**, which lean more heavily on general ability, **than on performance tests**. The practical implication: **allowing retakes can *exacerbate* adverse impact** — a substantially lower pass rate for one group than another. These are adult job applicants, an adjacent population rather than K–8 children. `[T1]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Retest gains differ by race, gender, and age, and they were largest for the group already scoring highest. An unmanaged right to retake therefore risks widening subgroup gaps rather than closing them.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018920](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018920)
- **Subcategory 3.10: What the professional rulebook requires when a test sorts children into categories**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014), Standards 2.16, 2.14, 13.2, 10.7, ch. 12
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **When a test or composite is used for classification decisions, provide estimates of the percentage of test takers who would be classified the same way on two replications**; decision consistency "can and should be estimated directly through the use of a **test–retest approach**, if consistent with … test security and if the assumption of no change in the construct is met." *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 2.16** + comment.)* `[T2]`
      - Report the **conditional standard error of measurement in the vicinity of each cut score** — the width of the error bar right at the line where the decision is made, rather than averaged over everybody who took the test. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 2.14**.)* `[T2]`
      - **When change/gain scores are used, report the construction procedure, technical qualities, and the time between administrations, and "care should be taken to avoid practice effects"**; the error of a change score exceeds the error of the scores it is built from. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 13.2** + comment.)* `[T2]`
      - Test takers must be told **before testing "whether the test taker will have an opportunity to retest, and under what circumstances retesting could occur."** *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, **Standard 10.7** comment; stated in the psychological-assessment chapter, but the disclosure principle is general.)* `[T2]`
      - In high-stakes decisions about students, fairness "can be enhanced by … providing students with **multiple opportunities** to demonstrate their capabilities." *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, ch. 12, educational-testing fairness discussion.)* `[T2]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Standards treat repeated measurement as a decision-quality tool that arrives with documentation duties: estimate how consistently the test classifies the same child (ideally by test–retest), report the error bar right at the cut, report the interval and guard against practice effects whenever gain scores are used, and disclose the retake policy before the child sits down.

### Category 4: Presentation & interaction design

- **Subcategory 4.1: Is the reading a text-only test demands part of what you are measuring, or is it noise?**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014); Haladyna & Downing (2004); Abedi & Lord (2001)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The 2014 *Standards* define **construct-irrelevant variance** as the "degree to which test scores are affected by processes that are extraneous to the test's intended construct" — in plainer terms, score movement caused by something other than the ability you meant to measure. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014 Standards; T2.)*
      - Standard 3.0 states all steps of the testing process "should be designed in such a manner as to **minimize construct-irrelevant variance**"; Standard 3.2 makes developers responsible for "minimizing the potential for tests' being affected by construct-irrelevant characteristics, such as **linguistic, communicative, cognitive, cultural, physical, or other characteristics**." *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014 Standards, pp. 63–64; T2.)*
      - Whether a given demand — reading, for instance — is "construct-relevant" or "construct-irrelevant" is defined **relative to the intended construct definition**, not in the abstract. *(Haladyna & Downing, 2004, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2004.tb00149.x — a taxonomy of construct-irrelevant variance inside a unitary validity framework; T1.)*
      - The same definitional logic runs through the 2014 *Standards*. *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014; T2.)*
      - Empirically, when the intended construct was **mathematics**, reducing the **linguistic complexity** of released NAEP items produced significantly higher scores; English-language learners, low-SES students, and students in low or average math classes benefited most (n = 1,174 8th graders). The authors present this as evidence that linguistic demand was construct-**irrelevant** variance *for the math construct*. The study is grade 8 math achievement with an English-language-learner focus, an adjacent setting rather than a K–8 text-only reasoning screener. *(Abedi & Lord, 2001, Applied Measurement in Education 14(3):219–234, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1403_2; T1.)*
      - The 2014 *Standards* distinguish **accommodations** (change the delivery while keeping the intended construct, so scores are meant to stay comparable) from **modifications** (change the intended construct, so scores are not necessarily comparable). *(AERA/APA/NCME, 2014 Standards; T2.)*

      > Note (a boundary marker, not an interpretation): whether *defining reading into the construct* converts the Abedi-type "reading = CIV" finding — CIV being construct-irrelevant variance — into "reading = construct-relevant" is a **design and definitional stance**, not an empirical result — no source settling it was located. Recorded under Tensions, not asserted here.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** For a text-only screener the working stance is that reading counts as part of what is being measured, while every other presentation factor — interface, gesture, device, delivery mode — is construct-irrelevant variance to be minimised and evidenced (4.2 through 4.6). That stance is a design choice rather than a finding: whether a demand such as reading is construct-relevant or construct-irrelevant is defined relative to the construct you declare, and the firm obligation the *Standards* impose is simply that whatever you do not intend to measure must be minimised.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2004.tb00149.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2004.tb00149.x) | [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1403_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1403_2)
- **Subcategory 4.2: Letting children go back and change answers is mostly banned — but the evidence is friendlier than the ban suggests**
  - **Source:** Han (2013); Vispoel, Hendrickson & Bleiler (2000); Revuelta, Ximénez & Olea (2003); Olea et al. (2000)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Most adaptive-testing programs do not allow test takers to review and change responses**, because unrestricted review "could seriously deteriorate the efficiency of measurement and make tests vulnerable to manipulative test-taking strategies" — the "Wainer-like" trick of deliberately answering early questions wrong so the software serves up easier ones, then going back to fix them. The evidence comes from high-stakes adult adaptive testing rather than from children. *(Han, 2013, Applied Psychological Measurement 37(4):259–275, DOI 10.1177/0146621612473638; T1.)*
      - When review is **restricted to items within successive blocks**, live testing showed **no trustworthy differences** across review conditions in proficiency estimates, measurement error, or testing time; more answers changed wrong→right than right→wrong, and most examinees who changed answers improved their estimate. This was adult vocabulary adaptive testing, an adjacent population. *(Vispoel, Hendrickson & Bleiler, 2000, Journal of Educational Measurement 37(1):21–38, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2000.tb01074.x; T1.)*
      - Across administration types (adaptive, easy-adaptive, fixed) and four review conditions, **response review improved ability estimates and increased testing time**, and did not degrade estimation precision beyond the test-type differences already present. Adults again, n = 557 undergraduates. *(Revuelta, Ximénez & Olea, 2003, Educational and Psychological Measurement, DOI 10.1177/0013164403251282; T1.)*
      - **The anxiety effect of review is not consistently replicated.** One study found state anxiety — anxiety felt in the moment — **decreased** in review conditions and slightly increased without review; the sample was adults. *(Olea, Revuelta, Ximénez & Abad, 2000, Psicológica 21:157–173; T1.)*
      - A later study by an overlapping team found **no psychological effect on anxiety** from review, again in adults. And no study measuring item-review anxiety effects in **K–8 children** was located. *(Revuelta, Ximénez & Olea, 2003, DOI 10.1177/0013164403251282; T1.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Adaptive tests switch review off by default to protect measurement efficiency and to block a known gaming strategy. But when review is restricted to one block of questions at a time, the measured consequences are benign or positive: precision holds up, estimates tend to rise, and children fix more wrong answers than they break right ones. The anxiety story is inconsistent between studies, and has never been examined in K–8 children at all.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621612473638](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621612473638) | [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2000.tb01074.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2000.tb01074.x) | [https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164403251282](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164403251282)
- **Subcategory 4.3: How a child answers — choosing an option, producing an answer, tapping, dragging — is its own design decision**
  - **Source:** Rodriguez (2003); Bhavnani et al. (2019); Passell et al. (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a meta-analysis of 67 studies (56 disattenuated correlations — correlations adjusted upward to strip out the blurring caused by each measure's own error), **multiple-choice (selected-response) items, where the child picks from options supplied, and constructed-response items, where the child produces the answer, measured essentially the same construct when they were stem-equivalent** — that is, when the question itself was identical and only the answering mode differed (mean disattenuated correlation approaching unity, ≈ .95). They diverged when non-stem-equivalent (≈ .85–.86, lowest for essay-type constructed response). The samples are mixed ages, largely not young children. *(Rodriguez, 2003, Journal of Educational Measurement 40(2):163–184, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2003.tb01102.x; T1.)*
      - In young children, **gesture type is not equally easy**: in DEEP piloting, tapping "came intuitively to the majority of children (9/10)," whereas **drag-and-drop was more difficult for children without prior smartphone exposure and required practice to master**. The developers therefore **clustered games by response type (tapping first, then drag-and-drop)** to avoid repeated gesture-switching. The children were rural Indian preschoolers with an assessor present, an adjacent setting rather than an unsupervised K–8 screener. *(Bhavnani et al., 2019, Global Health Action 12:1548005, DOI 10.1080/16549716.2018.1548005; T1 pilot.)*
      - Cross-domain corroboration that interface *input mode* is a distinct source of variance: device differences (screen size, mouse versus touchscreen) produced significant performance differences on tasks "requiring fast reactions or fine motor movements," while an **untimed vocabulary measure was relatively unaffected**. **Conflict of Interest:** the senior author directs or advises nonprofit assessment organisations, with no financial compensation reported. The sample is adults online, adjacent rather than K–8. *(Passell, … & Germine, 2021, Behavior Research Methods 53(6):2544–2557, DOI 10.3758/s13428-021-01597-3; N = 59,587; T1.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Whether a child picks an answer or produces one barely matters for what actually gets measured, as long as the underlying question is the same. What does matter is the physical gesture: tapping is intuitive for nearly every young child, while drag-and-drop is genuinely harder for those without prior touchscreen exposure and has to be taught before it is scored.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2003.tb01102.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2003.tb01102.x) | [https://doi.org/10.1080/16549716.2018.1548005](https://doi.org/10.1080/16549716.2018.1548005) | [https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-021-01597-3](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-021-01597-3)
- **Subcategory 4.4: Phone-versus-computer score gaps are real, but roughly half as large as they first appear**
  - **Source:** Traylor et al. (2021); Brown, Grossenbacher & Warman (2023); Passell et al. (2021); Way et al.
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A meta-analysis reported that in **operational (real applicant) samples**, non-mobile test takers scored on average **0.79 standard deviations higher** on general-mental-ability tests than mobile test takers (15 operational samples), but in **non-operational samples the device effect was negligible (d = 0.05)**. The exact venue and DOI of this meta-analysis were not located, so the citation remains unverified, though the magnitude is corroborated by at least two independent sources. The population is adult employment selection, adjacent rather than K–8. *(Traylor et al., 2021, as reported and corroborated in Brown, Grossenbacher & Warman, 2023; T1 meta-analysis.)*
      - Directly testing that gap: before adjustment, non-mobile applicants scored **0.58 standard deviations** higher; after **propensity-score weighting** — a statistical adjustment that compares like with like, to strip out who chose which device — **the device effect shrank by over 50% to d = 0.25** (and smaller still under the average-treatment-on-matched estimate). The authors conclude operational device effects are likely inflated by **self-selection**, and recommend **random assignment** to establish device effects for any new or adapted test. The sample is adults. *(Brown, Grossenbacher & Warman, 2023, Journal of Applied Psychology 108(7):1190–1206, DOI 10.1037/apl0001067; T1.)*
      - Device effects concentrate in **speeded, reaction-time, and fine-motor** measurement, not in untimed ability measures. Adults again, with the same conflict of interest noted above. *(Passell, … & Germine, 2021, DOI 10.3758/s13428-021-01597-3; T1.)*
      - Practitioner framing of "form factor": desktop and laptop scores are generally comparable because the form factor is similar, but tablets differ — smaller screens limit how much is visible at once, and input is by finger — so tablet-to-computer comparability should not be assumed without evidence. This is practitioner and conference material from an adjacent setting rather than peer-reviewed K–8 evidence. *(Way, Davis, Keng & Strain-Seymour, device-comparability paper, NCME/ACARA; T3 practitioner/conference.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The headline device gap — non-mobile test takers scoring far higher — is real in live applicant data, but roughly halves once you account for the fact that people choose their own device. What is left concentrates in speeded and fine-motor tasks rather than untimed reasoning. The practical rule is that comparability across devices has to be established, ideally by randomly assigning the device, rather than assumed.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001067](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001067) | [https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-021-01597-3](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-021-01597-3)
- **Subcategory 4.6: A practice phase can cancel out prior tech experience — but the one study that showed it used a human helper**
  - **Source:** Mukherjee et al. (2020); companion GA 7.7
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In the gamified preschool assessment **DEEP**, prediction accuracy was **not impacted by prior exposure to touchscreen games**: of 200 children, 87 (43.5%) had no prior touchscreen-game experience, yet the gap between DEEP's prediction and the reference developmental assessment (BSID) did **not differ significantly** between the prior-exposure and no-exposure groups (unpaired t-test **p = 0.28**, nowhere near the conventional .05 threshold for significance). The authors attribute this to "the ease of play engineered during DEEP's design, as well as the addition of a 'demo' phase which allowed children to practice playing the games before data was collected." Performance was recorded only during play-mode. *(Mukherjee et al., 2020, Frontiers in Psychology 11:1202, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01202; T1 proof-of-concept.)*
      - **Critical constraint-relevant caveat:** DEEP's demo phase was **not wordless and not self-administered**. "The assessor delivers **verbal instructions** during a demo-mode," teaches and lets the child practice "with help from the assessor if required," and "in cases where children are not able to follow verbal instructions … the assessor **holds the child's index finger to guide** him/her" on the tap or drag gesture. Therefore this study does **not** demonstrate that a **text-only, no-audio, fully-automated self-teaching demo** (the text-only, automated-scoring requirement) neutralizes prior-exposure bias; it demonstrates that **human verbal and physical scaffolding** did. That leaves a real hole in the evidence: no study of a fully automated, text-only demo was located, and the DEEP result itself comes from an adjacent, assessor-supported setting rather than the unsupervised one being designed for. *(Mukherjee et al., 2020, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01202; T1.)*
      - The general principle that an assessment should **teach the task format before scoring** (demonstration, sample, and teaching items) so that "did not understand the task/controls" is not scored as low ability is established at the measurement-quality level and is **not re-derived here** (see Cross-references, **GA 7.7**).
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The strongest evidence that a demo phase neutralizes prior-exposure bias comes from DEEP — but DEEP's demo used a human assessor giving verbal instructions and, where needed, physically guiding the child's finger. That does not validate a text-only, no-audio, fully-automated self-teaching demo, which is what a screener under those constraints would actually ship. It is a genuine evidence gap.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01202](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01202)

### Category 5: Cognitive load & working memory

- **Subcategory 5.1: A child has one small pool of mental effort, and the way a question is presented draws on the same pool as the question itself**
  - **Source:** Sweller (1988); Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (1998/2019); Sweller (2010); AERA/APA/NCME (2014), ch. 3 'Fairness in Testing'
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Working memory" is the small amount of information a person can actively hold and juggle at one moment. It is severely limited when it is handling **novel** information, so task and instructional design should aim that limited capacity at the thing being measured and away from incidental processing. This evidence comes from research on learning and instruction rather than from scored testing, and it was produced by the theory's own originators.
      - Cognitive load theory, the framework behind all of this, names the kinds of demand that compete for that pool. **Intrinsic load** is the number of pieces of a problem that have to be held in mind at the same time because they only make sense together — the field's name for this is **element interactivity** — judged *relative to the solver's existing schemas*, meaning what that particular person already knows. It is therefore the task's inherent difficulty for that person. **Extraneous load** is element interactivity created by *how* the material is presented rather than by the task itself. In the 2019 reconceptualization, **germane load** is *not* a third additive source of demand but the working-memory resources actually devoted to the essential (intrinsic) interactivity. Learning-and-instruction research again, written by the theory's proponents.
      - Intrinsic and extraneous load draw on the **same fixed working-memory budget**; when their sum exceeds capacity, performance and learning degrade. Learning-and-instruction evidence rather than scored-testing evidence.
      - The AERA/APA/NCME (2014) *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* — the joint manual issued by the three main professional bodies in American educational measurement — place **fairness** in the foundational tier, parallel to validity and reliability. An **overarching standard** is that the **entire testing process be designed to minimize construct-irrelevant variance**, meaning score movement caused by something other than the ability the test set out to measure. **Accessibility** is defined as "the *unobstructed opportunity* for all examinees to demonstrate their standing on the construct(s) being measured," and test design should reduce barriers through universal design. The fairness chapter's examples **explicitly include young children.** (Ch. 3, "Fairness in Testing"; ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.) `T2`, and directly on point rather than borrowed from an adjacent field.
      - **The bridge:** extraneous cognitive load from the interface or the instructions is, in *measurement* language, a source of **construct-irrelevant variance** and an **access barrier**. That equivalence is the mechanism by which cognitive load theory — a theory about learning — attaches to a *scored* screener at all.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A fixed, limited working memory has to serve both intrinsic load (the essential element interactivity of the question) and extraneous load (whatever the presentation adds), and performance degrades once their sum exceeds capacity. This is the engine driving the rest of the category: it is what lets you separate a scored task's essential, construct-relevant difficulty from removable presentation artifacts, and it is why trimming presentation clutter is a measurement decision rather than a cosmetic one. In measurement language that extraneous load *is* construct-irrelevant variance and an access barrier — the equivalence that lets a learning theory attach to a scored screener at all — and the *Standards* make minimizing it a foundational fairness obligation that explicitly includes young children.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4) | [https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022193728205](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022193728205) | [https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5) | [https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9128-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9128-5) | [https://www.testingstandards.net/](https://www.testingstandards.net/)
- **Subcategory 5.2: The strongest known way to unload a crowded screen is to speak part of it aloud — and a text-only rule forbids exactly that**
  - **Source:** Chandler & Sweller (1991, 1992); Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller (1999); Ginns (2005)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Split-attention effect:** when two sources of information refer to each other but are separated in space or time — a diagram in one place and the text explaining it in another — the learner has to hold one in mind while hunting for the other. That raises extraneous load and lowers outcomes compared with a physically **integrated** presentation that puts them together. Adding *seemingly useful but nonessential* explanatory material can itself be deleterious. Learning-and-instruction evidence rather than scored-testing evidence.
      - **Redundancy effect:** presenting the *same* information in two forms at once — identical spoken **and** on-screen text, for instance — imposes load from the duplicate stream and can lower outcomes; removing the redundant source helps. Learning-and-instruction evidence.
      - **Modality effect:** distributing essential information across the **visual + auditory** channels (graphics plus *spoken* text) outperforms putting all of it in the eyes (graphics plus *printed* text). A meta-analysis — a study that statistically pools the results of many separate studies — of 43 independent effects supports it, with larger effects for material of high element interactivity and for presentation paced by the system rather than by the learner. Learning-and-instruction evidence.
      - **Applied note.** The single most powerful classical remedy for split-attention and high element interactivity is **off-loading text to the auditory channel** — the modality effect. Under **the text-only design (text-only, never audio)** that remedy is **foreclosed by design**. Because item stems, options, *and* stimuli must all compete for the **visual** channel, the split-attention and redundancy risk is if anything **heightened**, and extraneous-load control must fall back on the remaining visual-only levers: physically integrating things that refer to each other, eliminating redundancy, segmenting so only one thing appears at a time, and pre-teaching formats. This is a reasoned extension of Ginns (2005), Kalyuga (1999), and Chandler & Sweller (1991) applied to the text-only design, not something those sources state directly.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Three well-established remedies reduce presentation-created load: put mutually referring things in the same place (split-attention), delete anything said twice (redundancy), and move some of the words from the eye to the ear (modality). The strongest of the three, offloading text to audio, is foreclosed by a text-only rule, which if anything intensifies split-attention risk because everything now competes for one visual channel.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0804_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0804_2) | [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1992.tb01017.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1992.tb01017.x) | [https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0720(199908)13:4<351::AID-ACP589>3.0.CO;2-6](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0720(199908)13:4<351::AID-ACP589>3.0.CO;2-6) | [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2005.07.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2005.07.001)
- **Subcategory 5.3: A mind holds about four things at once, not the famous seven**
  - **Source:** Miller (1956); Cowan (2001)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "chunk" is one meaningful unit rather than one symbol: the digits 4, 7, 1 are three chunks, but "471" is a single chunk to someone who already recognizes it as a whole.
      - Immediate-memory span is about **7 ± 2 "chunks"** on Miller's classic estimate; capacity is counted in *chunks*, and what counts as a chunk **depends on the person's prior knowledge**. This is general cognitive science measured on adults.
      - When chunking and rehearsal (silently repeating items to keep them alive) are experimentally blocked, the **"pure" capacity limit is ~4 chunks (range ≈ 3–5)** — materially lower than 7. General cognitive science on adults, so carrying it over to children is an extension to an adjacent population.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Working memory holds only about four chunks once chunking and rehearsal are blocked — materially fewer than the classic 7±2 — and what counts as a chunk depends on prior knowledge. The practical ceiling on how many separate pieces a question may ask a child to juggle is therefore closer to four than to seven, and it shifts with what that child already knows.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158) | [https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922)
- **Subcategory 5.4: Working memory keeps growing right through the K–8 years, so one fixed design is not one fixed difficulty**
  - **Source:** Gathercole, Pickering, Ambridge & Wearing (2004); Baddeley & Hitch (1974)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across ages **4–15**, the three-component structure of working memory — a **central executive** that directs attention, a **phonological loop** that holds words and sounds, and a **visuospatial sketchpad** that holds shapes and positions, the model proposed by Baddeley & Hitch in 1974 — is identifiable **from age 6**, and **each component's functional capacity increases roughly linearly from early childhood through adolescence.** This study sits directly on the K–8 age range rather than being borrowed from adults.
      - **Drawn from the same data:** the K–8 band straddles a period of **large working-memory growth**, so a *fixed* item or interface complexity imposes a **different relative load** on a kindergartener than on an 8th-grader. Also directly on point.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Working-memory capacity grows roughly linearly across the K–8 span, so a fixed item and interface complexity imposes a different relative load on a kindergartener than on an eighth-grader. One screen delivered unchanged across the whole band is not one difficulty; it is a light demand at the top of the range and potentially an over-the-limit demand at the bottom.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.40.2.177](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.40.2.177)
- **Subcategory 5.5: Reasoning and working memory are so entangled that memory demand cannot be stripped out of a reasoning test**
  - **Source:** Kyllonen & Christal (1990); Hagemann et al. (2023); Sweller (2010)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Reasoning ability and working-memory capacity, estimated as underlying (latent) factors rather than as single test scores, **correlate r ≈ .80–.90** across four adult samples (N ≈ 723, 412, 415, 594) — close enough to be very nearly the same thing. Adult evidence, so applying it to children is an extension to an adjacent population.
      - **Experimentally** loading the central executive while adults worked through the Advanced Progressive Matrices — a standard nonverbal reasoning test — **causally lowered** matrix performance, accounting for ~15% of the variation in those scores. Yet working memory did **not** explain all of the variation in fluid reasoning, the ability to solve novel problems without leaning on prior knowledge, so fluid reasoning is *more than* working memory. Adult evidence again.
      - **A reasoned extension of the published findings, not something the sources state directly:** for fluid-reasoning items, a substantial share of the working-memory demand is **intrinsic and construct-relevant** — it cannot be removed without changing what is being measured. Only **extraneous** load (interface, controls, instructions) is cleanly reducible. Synthesis of Kyllonen & Christal (1990), Hagemann et al. (2023), and Sweller (2010).
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Fluid reasoning is itself heavily working-memory-loaded (r ≈ .80–.90), so working-memory demand cannot be purged from a reasoning test without changing the construct the test is measuring. "Measure reasoning, not working memory or load" is therefore only partly achievable: only extraneous load from the interface and the instructions is cleanly reducible.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1016/S0160-2896(05)80012-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0160-2896(05)80012-1) | [https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence11040070](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence11040070)
- **Subcategory 5.6: The harder an adaptive test pushes, the less room is left for anything else — and help that suits a beginner starts hurting an expert**
  - **Source:** Sweller (2010); Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Because intrinsic load scales with element interactivity, **harder adaptive items inherently impose more working-memory load**; as difficulty adapts upward, the working-memory "headroom" left over to absorb any extraneous load **shrinks**. This is an adjacent-domain finding carried across by reasoned extension rather than a direct result.
      - **Expertise reversal effect:** load-reducing supports such as worked examples and extra guidance, which help low-knowledge learners, **lose effectiveness or become harmful** for higher-knowledge learners, because guidance they no longer need turns into one more thing to process. Learning-and-instruction evidence produced by the theory's proponents.
      - **A reasoned extension, not a stated finding:** a *fixed* scaffold or instruction level is **not load-neutral** across the wide ability range an adaptive test deliberately targets; and front-loading high-intrinsic-load items leaves no low-load warm-up in which to build a mental model of the format first (see GA 7.7 on teaching items; the ordering detail itself belongs to Cat 2). Extension of Kalyuga et al. (2003) and Sweller (2010).
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The reducible headroom shrinks precisely as adaptivity pushes items harder, and a fixed scaffold is not load-neutral across the wide ability range an adaptive test targets — that second point is the expertise-reversal effect, where support that helps a novice becomes dead weight for an expert.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3801_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3801_4)
- **Subcategory 5.8: Making a task harder can genuinely improve long-term learning — but a screener is not measuring learning**
  - **Source:** Bjork & Bjork (2011); Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Desirable difficulties** are obstacles deliberately introduced during practice: spreading practice out over time (spacing), mixing topics instead of blocking them (interleaving), varying the conditions of practice, and using tests and acts of recall as study events. They **reduce performance *during* acquisition but improve long-term retention and transfer.** They are "desirable" **only if** the learner has the background to respond successfully; otherwise they are "**undesirable** difficulties." The source is E. L. Bjork & R. A. Bjork, 2011, "Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way," in *Psychology and the Real World*, Worth, pp. 56–64, with the term originating in R. A. Bjork, 1994; no DOI exists for it, because it is a book chapter. Learning-and-instruction evidence written by the approach's proponents.
      - **Learning is not performance:** learning (a durable, latent change in capability) and performance (current, observable behavior) are **dissociable**, and some manipulations have **opposite** effects on the two; **current performance is therefore an unreliable index of learning.** Learning-and-instruction evidence.
      - **The tension this seeds, deliberately left unresolved here:** a gifted screener is a **one-shot performance and measurement** event that must read *current standing* accurately — it is **not** a learning event. The regime in which desirable difficulties pay off, durable learning measured *later*, is **not** the regime the screener operates in. This is carried up to the insight layer as a live tension rather than settled in the knowledge tree.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Desirable difficulties improve durable learning measured later but depress current performance, and since a one-shot screener must read current standing rather than learning, it sits in the opposite regime — a live tension, not a resolution.

### Category 6: Session design & developmental fit

- **Subcategory 6.1: A long test is not automatically worse — but children score lower the later in the day they are tested**
  - **Source:** Ackerman & Kanfer (2009); Sievertsen, Gino & Piovesan (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a within-subject experiment, meaning the same people sat every condition (N=239 first-year university students), SAT batteries of 3.5, 4.5, and 5.5 hr were compared. **Subjective fatigue rose monotonically with time-on-task — how long a person has been working continuously — but mean *performance* did not fall; it was slightly *higher* in the longer conditions** (≈ +15 points for the 5.5-hr session versus the shorter ones), and clusters of personality, interest, and motivation traits predicted fatigue better than test length did. The participants were motivated, incentivized **adults** rather than children, so this establishes that "long ≠ automatically worse data" only where effort is high — which is not guaranteed in a low-stakes child screener (see 6.2).
      - Using the **full population** of Danish public-school children (ages 8–15, 2009/10–2012/13), **each additional hour later in the day reduced test performance by 0.9% of an SD (95% CI 0.7–1.0%)** — an SD, or standard deviation, being the typical spread of scores across the whole population — and **low-performing students were hurt more** by accumulated fatigue. T1. No study covering the K-1 band was located: the sample starts at age 8, so this evidence is weakest for the K-1 band (ages 5–6).
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Motivated adults tolerate very long batteries without a performance drop, but in a full child population scores fall ≈0.9% SD per hour later in the day and low performers are hurt more — so for a low-stakes child screener, "too long" fails through disengagement rather than through classical exhaustion. That is why session design should follow measured effort and the developmental attention gradient rather than a fixed clock.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015719](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015719) | [https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113)
- **Subcategory 6.2: Children stop trying as a test goes on, and a guessed answer carries no information about ability**
  - **Source:** Wise (2006); Wise & Kong (2005); Wise, Pastor & Kong (2009); Wise (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On a low-stakes computer-based test, **the two strongest predictors of the effort an item received were item length (the amount of reading and scanning it demanded) and item *position*** — items later in the test received less effort. Treating rapid-guess responses as missing **lowered reliability but raised validity**; that is, the score became slightly less repeatable but a better measure of the ability it was after. T1. The sample was older students in a low-stakes setting rather than K–8 children.
      - **Response Time Effort (RTE)** turns response times into a judgment about whether a child was genuinely solving an item or merely clicking through — "solution behavior vs. rapid-guessing". Rapid-guessed items are answered too fast for the item to have been read, and are **correct only at chance rates**. T1.
      - Modeling which examinees and which items produce rapid guessing, using a hierarchical generalized linear model (HGLM) that separates person-level from item-level effects: **items with more text, or occurring later in the test, produced more rapid guessing; adding a graphic reduced it.** T1. The sole examinee predictor available was SAT score, so this too is a college sample rather than a K–8 one.
      - A rapid guess is framed as the examinee **"opt[ing] out of being measured"; it carries no ability signal and distorts scores regardless of high- or low-stakes context**, so scoring it "makes little sense." T1.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Effort declines and rapid-guessing rises with item length and later position; rapid guesses carry no ability signal, so scoring them makes little sense and treating them as missing raised validity.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1902_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1902_2) | [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2) | [https://doi.org/10.1080/08957340902754650](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957340902754650) | [https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12165](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12165)
- **Subcategory 6.3: One well-placed break buys back more than an hour of fatigue, but a break every hour is a net loss**
  - **Source:** Sievertsen, Gino & Piovesan (2016); AERA/APA/NCME (2014), ch. 6
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In the same Danish population, **a 20-to-30-minute break improved subsequent performance by 1.7% of an SD (95% CI 1.2–2.2%) — a gain *larger* than the ≈0.9%/hr fatigue decrement**. The authors caution that **a break after *every* hour nets negative**, because the loss accumulated in an hour is smaller than the cost of one break's setup, so breaks should be placed to recharge rather than to fragment. T1.
      - Standardized-administration conditions — **including timing, breaks, and instructions — are part of the standardization that must be defined, held constant, and documented**; departures can threaten score comparability, meaning whether two children's scores still mean the same thing. T2. (Ch. 6, "Test Administration, Scoring, Reporting, and Interpretation.") Interacts with 6.7 (multi-sitting) and **(GA 7.7)**.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A single 20–30-minute break restores more than an hour's fatigue loss, but breaking too often nets negative, so segmentation should recharge rather than fragment — and timing and breaks are part of documented standardization.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113)
- **Subcategory 6.4: Sustained attention grows sharply from age 5 to 9 and then levels off**
  - **Source:** Betts, McKay, Maruff & Anderson (2006); Ruff & Capozzoli (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In children 5–12 (N=57), **sustained attention — the ability to keep concentrating on one task over time — develops rapidly from 5–6 to 8–9 years and then plateaus from 8–9 to 11–12** (only minor further gains, i.e., roughly adult-like by ~age 10); **high-load tasks produced poorer performance than low-load at every age, with the same developmental trajectory.** T1. **Conflict of Interest:** author Maruff is affiliated with CogState, which makes the instrument used; the age-plateau pattern is corroborated by the study below.
      - Observational study (N=172 at 10, 26, 42 months): **focused attention increases and casual/distractible attention decreases with age**, i.e., a steep early developmental gradient in the capacity to sustain and defend attention. T1. These children are below school age, so the study establishes the direction of the age gradient rather than values for the K–8 band.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Sustained attention climbs steeply from ages 5–6 to 8–9 and then plateaus (roughly adult-like by ~age 10), and high-load tasks depress performance at every age.

- **Subcategory 6.5: The familiar rules of thumb about children's attention spans trace back to no real study**
  - **Source:** Bradbury (2016); Wilson & Korn (2007); Maybin/BBC News (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The popular rule that a child's attention span equals **"~2–3 minutes per year of age"** (or the softer "1 minute per year") is repeated across parenting and education outlets **as a rule of thumb with no identifiable peer-reviewed primary source** — one popular restatement is Times of India parenting, 2021. **T4**, and the rule remains unverified. It also contradicts the developmental evidence in 6.4, which shows attention is **task-, load-, interest-, and context-dependent**, not a fixed minutes-per-year constant.
      - The analogous classroom claim that **student attention "declines after 10–15 minutes" is not supported by primary data** — it traces to a single 1978 report, is never quantitatively defined, and studies using classroom clickers and direct observation show attention waxing and waning with **no reliable downward trend**; individual differences dominate. T1 (reviews).
      - The "human attention span is 8 seconds, less than a goldfish" statistic traces to a **2015 Microsoft Canada "Attention Spans" report citing "Statistic Brain," with no verifiable underlying study** (Maybin, BBC News, 2017 — "Busting the attention span myth."). **T4**, and the statistic remains unverified; it has been debunked.
      - **Net:** design to *measured* effort and attention — the response-time effort measure in 6.2, and response-time logging — and to the developmental gradient in 6.4, **not** to any fixed "minutes = age" or "10-minute" clock. Claims that remain unverified must not become config constants.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The 'attention span ≈ age in minutes,' '10–15-minute,' and '8-second' rules trace to no credible primary source and contradict the developmental evidence, so they must not be hard-coded as design constants.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016) | [https://doi.org/10.1080/00986280701291291](https://doi.org/10.1080/00986280701291291)
- **Subcategory 6.7: What actually limits each age band — touch precision for the youngest, and above all reading**
  - **Source:** Vatavu, Cramariuc & Schipor (2015); Hourcade (2008); Seymour, Aro & Erskine (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Motor/touch (K-1, 2-3):** In children 3–6 (N=89 children + 30 adults), **tap and drag-and-drop touch performance — how accurately they hit a target and how long it took — improved significantly with age and remained worse than adults**, and correlated with finger dexterity and with graphomotor (handwriting) and visuospatial ability. T1.
      - **Design synthesis for children, from Hourcade's canonical review "Interaction Design and Children":** interface and motor demands must be matched to developmental level; young children show high variability both within and between individuals and slower input, so targets, gestures, and visual complexity must be simplified. T2 (canonical review). Effort spent decoding controls is effort not spent reasoning — see **(GA 7.4)**.
      - **Reading (K-1 barrier under a text-only design):** in English, **word-reading accuracy is only ~34% in grade 1, rising to ~76% in grade 2, and English foundation literacy develops >2× slower than shallow orthographies** — languages whose spelling maps far more directly onto sound — needing ≥2 years of experience. T1. **Implication:** a **text-only** screen at K-1 largely measures decoding, not reasoning, for most 5–6-year-olds — a hard session-design and feasibility constraint, not an accommodation question. No verified per-band "max on-screen minutes" standard for young children was located.
      - **Attention band mapping (from 6.4):** 6-8 ≈ near-plateau, with the most adult-like sustained attention; 4-5 mid-development; 2-3 and especially K-1 carry the steepest fatigue and off-task risk together with the tightest motor and reading limits.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The binding K-1 constraints are touch and motor precision and — critically under a text-only design — English decoding, which is only ~34% accurate in grade 1, so a text-only screen at K-1 largely measures decoding rather than reasoning.

- **Subcategory 6.8: Spreading a test across days relieves fatigue only by adding a different kind of error**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014), ch. 6
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Within-day segmentation with a recharge break is net-positive** (6.3), but **splitting across separate sittings/days trades fatigue relief for occasion variance and standardization risk**: multiple occasions add test–retest and practice effects plus variation in administration conditions, all of which must be controlled and documented. T2. Cross-refs this brainlift's **Category 3** (retakes/occasions) and **(GA 4.8)**.
      - **Direction of evidence, not a settled number:** no study fixing an optimal number of sittings for a K–8 cognitive *screener* was located. 6.1 (motivated adults tolerate long single sittings) and 6.3 (children benefit from breaks) bound the tradeoff but do not resolve it.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Splitting a battery across sittings or days relieves fatigue only by adding occasion and standardization variance, and no located study fixes an optimal number of sittings for a K–8 screener.

### Category 7: Motivation, effort & engagement structure

- **Subcategory 7.1: Test anxiety measurably lowers K–8 scores — the baseline anchor for everything else in this category**
  - **Source:** von der Embse et al. (2018); Robson et al. (2023); companion *Gifted-Assessment Quality* BrainLift (GA) 7.1
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Test anxiety" is the worry and physical stress a child feels because they are being evaluated, as distinct from any real difficulty with the material. A "meta-analysis" pools the results of many separate studies into one overall figure, and *k* is the number of studies behind that figure. The relationship is reported as a correlation *r*, where the minus sign means more anxiety goes with lower scores.
      - Test anxiety is negatively associated with achievement in the target age band: **Grades 1–5 r ≈ −.22 (k = 9); Grades 6–8 r ≈ −.25 (k = 16)**, from a 238-study meta-analysis. *(T1; cross-ref **GA 7.1**.)*
      - A 20-year meta-analysis of primary-school children (ages 5–12; **N ≈ 53,617** per GA 7.1) confirms the negative anxiety–achievement link in exactly this population. *(T1; cross-ref **GA 7.1**.)*
      - **Load-bearing implication:** anxiety is a *known downward bias* on the score, so structure that lowers it can raise measurement fidelity **without** making the test easier. The full treatment lives in **GA 7.1**; here it is only the anchor for the structural levers below.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Anxiety pushes children's scores down by a measurable amount in exactly the grades this screen covers (a correlation of about −.22 to −.25), which makes it a known bias on the number rather than a comfort issue. Anything in the *structure* of the test that lowers anxiety can therefore make the score a more faithful reading of real ability without making the test any easier — that is the premise the rest of this category builds on.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.11.048](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.11.048) | [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2023.02.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2023.02.003)
- **Subcategory 7.2: A visible timer calmed children down — hidden, uncertain time pressure is what actually hurts**
  - **Source:** Hallez & Vallier (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Speededness" is the part of a score that reflects how fast a child works rather than how well they reason; it creeps in whenever the clock is tight enough that finishing becomes part of the challenge. Time pressure and whether the clock is *visible* are two separate design choices, and the study below pulls them apart.
      - **Time pressure is a documented anxiety amplifier**, and time-constrained assessment *without* a visible time cue produced higher evaluation anxiety and lower math scores in children ages 7–9. *(T1, **small n = 44**; the evidence comes from a timed math task in France rather than a gifted reasoning screen, so it is an adjacent setting.)*
      - **The visible timer *reduced* anticipatory anxiety** (versus no timer) and **reduced inattentive / motor-instability behavior** — fidgeting and difficulty staying settled — most of all for children at higher risk of ADHD, **with no significant change in performance**. Timer-checking varied widely between children (25% checked >7×/5 min). The mechanism the authors propose is that a visible timer *offloads* the cognitive load — the mental effort a task demands of a limited pool of working memory — of tracking the time in your head. *(T1, small n; again an adjacent population and setting.)*
      - **Direction-of-effect caveat:** this evidence *complicates* the intake hypothesis "no visible countdown timer." For young children the harmful element is **uncertain or hidden time pressure and speededness**, not the visibility of a calm timer as such. No study was located that isolates *no timer and no time limit* (fully untimed) against *a visible timer* for a gifted reasoning screen.
      - **Retry-friendly / mastery framing** — letting a child re-attempt an item or a section instead of getting one final shot — is offered as an anxiety lever mainly in practitioner sources. Its *psychometric* cost and benefit (practice effects, test security, and whether a score change is a real change) is a **retake-policy** question owned by **Category 3** — cross-ref, do not decide here. What evidence exists comes from adjacent populations and settings, and no study establishing that per-item retries lower anxiety without inflating scores in a K–8 gifted screen was located.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** For ages 7–9 a visible timer reduced anxiety and off-task behavior compared with hidden or uncertain time pressure, so the thing that does the damage is the time pressure itself and not knowing how much is left — not the sight of the clock. That inverts the intuitive "hide the timer" fix, though it rests on one small study in an adjacent setting, and the fully untimed option has never been tested against it.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15120243](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15120243)
- **Subcategory 7.3: Making a test feel low-stakes calms children down and also makes them try less — the central tension of this category**
  - **Source:** Wise & DeMars (2005); Finn (2015); AERA/APA/NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An "effect size" written as *d* expresses a gap in standard deviations — the typical spread of scores across a population — so *d* ≈ 0.6 is a large, plainly visible difference rather than a rounding error. "Construct-irrelevant variance" is score movement caused by something other than the ability you meant to measure: noise that behaves like bias.
      - **Lowering stakes lowers anxiety but also lowers effort.** A synthesis of 12 studies / 25 comparisons found motivated examinees outscored less-motivated examinees by an **average d ≈ 0.6**, and low motivation "is associated with a substantial decrease in test performance" and threatens validity. *(T1; the evidence is from higher education rather than K–8, so it is an adjacent population.)*
      - Unmotivated test-takers inject **construct-irrelevant variance (CIV)**; test-taking motivation is positively correlated with performance, and score interpretation is biased if motivation is ignored. *(T3 institutional; adjacent again — higher education and accountability testing rather than a children's screen.)*
      - **Construct-irrelevant variance is a formal validity threat, not a stylistic complaint.** The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* — the joint manual issued by the three main professional bodies in American educational measurement — define construct-irrelevance as "the degree to which test scores are affected by processes that are extraneous to" the construct, and name it (with construct underrepresentation) as one of the two principal threats to validity. *(T2.)*
      - **Extracts identically:** you cannot buy engagement by simply "making it low-stakes." A calm, low-anxiety frame must be paired with a **structural effort guarantee** (7.4–7.6) or effort itself becomes the leak.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the defining tension of the whole category: the obvious fix for anxiety — lowering the stakes — also lowers effort, so engagement has to be protected by the structure of the test rather than by softening what the test measures. Motivated test-takers outscore unmotivated ones by roughly six-tenths of a standard deviation (d ≈ 0.6), and that entire gap is construct-irrelevant variance, which the profession's own rulebook names as one of the two principal threats to a test's validity. A calm frame therefore has to arrive packaged with a structural guarantee that children are actually trying (7.4–7.6).
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326977ea1001_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326977ea1001_1) | [https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12067](https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12067)
- **Subcategory 7.4: You can tell from the clock when a child has stopped trying, without that telling you anything about their ability**
  - **Source:** Wise & Kong (2005); Wise (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Response time" (RT) is simply how long a child spends on a question before answering. "Rapid-guessing" is answering so fast that the child cannot have read the question, which means the answer carries no information about their ability; "solution behavior" is the opposite, spending long enough to have genuinely attempted it. "Response Time Effort" (RTE) turns that distinction into a single number for how much of the test a child actually tried.
      - **RTE** operationalizes effort from behavior, not self-report: for each item *i* a threshold **Tᵢ** separates *rapid-guessing* (RTᵢⱼ < Tᵢ → solution-behavior indicator SBᵢⱼ = 0) from *solution behavior* (RTᵢⱼ ≥ Tᵢ → SBᵢⱼ = 1) — in words, every answer is scored 1 if the child spent at least the threshold time on it and 0 if they did not. An examinee's **RTE = mean(SBᵢⱼ) over k items**, ranging 0–1 (1 = full effort), which is just the fraction of questions they genuinely attempted. In validation it showed internal consistency **α = .97** (the measure hangs together almost perfectly across items), converged with self-reported effort (**r = .25**) and with person-fit — how normal a test-taker's overall pattern of right and wrong answers looks (**r = −.42**) — and was **near-zero correlated with SAT** (discriminant — effort ≠ ability). *(T1; adjacent — an 80-item university test taken by adults.)*
      - **A rapid guess is a choice to "momentarily opt out of being measured,"** occurs in **both high- and low-stakes** contexts, "does not reflect what a test taker knows and can do," and therefore "tends to negatively distort scores and… diminish validity"; the author concludes it "makes little sense to include them in scoring." *(T1. **Conflict of Interest:** the author works for NWEA, a commercial adaptive-test vendor.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Response Time Effort reads effort straight off the child's behavior instead of asking them about it, sorting every answer into a real attempt or a rapid guess and reporting the share that were real attempts. Because that number is close to uncorrelated with ability, effort can be measured and dealt with separately from the thing the test is trying to score — which is what makes the structural effort guarantee demanded by 7.3 buildable rather than aspirational.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2) | [https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12165](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12165)
- **Subcategory 7.5: Once you can spot a non-attempt, you can stop it from contaminating the score**
  - **Source:** Wise & DeMars (2005, 2006); Wise, Bhola & Yang (2006); Finn (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Item response theory" (IRT) is the standard scoring math that places children and questions on one shared difficulty scale. The **3PL**, or three-parameter logistic model, is its workhorse version: it describes each question by how hard it is, how sharply it separates stronger children from weaker ones, and how likely a blind guess is to land on the right answer.
      - **Effort-moderated IRT** models each response by the strategy behind it: responses below Tᵢ (rapid guesses) get a **constant chance probability (gᵢ = 1/#options)** — whatever the odds of guessing right are, given the number of answer choices — while responses at or above Tᵢ are modeled by the standard 3PL. With rapid-guessing present it produced **better model fit, more accurate item-parameter estimates, more accurate test information, and higher convergent validity** than the standard 3PL. *(T1; adjacent population and setting rather than a K–8 gifted screen.)*
      - **Motivation filtering** — excluding or down-weighting low-effort responses — has been shown to **remove the construct-irrelevant variance caused by lack-of-effort behavior** and to bring low-stakes scores into closer agreement with an independent high-stakes estimate of the same children's ability. *(T1/T3; adjacent population and setting.)*
      - An **effort-monitoring computer-based test (CBT)** design uses response-time effort *during* delivery to identify and act on low-effort responding as it happens, rather than only cleaning it up afterwards. *(T1; adjacent population and setting.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Treating a rapid guess as a coin flip rather than as a wrong answer, or dropping it from the scoring altogether, removes the score movement that low effort creates and pulls a low-stakes score back into line with what the same child produces when the stakes are real. This is the mechanism that lets a deliberately calm, low-pressure test still yield an effort-clean number, and it can run live during the session instead of only in post-hoc cleanup.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x) | [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00054.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00054.x)
- **Subcategory 7.6: The cut-off for "too fast to count" can be fixed in advance — but heavy text is what drives children to guess in the first place**
  - **Source:** Kong, Wise & Bhola (2007); Wise, Pastor & Kong (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The threshold Tᵢ from 7.4 — the time below which an answer counts as a rapid guess rather than a real attempt — has to be set by somebody, which raises the obvious worry that the choice of threshold, rather than the child's behavior, is what decides who gets filtered.
      - **Thresholds can be set reproducibly.** Four Tᵢ-setting methods — a **fixed** threshold, a **surface-feature** rule keyed to the amount of reading a question requires, **visual inspection** of the response-time distributions, and a **two-state mixture model** that lets the data sort itself into a fast-guessing group and a real-attempt group — yielded **only minor differences** in the resulting effort scores. *(T1; adjacent population and setting.)* For reproducibility, that means a **pre-registered, locked** Tᵢ rule — chosen and written down before any responses are scored — makes effort detection deterministic and auditable — the same rule applied to the same responses always filters the same answers (ties auditability, cross-ref **Category 9**).
      - **Structural features predict disengagement.** Items with **more text** and items **later in the test** drew **more** rapid-guessing; items **with a graphic** drew **less**; the only significant *examinee* predictor was SAT total score. *(T1; adjacent population and setting.)*
      - **Extracts identically / the text-only design collision:** a **text-only** screener (no graphics, with reading itself part of the capability being measured) sits on the two features that *raise* rapid-guessing — heavy text, and accumulated position in the test. This is a first-order structural risk for this specific product and ties directly to **session design / fatigue (Category 6)** and **cognitive load (Category 5)**. No study of this effect in the K–8 gifted upper tail was located.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The line between a real attempt and a rapid guess is not arbitrary: four different ways of drawing it produce nearly the same effort scores, so committing to one rule before scoring begins makes the filter deterministic and auditable. The harder news is what provokes guessing in the first place — long text and late position — which is precisely the profile of a text-only screener, making this a design risk rather than a scoring detail.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164406294779](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164406294779) | [https://doi.org/10.1080/08957340902754650](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957340902754650)
- **Subcategory 7.7: Matching question difficulty to the child protects effort and sharpens the measurement with the same move**
  - **Source:** Csikszentmihalyi (1990); companion GA 7.2
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Flow" is the absorbed state people reach when a task's difficulty matches their skill — the feeling of being neither out of your depth nor bored by something too easy.
      - Engagement peaks when **challenge matches skill**: challenge above skill breeds **anxiety**, below it breeds **boredom**. *(T2; cross-ref **GA 7.2**.)*
      - **Structural bridge:** an adaptive engine — one that picks each next question based on how the child has answered so far — targets item difficulty near the child's provisional ability estimate, aiming at a moderate chance of success (the exact target depends on the model). That is *the same mechanism* that maximizes measurement information (GA 4.1 / Category 1) **and** sustains the challenge–skill balance that protects effort. In other words, adaptivity is an **effort lever that does not touch the score construct**: it keeps the child trying without changing what is being measured. Detailed adaptive item-selection and stopping mechanics are owned by **Category 1** and are not re-derived here.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Aiming each question at the child's current level is simultaneously the move that squeezes the most measurement information out of the test and the move that keeps the child in the absorbed zone between boredom and panic. That makes adaptivity the rare engagement lever with no measurement cost attached: it protects effort without altering what the test is measuring.
    - **Link to source:** [https://worldcat.org/title/20392741](https://worldcat.org/title/20392741)
- **Subcategory 7.8: Telling a child how they are doing mid-test can help or backfire, and it is never free**
  - **Source:** Kluger & DeNisi (1996); Hattie & Timperley (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Feedback is not reliably positive.** In a meta-analysis of **607 effect sizes / 23,663 observations**, feedback interventions raised performance on average (**d = .41**, a moderate gain in standard-deviation terms) but **over one-third *decreased* performance**; the drop was **not** explained by sampling error or by whether the feedback was positive or negative, and effectiveness fell as attention moved **from the task toward the self**. *(T1; adjacent — learning and workplace settings rather than a scored screening test.)*
      - Feedback impact is **positive or negative depending on type/level**: **self-level praise — praise aimed at the child rather than at the work — is least effective**, while feedback about the **task, process, and self-regulation** (and the "where to next?" question) is most effective; **timing matters**. *(T1; adjacent — a learning context rather than a scored screen.)*
      - **Extracts identically:** any within-test correctness or score feedback is a **live variable that can change how hard the child tries next, and that change then leaks into the ability estimate** — the opposite of a reproducible, effort-clean score.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Feedback is usually treated as a free way to keep a child motivated, but across a very large body of studies it made performance *worse* more than a third of the time, and it is at its worst when it points at the child instead of at the work. Inside a scored test that is a measurement problem rather than a morale one: feedback changes how hard the child tries next, and changed effort ends up inside the ability estimate.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) | [https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487)
- **Subcategory 7.9: Points and badges can kill the interest they are meant to create, and children are hit hardest**
  - **Source:** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); companion GA 7.3 (rewards), GA 7.5 (gamification)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Intrinsic motivation" is doing something because the thing itself is interesting; "extrinsic motivation" is doing it for a reward attached from outside, such as points, a badge, or money. "Crowding out" is the finding that adding the second can subtract from the first — the reward displaces the interest instead of adding to it. A "performance-contingent" reward is one that depends on how well the child does.
      - **Tangible / performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d ≈ −0.28 to −0.40) and were "more detrimental for children than college students," whereas positive *verbal/informational* feedback *enhanced* it (d ≈ 0.33).** In plain terms, the rewards cost roughly a third of a standard deviation of genuine interest, measured by whether children went back to the task voluntarily once nobody was making them, while plain informational feedback raised it by a comparable amount. *(T1; cross-ref **GA 7.3**.)*
      - **Extracts identically:** points/badges/leaderboards tied to performance are contraindicated in a children's screener; the *informational* half of the same literature is what 7.8 says to keep — neutral, task-level, non-contingent framing that tells a child what is happening without paying them for it. (Gamification's engagement≠validity distinction is owned by **GA 7.5**.)
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Rewarding a child for scoring well reliably reduces how much they want to do the task for its own sake, and the damage is larger for children than for college students, so points, badges, and leaderboards are the wrong instrument inside a screener. The same research says what to use instead: plain informational feedback that is not tied to performance, which raised interest by about as much as rewards eroded it.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)

### Category 8: Fairness & access in structure

- **Subcategory 8.1: Where a question sits in the test changes how hard it is — and that can look like bias**
  - **Source:** Debeer & Janssen (2013); Albano (2013); Kingston & Dorans (1984); Weirich, Hecht, Penk, Roppelt & Böhme (2017, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 41(2), 115–129); Nagy et al. (2018, *Psychological Test and Assessment Modeling* 60(2))
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Differential item functioning**, usually shortened to DIF, is the statistical check for whether a specific question is harder for one group than another *among children of equal ability*. A **position effect** is a change in how hard a question is caused purely by where it falls in the sequence, rather than by anything about the question itself.
      - Changing the order of questions from one form to the next — a routine **test-security** practice — can change how hard those questions are. Position effects can be built into the standard scoring math, **item response theory**, which places children and questions on one shared difficulty scale. Children also differ in how sensitive they are to position, so if two groups receive different question sequences, **that difference can masquerade as differential item functioning**. [T1]
      - The size of the effect is measurable as an **item-by-position interaction** — how much a question's difficulty shifts for each place it moves in the sequence — demonstrated on a K-12 reading test (>90,000 students) and on pilot sections of the GRE, the graduate-admissions exam. The same paper notes that in a **computer-adaptive test**, which chooses each next question based on how the child has answered so far, **the item set *and sequence* differ significantly across examinees** — so the assumption that position is negligible is least safe precisely in adaptive delivery. [T1]
      - Changing where an item sits has **direct implications for equating** — putting two forms on genuinely the same scale — **and for adaptive testing**. Position has therefore been a recognized threat in adaptive-style delivery for decades, not only on fixed forms. [T1]
      - The position effect is **person-varying and partly driven by change in test-taking effort**: difficulty rose across a 60-min assessment, and *change in effort* moderated the position effect, with people differing in how much it moderated them — so late items are differentially harder for examinees whose effort declines. Position effects **did not vanish** even for examinees who kept their effort high, so several causes are in play at once: fatigue, listlessness, and declining effort. This is a concrete channel by which **position can introduce subgroup-differential difficulty**. [T1]
      - In a **reading-comprehension** test, how sensitive an individual was to position correlated with **decoding speed and reading enjoyment / persistence** — fluent decoders showed more persistence and a smaller position-related decline. The evidence comes from a reading test rather than an ability screen, so it is an adjacent setting; the full author list beyond the first author remains unverified, and no DOI was located. [T1]
      - Empirically, **different test booklets — that is, different item orders — produce variant DIF flagging patterns** for the same items across three standard detection methods (Mantel–Haenszel, Raju area, and Wald), consistent with order changing item parameters differentially. This comes from a study titled "Impact of retrofitting and item ordering on DIF" in the *Journal of Measurement and Evaluation in Education and Psychology* (EPOD); its authors remain unverified, and it uses retrofitted real testing data rather than a gifted screener, so it is adjacent evidence. [T1]
      - **No study measuring item-position bias in a K-8 gifted or high-ability screener specifically was located**; the evidence base is general large-scale testing and adult testing.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Where a question falls changes how hard it is, and it changes more for some children than for others depending on their effort and their reading fluency. Question order can therefore quietly make a test harder for one group than another at equal ability — mimicking item bias or adding to it — and the risk is greatest in adaptive delivery, where every child receives a different sequence.

- **Subcategory 8.2: Every child sees a different set of questions — bias is still detectable, but harder to catch**
  - **Source:** Zwick, Thayer & Wingersky (1994); Zwick & Thayer (2002); Nandakumar & Roussos (2004)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The usual way to check a question for bias is to compare how two groups of *equal ability* answered it, where equal ability is defined by the total raw score. Because each examinee in an adaptive test sees a different, tailored subset of items, there is no common raw score to match on, so bias checks and **measurement invariance** checks — the whole-test version of the same fairness question — must be **modified for the adaptive setting**, and **fairness in adaptive testing is actively debated**. [T1]
      - **Bias is still detectable in an adaptive test.** Two standard detection methods (Mantel–Haenszel and standardization), modified to match children on their model-estimated expected true score instead of a raw score, recovered bias flags that were **highly correlated with the flags produced from the full, nonadaptive item pool**, with each simulated test-taker seeing 25 of 75 items. The fairness of an adaptive form can therefore be audited. [T1]
      - Detection is **harder with sparse per-examinee data**, because each child answers only a fraction of the bank. An **empirical-Bayes enhancement** of Mantel–Haenszel — which steadies each item's estimate by borrowing strength from the other items — was developed specifically to stabilize adaptive-test bias estimates. [T1]
      - A version of the SIBTEST bias-detection procedure built for adaptive testing, **CATSIB**, adds a regression correction for genuine ability differences between the groups, **but its performance is condition-dependent**: later work reports inflated false-positive rates under some conditions, and finds an odds-ratio method plus scale purification more robust when many items carry bias (Shih et al., *Journal of Applied Measurement* / JAM, 2024). The evidence comes from LSAT data and simulation rather than from a child ability screen. [T1]
      - A biased item **encountered early in an adaptive test distorts the interim ability estimate and therefore the routing** of every item that follows, so one unfair item can cascade through the session rather than stay local; Mantel–Haenszel-style correction was **insufficient to fully mitigate** the effect under some simulated conditions. This comes from a 2024 adaptive-testing bias simulation (PMC11501093), so it is simulated rather than operational evidence. [T1]
      - **Whether adaptivity nets out as more or less fair than a fixed form at a gifted cut is unresolved in the located evidence** — no study settling it was found. The answer depends on how much bias the item pool carries, how routing works, and where the test's precision is concentrated.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Bias remains detectable in an adaptive test if you match children on their model-estimated ability rather than on a raw score and steady the estimates statistically. But detection is harder because each child answers so few items, and a biased item met early can cascade through every routing decision after it — so whether adaptivity ends up fairer than a fixed form is unresolved.

- **Subcategory 8.3: A large rotating bank of never-reused questions is a fairness lever, not just a security one**
  - **Source:** Stocking & Lewis (1995, 1998); Kingsbury & Zara (1989); van der Linden & Veldkamp (2007, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 32(4), 398–418); Kulik, Bangert-Drowns & Kulik (1984); Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007); Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Item exposure** is how often a given question is put in front of test-takers. The more often a question is seen, the more likely it is to leak, be memorized, or be coached — which is why exposure is a security problem and a fairness problem at the same time.
      - In paper-and-pencil testing, item exposure is controlled **by policy** — by regulating **reuse of forms and how often a candidate may retake**. Continuous, on-demand computer delivery removes those natural limits, so exposure must be controlled algorithmically instead. **Conflict of Interest:** the foundational work here is ETS and Navy research reports (Stocking & Lewis, 1995; Sympson & Hetter, 1985, Proceedings of the 27th Military Testing Association conference, for which no DOI exists). [T1]
      - A CAT item-selection algorithm is **greedy**: left unconstrained it over-administers the most informative items, creating a **security/exposure risk**. Four standard remedies exist — **Randomesque**, which randomly picks among the top-k best items (Kingsbury & Zara, 1989); **Sympson–Hetter**, which admits a chosen item only with some probability (Sympson & Hetter, 1985); **conditional-on-ability multinomial control**, which manages exposure separately at each ability level (Stocking & Lewis, 1998); and **item-ineligibility probabilities** applied to a shadow test, which can push exposure below 0.025 **at the cost of ability-estimate accuracy** (van der Linden & Veldkamp, 2007). [T1]
      - Exposure control **trades measurement precision for security**, and the loss is largest at **extreme ability levels** — exactly the gifted tail where the cut sits. This finding (Chang & Twu, 1998) reaches us through a review rather than the primary study: Georgiadou, Triantafillou & Economides, 2007, *Journal of Technology, Learning, and Assessment*, 5(8), ERIC EJ838610. [T1]
      - Test security is explicitly a **fairness** mechanism, not only an integrity one. The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* — the joint manual issued by the three main professional bodies in American educational measurement — puts it verbatim: "Maintaining test security also helps ensure that no one has an unfair advantage." (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, test-administration/security standards.) [T2]
      - **Coaching** — paid or structured test preparation — raises scores modestly on well-controlled admissions tests but more on ability/IQ-type tests: SAT ≈ **0.15 SD**, other aptitude/intelligence tests ≈ **0.43 SD**, across 38 studies (Kulik, Bangert-Drowns & Kulik, 1984). "SD" is standard deviations, the typical spread of scores across the whole population, so 0.15 SD is a nudge and 0.43 SD a substantial shift. Published comparison studies show SAT-V ≈ **0.09 SD** and SAT-M ≈ **0.16 SD** (Becker, 1990), and matched/randomized estimates are small, roughly **10 SAT points** (DerSimonian & Laird, 1983). Coaching and prep access covaries with family resources, which makes it an equity concern as well as a gaming and burden concern. The evidence comes from adults and college admissions rather than young children. [T1]
      - **Retest / practice effects** are real and repeatable: taking a test again lifts scores by an overall adjusted **d ≈ 0.26** across 50 studies (N=134,436) — again in standard-deviation units — and gains are **larger when identical forms are reused and when coaching is added** (Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard, 2007). A larger meta-analysis (174 samples, N=153,185) finds gains continuing up to the **3rd administration then a plateau**, with **form equivalence** a significant moderator, since alternate forms reduce the gain (Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018). This too is adult and general-testing evidence rather than gifted-screen evidence. [T1]
      - Therefore **rotating, never-reused forms drawn from a large bank act as a combined security *and* anti-coaching/anti-retest-advantage (fairness) lever**: they blunt memorization and pre-knowledge, and they shrink the retake and coaching edge that otherwise accrues to repeat-testers and to the coached. This is a reasoned extension of the cited effects — a synthesis of Sympson & Hetter (1985), Kingsbury & Zara (1989), Hausknecht et al. (2007), and Scharfen et al. (2018) — rather than something any one source states directly. [T1]
      - Retesting can also **threaten measurement invariance**: a retest score may not measure the same thing as the first-attempt score, so a changed score is not automatically a truer score (Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad, 2007). The evidence comes from adjacent personnel-selection research rather than gifted identification. [T1]
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** On-demand computer delivery strips away the paper era's natural limits on how often a question is seen, so exposure control plus a large, rotating, never-reused bank does double duty. It is a security control and a fairness lever at once, because both coaching and practice effects favour the families who can pay for preparation and the children who can afford to test more than once.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.1995.tb01660.x](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.1995.tb01660.x) | [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame0204_6](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame0204_6) | [https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986023001057](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986023001057) | [https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998606298044](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998606298044) | [https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.95.2.179](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.95.2.179) | [https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060003373](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060003373) | [https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.53.1.n06j5h5356217648](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.53.1.n06j5h5356217648) | [https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373) | [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003) | [https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672)
- **Subcategory 8.4: Designing for the whole population — and the unsettled question of whether reading belongs in the score**
  - **Source:** Thompson, Johnstone & Thurlow (2002); AERA/APA/NCME (2014); Sireci, Scarpati & Li (2005); Li (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Construct-irrelevant variance** is score movement caused by something other than the ability the test means to measure — a heavy reading demand on a reasoning test, for example. An **accommodation** removes such a barrier without changing what is being measured; a **modification** changes what is being measured, so its scores are not comparable to standard ones.
      - **Universal Design for Assessment (UDA)** proposes that large-scale tests be designed from the start for the widest population — **including students with disabilities and limited-English-proficient students** — via seven elements: (1) inclusive assessment population, (2) **precisely defined constructs**, (3) accessible, non-biased items, (4) **amenable to accommodations**, (5) simple/clear/intuitive instructions & procedures, (6) **maximum readability & comprehensibility**, (7) maximum legibility. This comes from NCEO Synthesis Report 44, a technical report rather than peer-reviewed work. [T2]
      - The 2014 **Standards make fairness/accessibility fundamental to validity** and set an **overarching Standard 3.0**: "All steps in the testing process … should be designed … to minimize construct-irrelevant variance and to promote valid score interpretations … for **all examinees** in the intended population," the central idea being to "identify and remove **construct-irrelevant barriers** to maximal performance" (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, ch. 3, Standard 3.0, p. 63). [T2]
      - The Standards **endorse universal design** and, as a fairness example, explicitly list **"minimizing the linguistic load"** of items whose reading demand is not part of the ability being measured (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, ch. 3). [T2]
      - **Whether reading load is part of the ability being measured is the hinge.** The Standards state: if a test is **not** intended to measure reading, then for weak readers and limited-English-proficient test takers the **scores do not represent the same construct** as for fluent readers, because reading load is then contamination unrelated to the target ability. **But** where the impeding skill **is part of the construct** — the Standards' own example is **"dyslexia in the context of tests of reading"** — providing access "may require some adaptation of the construct as well," and comparable measurement across adapted and unadapted versions may be impossible (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, ch. 3, pp. ~51–52, 59). [T2]
      - Consistent with that, a read-aloud or screen-reader on a test **"that includes decoding as part of the construct" is classified as a *modification* (it changes the ability being measured), and its scores are not assumed comparable** to standard-condition scores — distinct from an *accommodation*, which removes a barrier unrelated to the target ability without changing what is measured (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014, ch. 12, p. ~190). [T2]
      - The **interaction hypothesis** — the expectation that an accommodation should help the students who need it more than those who don't, since if everyone benefits the scores may be invalidly inflated — is **not cleanly supported**: extended time tends to improve performance for **all** students, with students with disabilities gaining relatively more, and oral/read-aloud delivery on **math** helps some students with disabilities. (Sireci, Scarpati & Li, 2005.) [T1]
      - In a read-aloud meta-analysis (114 effect sizes, 23 studies), **both** students with disabilities and students without them benefited, students with disabilities **significantly more**, and — critically — the read-aloud effect was **significantly larger when the tested subject was *reading* than math** (and larger with a human reader than a computer). A read-aloud helps most exactly where it **removes the reading construct itself**, that is, where reading is part of what the test measures. (Li, 2014.) [T1]
      - **No evidence was located establishing, for a gifted screener, whether a text-only reading gate's foreseeable disparate impact on English-language learners, twice-exceptional children, young children, and lower-income readers is *offset* by genuine construct-relevance of reading.** That is precisely the adverse-impact and item-bias question this design defers, and the premise that reading is part of the capability being measured remains unverified.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Standards demand minimizing score movement that has nothing to do with the ability being measured, and they endorse designing for the widest population from the start. Yet they also hold that removing a skill that *is* part of the target ability — their example is reading on a reading test — changes the test rather than merely widening access. So the reading gate's legitimacy turns entirely on the unverified premise that reading is construct-relevant here.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543075004457](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543075004457) | [https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12027](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12027)

### Category 9: Auditability & reproducibility of structure

- **Subcategory 9.1: The rules that steer an adaptive test are written down in advance, so the same inputs always produce the same test**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014), Standard 4.3 & ch. 4; Kingsbury & Zara (1989, *Applied Measurement in Education* 2(4):359–375); van der Linden & Glas (Eds.) (2010), *Elements of Adaptive Testing*, Springer
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Routing** is the set of rules deciding which question a child sees next, where the test starts, when it stops, and how the answers become a score. A process is **deterministic** when the same inputs always produce exactly the same output, with nothing left to chance.
      - The 2014 *Standards* require that developers "document the rationale and supporting evidence for the administration, scoring, and reporting rules used in computer adaptive, multistage-adaptive, or other tests delivered using computer algorithms to select items," and that this documentation "include procedures used in **selecting items** … in determining the **starting point and termination conditions** … in **scoring the test**, and in **controlling item exposure**" (AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, **Standard 4.3, p. 86**). `T2` — i.e., the full routing rule set is treated as documentable, fixed content.
      - The specification of a CAT "refer[s] both to the **item pool** and to the **rules or procedures by which an individualized set of items is selected** for each test taker" (AERA et al., 2014, ch. 4, Test Design and Development, p. 80). `T2`
      - When an item response theory model is used — the scoring math that places children and questions on one shared difficulty scale — the test specifications "should indicate the form of the model, how model parameters are to be estimated, and how model fit is to be evaluated" (AERA et al., 2014, ch. 4, p. 79). `T2` — the scoring engine is itself a specifiable, lockable object.
      - Adaptive item selection decomposes into a small set of separable, individually specifiable components — a content-balancing rule, an item-selection (information) criterion, and an item-exposure control — each with named, published algorithms (Kingsbury & Zara, 1989; van der Linden & Glas, 2010). `T1`/`T2` — mechanics owned by **Category 1 (1.2–1.5)**; the point here is only that the rule set is *enumerable and fixable*.
      - Given a fixed calibrated bank, a fixed rule set, and a fixed response string, the sequence of items and the final ability estimate (written θ in the technical literature) and classification are a **deterministic function** of those inputs — a pure function of bank plus rules plus responses plus any random seed. That is a reasoned extension of the published material rather than something the sources state directly, and no study establishing it for a gifted screener specifically was located.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Standards require every routing rule — how items are selected, where the test starts, when it stops, how exposure is controlled, and how it is scored — to be documented. That makes an adaptive test's routing a deterministic function of a fixed item bank, a fixed rule set, and the child's string of answers: the same inputs give the same test and the same result every time.

- **Subcategory 9.2: The test makes random choices on purpose — and how to make those choices repeatable anyway**
  - **Source:** Kingsbury & Zara (1989); Stocking & Lewis (1998, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 23(1):57–75)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A computer does not produce true randomness. It produces a **pseudo-random** sequence generated from a starting number called a **seed**, so recording the seed lets you reproduce the identical "random" sequence later.
      - The "**randomesque**" strategy selects the next item **at random from among the several most-informative candidate items**, deliberately introducing a stochastic step into selection to spread item exposure (Kingsbury & Zara, 1989). `T1`
      - Conditional exposure control admits a selected item **probabilistically** — a random draw against an exposure-control parameter — so that, e.g., a high-information item is not administered to ~100% of examinees at one ability level even when its overall rate looks safe (Stocking & Lewis, 1998). `T1` — exposure-control *mechanics* are owned by **Category 1 (1.5)**; extracted here only as the source of routing stochasticity.
      - Because randomesque selection and probabilistic exposure control make item selection **stochastic** — a matter of chance — an operational session is reproducible **only if the pseudo-random draws are driven by a recorded seed**, a standard property of pseudo-random-number generators. Replaying the same seed plus bank plus rules plus responses then reproduces the identical item sequence. That is a reasoned extension of the cited methods rather than something the sources state, and no located psychometric study validates seeded replay for an operational gifted screener.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The only part of routing left to chance is the deliberate randomness that randomesque selection and probabilistic exposure control inject to stop the best items being over-used. Record the seed that drives every one of those draws and the session becomes bit-for-bit reproducible again.

- **Subcategory 9.3: Keeping a complete record of each session, so a decision can be re-run and answered for**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014), Standards 6.15, 6.8, 6.14
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A **test protocol** is the full record of one child's session — what was asked, what they answered, and how it was scored. An **audit trail** is that record kept in a form an outsider can re-examine afterwards.
      - **Reconstructability (a general auditability principle):** an independent reviewer "must be able to **reconstruct how students were selected**"; evidence includes that "selection rules and analysis choices are **locked before results are observed**" and that "**decisions and changes are logged**." This is stated as a general auditability principle rather than sourced to a specific standard.
      - The 2014 *Standards* require that, "when individual test data are retained, both the **test protocol** and any written report should also be preserved in some form," because "**the protocol may be needed to respond to a possible challenge from a test taker** or to facilitate interpretation at a subsequent time" (AERA et al., 2014, **Standard 6.15**). `T2`
      - Test scorers "should establish scoring protocols," and where complex responses are scored by computer, "the **accuracy of the algorithm and processes should be documented**" (AERA et al., 2014, **Standard 6.8**). `T2` — the CAT-specific documentation anchor is Std 5.16 (9.4); 6.8 adds the general scoring-protocol duty.
      - Organizations retaining identifiable test data "should maintain appropriate data security, which should include administrative, technical, and physical protections," under a documented retention policy (AERA et al., 2014, **Standard 6.14**). `T2` — audit-trail retention is bounded by privacy duties, because the record that lets an applicant challenge a decision is also a record about a child.
      - Standards 6.15 and 6.8 plus the auditability principle together imply that the auditable unit is a **complete session log** — item IDs, presentation order, responses, the interim ability estimate and its standard error (the width of the error bar on that estimate), each selection and stop decision, the seed, and the engine and item-parameter-snapshot versions — sufficient to **re-execute the session and reproduce the classification**. That is a reasoned extension of the standards rather than something any of them states directly, and no empirical validation of such deterministic replay for a K-8 gifted CAT was located.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Standards independently require preserving a child's test protocol so a challenge can be answered, and documenting that a computerized scoring algorithm is accurate. Put together, auditability comes down to logging the whole session and being able to re-run it and get the same classification back.

- **Subcategory 9.4: Every child takes a different test — why the scores can still be compared**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014), Standards 5.16, 5.17 & ch. 5; Wyse (2023, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 47(7-8):513–525)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Classical **standardization** equalizes conditions so examinees have "comparable contexts": "uniform directions, specified time limits, specified room arrangements," etc. (AERA et al., 2014, ch. 3, Fairness, p. 50). `T2` — the baseline that item-level adaptivity deliberately breaks, by giving each child different items in a different order.
      - The *Standards* defend adaptive comparability on a **common scale**, not identical items: "With some adaptive tests … two examinees rarely if ever receive the same set of items … [and] may be given sets of items that differ markedly in difficulty. **Nevertheless, adaptive test scores can be reported on a common scale and function much like scores from a single alternate form** of a test that is not adaptive" (AERA et al., 2014, ch. 5, p. 98). `T2`
      - Comparability must be **documented, not assumed**: "When test scores are based on model-based psychometric procedures, such as those used in computerized adaptive or multistage testing, documentation should be provided to indicate that the scores have **comparable meaning over alternate sets of test items**," including "clear descriptions of **model-based algorithms, software used, quality control procedures** followed, and technical analyses conducted" (AERA et al., 2014, **Standard 5.16**). `T2`
      - For links that cannot be equated — equating means putting two forms on genuinely the same scale, and adaptive tests are among the cases where it is not possible — "**direct evidence of score comparability should be provided**, and the examinee population for which score comparability applies should be specified clearly" (AERA et al., 2014, **Standard 5.17**). `T2`
      - Score comparability of CAT forms is an active, formalizable measurement problem: "CATs can have thousands of forms with **each examinee typically seeing a unique form** … items are selected from a calibrated item pool," and comparability is evaluated against (weakened forms of) **Lord's (1980) equity property** — first-order equity, meaning equal average scores at each true ability level (Divgi, 1981), and second-order equity, meaning equal error bars at each true ability level (Morris, 1982). (Wyse, 2023.) `T1` **Conflict of Interest:** the author is affiliated with Renaissance, a commercial adaptive-testing vendor.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Every child sees a different item set, which breaks the usual guarantee that everyone sat the same test. The Standards answer that adaptive scores are still comparable because they are reported on one common ability scale — but only where documented algorithms, software, and quality control plus an equity-property analysis support the claim.

- **Subcategory 9.5: Freezing the rules and the question difficulties before the first real applicant**
  - **Source:** AERA/APA/NCME (2014), ch. 3–4; Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven & Mellor (2018, *PNAS* 115(11):2600–2606)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - To **calibrate** a question is to estimate, from real test-takers, how hard it is and how sharply it separates stronger children from weaker ones. Those two estimates are the question's **item parameters**, and the scoring engine treats them as fixed facts about the question.
      - Deviations from standardized procedures can "**compromise the comparability of scores** or use of norms, and/or unfairly advantage some individuals," so the operating procedure itself is what must be held fixed (AERA et al., 2014, ch. 3, p. 53). `T2`
      - Item quality is fixed pre-operationally through "item review procedures and item tryouts, often referred to as pretesting," before items enter the operational pool (AERA et al., 2014, ch. 4, p. 81). `T2` — i.e., item parameters are established (calibrated) *before* operational scoring, not during it.
      - **Pre-registration** — "define the research questions and analysis plan **before observing the research outcomes**" — is the general method for distinguishing a prediction made in advance (confirmatory) from an explanation constructed after the fact (postdiction, exploratory), and is offered as the remedy for outcome-dependent analytic flexibility (Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven & Mellor, 2018). `T1` The evidence comes from open-science methodology rather than testing specifically, so it is an adjacent field rather than a direct precedent — it supports auditability's "rules locked before results," applied here to the routing/stopping/scoring rules and the item-parameter snapshot.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Comparability and fairness both require the operating procedure, the routing rules, and the question difficulties to be calibrated and locked before the first real applicant is scored — the testing analogue of pre-registering a study before you look at the data.

- **Subcategory 9.6: Question difficulties shift over time, which pulls reproducibility and validity apart**
  - **Source:** Veerkamp & Glas (2000, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 25(4):373–389); Wells, Subkoviak & Serlin (2002, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 26(1):77–87); AERA/APA/NCME (2014), ch. 4
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Item-parameter drift** is what happens when a question's real difficulty changes over time — because it leaked, because it was coached, or because the population shifted — while the scoring engine still treats its old, frozen difficulty as correct.
      - A previously exposed / disclosed item "**is bound to show drift in the item parameter values**," and a **statistical quality-control** method can detect such "known items" by re-estimating item parameters from adaptive-test data and testing for parameter drift, worked out for the one-parameter and three-parameter models (Veerkamp & Glas, 2000). `T1` — links drift monitoring ↔ security ↔ ongoing audit of a "frozen" bank.
      - Under simulated two-parameter conditions across two occasions, "item parameter drift … **had a small effect on ability estimates**": even with the discrimination and difficulty parameters (a and b) increased for **20% of items**, θ estimates were expected to deviate "**by no more than 0.14 logits**, for any true θ value." A logit is the unit of that shared ability scale and θ is a child's position on it, so this is a small shift (Wells, Subkoviak & Serlin, 2002). `T1` — reassuring for freezing parameters, but the study is simulated and not run at a top-percentile cut, and no study doing so was located.
      - Adaptivity adds an **order-dependent** threat to parameter stability: adaptive tryout data "should be examined for possible **context effects** to assess how much **item parameters might shift when items are administered in different orders**" (AERA et al., 2014, ch. 4, p. 80). `T2` — because a CAT gives each child a different order, the same item's parameters are not guaranteed invariant across sessions (position/context effects owned by sibling **Category 2**), which is a reproducibility concern for a frozen scale.
      - Reproducibility (identical re-scoring) is maximized by a **permanently frozen** item-parameter file, whereas validity and security argue for **periodic recalibration**, because parameters drift and items leak (Veerkamp & Glas, 2000); the two goals are reconcilable only via **versioned, hashed parameter snapshots** — freeze per version, recalibrate to a *new* documented version. That reconciliation is a reasoned extension of the cited findings rather than something a source prescribes, and no source prescribing a recalibration cadence for a gifted screener was located.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Reproducibility wants a permanently frozen file of question difficulties, while validity and security want those difficulties re-estimated periodically, because parameters drift and items leak. The two are reconcilable only through versioned, hashed parameter snapshots paired with a separate drift and security monitor.
