# BrainLift: Scoring How Fast a Child Learns, to a Standard That Could Carry an Admissions Decision

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose

Selection processes that want to look past what a child already knows keep arriving at the same idea: measure how fast the child learns, and decide on that instead. This BrainLift asks what it would actually take to do it — **what has to be true about a learning curve, an estimator, a population, and an evidence base before a number describing one child's rate of learning is fit to affect where that child is placed.** The organising discipline is that *a rate exists as a well-defined quantity*, *a rate can be estimated for one individual*, *that estimate is stable enough to order people*, and *that ordering is fit to carry or contribute to a consequential decision* are four separate claims resting on four separate evidence bases, and that failing any one of them does not merely weaken the next — it makes the next unanswerable. It argues from external evidence and general principles only, and is written to be read with no knowledge of any particular system or implementation.

### In Scope

- **The functional form of a practice curve, and what averaging does to it** — the power law and the evidence base that established it, the exponential repeal on unaveraged data, the delayed and piecewise forms that no single replacement has beaten, and the mechanism and boundary conditions of the averaging artifact.
- **Whether an individual person's rate can be estimated reliably at all** — the difference-score prohibition and its rebuttal, what determines the reliability of measured change, the latent-variable remedies and their limits, the reliability paradox, and the measured retest reliability of fitted learning-rate parameters together with the estimation configuration that produced each number.
- **The observation budget question** — what published work needed in order to see the structure it saw, and what the located literature does not state.
- **Whether learning rate is a stable, individually varying property at all** — the eighty-year-old null and its modern re-examination, the near-constant-rate finding at scale and its replication, the truncation sensitivity that challenges it, the implicit-learning tasks where stable differences do appear, and what the dynamic-testing tradition delivers over static ability.
- **What an observed climb is actually made of** — the free gain from mere repetition, what the retest score turns out to measure instead, unequal retest gains across groups and formats, and the gap between performance during acquisition and durable learning.
- **The evidentiary bar for a consequential score use, and the ladder of weaker claims beneath it** — classification evidence, error at the cut, subgroup fairness, incremental validity, and then rank, coarse band, tie-breaker, follow-up flag, and research-only signal, each with its own preconditions.
- **Provenance discipline throughout** — what is peer-reviewed, what rests on animal or simulated rather than human-learner data, what carries a publisher correction, what is a characterisation read through a citing source, and what was searched for and not found.

### Out of Scope

- **The structure and sequencing of the test experience** — adaptive routing, item ordering, session design, and retake policy are owned by the companion `test-structure` BrainLift. This one treats practice sequences only as inputs to a rate estimate.
- **Instrument-level measurement validity and tail quality** — owned by the companion `gifted-assessment-quality` BrainLift. The question here is whether a *change* between measurements is estimable, not whether any single measurement is good.
- **The statistical methodology for validating a new instrument against an established one** — owned by the companion `test-evaluation` BrainLift.
- **Which measured signals earn their place in an ability estimate** — owned by the companion `metric-evidence` BrainLift. A learning-rate score appears here as a candidate quantity to be measured, not as a candidate feature in a composite.
- **Causal identification, and separating a programme's effect from selection into it** — owned by the companion `gt-school-counterfactual` BrainLift. Nothing here licenses a claim that a fast-learning child learns fast *because of* anything.
- **Whether the observed session is the child's own unaided work** — owned by the companion `proctoring-integrity` BrainLift. This document assumes the responses are the child's and asks whether a rate can be recovered from them.
- **Instructional design** — how to make children learn faster, which practice schedules produce the most durable learning, and how to sequence content. Learning-rate *measurement* and learning-rate *improvement* are different problems with different evidence bases.
- **Any mapping of these findings onto a particular system, configuration, policy, or plan** — belongs in a separate design document, not this BrainLift.

### Category 1: Learning-curve functional form, and the averaging artifact

*General idea: A rate is not a raw observation — it is a parameter, and a parameter exists only relative to an assumed functional form. The form the field treated as settled for forty years, the power law of practice, was established almost entirely on curves averaged across people, and averaging is not a neutral operation on nonlinear functions. When the averaging bias is removed, the individual-level winner is generally exponential rather than power; in a clear majority of paradigms the best-supported form carries an initial delay that both classical laws forbid; in long individual records the curve fragments into piecewise segments joined at strategy shifts; and in one body of conditioning data it is not a smooth curve at all but a near-step. This category assembles what has actually been measured on that question, because every downstream claim — that a child's rate is some number of log odds per opportunity, that one child's rate exceeds another's, that a rate observed over ten trials extrapolates to a hundred — is conditional on getting the form right, and no single replacement form has won.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: The power law of practice, and the evidence base that established it**
  - **Source:** Newell & Rosenbloom (1981); result as re-reported by Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Newell and Rosenbloom examined 15 experiments spanning motor skills, perceptual and memory decisions, complex routines, and problem solving, and after averaging each data set across participants found the power function fit better than the exponential in all 15. *(Newell & Rosenbloom, 1981, in *Cognitive Skills and Their Acquisition*, pp. 1–55, as re-reported in Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote, 2018, *Psychological Review* 125(4):592–605, DOI 10.1037/rev0000105.)* `T2`
      - That result gave the power function the status of a behavioural law and was subsequently built into Instance Theory, PDP accounts, and cognitive architectures including SOAR. *(Evans et al., 2018, p. 592, DOI 10.1037/rev0000105.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The canonical power law of practice rests on a survey in which every one of 15 averaged data sets favoured the power function, and that uniformity is exactly why it became load-bearing for a generation of skill-acquisition theory. It matters here because the strength of the original evidence and the strength of the later artifact finding have the same source — averaging — so the more convincing the original result looked, the more of it was measuring the operation rather than the learners.
    - **Link to source:** [Mechanisms of skill acquisition and the law of practice (volume DOI)](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203728178), [Refining the law of practice](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000105)

- **Subcategory 1.2: The exponential repeal on unaveraged data, and the general result that licenses it**
  - **Source:** Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort (2000); Estes (1956)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Power and exponential functions were fit to 40 sets of data representing 7,910 learning series from 475 subjects across 24 experiments. *(Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort, 2000, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 7(2):185–207, p. 185, DOI 10.3758/BF03212979.)* `T2`
      - The exponential function fit better than the power function in all of the unaveraged data sets. *(Heathcote et al., 2000, p. 185.)* `T2`
      - Averaging produced a bias in favour of the power function. *(Heathcote et al., 2000, p. 185.)* `T2`
      - A new function based on the exponential, APEX, fit better than a power function given an extra pre-experimental practice parameter. *(Heathcote et al., 2000, p. 185.)* `T2`
      - A curve fitted to group-averaged data need not share the functional form of the individual curves it was computed from. *(Estes, 1956, *Psychological Bulletin* 53(2):134–140, DOI 10.1037/h0045156.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the largest body of real human practice data brought to bear on the form question, and its result is categorical rather than probabilistic — the exponential beat the power function in every unaveraged data set, and the same analysis diagnoses why the field believed otherwise. Estes had established the licence condition forty-four years earlier and it is not specific to learning: absent an argument that averaging preserves form, a group curve carries no information about individual form. Getting the form wrong is not cosmetic, because a power fit and an exponential fit to the same series imply different rate parameters and different extrapolations to trials not yet observed.
    - **Link to source:** [The power law repealed: the case for an exponential law of practice](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212979), [The problem of inference from curves based on group data](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045156)

- **Subcategory 1.3: The mechanism of the averaging artifact, and the conditions under which it is small**
  - **Source:** Anderson & Tweney (1997); Myung, Kim & Pitt (2000); Anderson (2001); Haider & Frensch (2002)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using computational analysis together with reanalyses of published data, arithmetic averaging of exponential curves was shown to produce an artifactual power curve, particularly when there are large and systematic differences among the slopes of the component curves. *(Anderson & Tweney, 1997, *Memory & Cognition* 25(5):724–730, p. 724, DOI 10.3758/BF03211315.)* `T2`
      - The amount of power artifact was small when component slopes were normally or rectangularly distributed and the performance measure was noise-free. *(Anderson & Tweney, 1997, p. 724.)* `T2`
      - The artifact could be large depending on the shape of the noise distribution and on restrictions in the performance range. *(Anderson & Tweney, 1997, p. 724.)* `T2`
      - A geometric analysis of the relations among model-generated responses showed the power-law artifact arises from arithmetic averaging of data generated by a nonlinear model in the presence of individual differences. *(Myung, Kim & Pitt, 2000, *Memory & Cognition* 28(5):832–840, p. 832, DOI 10.3758/BF03198418.)* `T2`
      - In a simulation study of 100 decaying traces per run, the aggregate curve's relative fit to a power function increased with intercomponent slope variability irrespective of whether the component curves were exponential, range-limited linear, range-limited logarithmic, or power. *(Anderson, 2001, *Memory & Cognition* 29(7):1061–1068, p. 1061, DOI 10.3758/BF03195767.)* `T2` `[computer simulation of decaying traces — simulated components, not observed learners]`
      - Aggregated learning data follow the power law of practice in circumstances where the underlying individual learning does not. *(Haider & Frensch, 2002, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 28(2):392–406, DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.28.2.392.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The mechanism behind the artifact is nonlinearity plus between-unit variability, not anything specific to the exponential, which is why the aggregate drifts toward power form regardless of what the component curves are. That cuts both ways: an observed group power law is not diagnostic evidence that individuals are exponential either, only that the group curve is uninformative about individual form in both directions. The originating source is also more conditional than the claim as it is usually repeated, since the artifact is modest when component slopes are well behaved and measurement is noise-free — averaging *can* fabricate a power law under identifiable conditions, not every observed power law *is* one.
    - **Link to source:** [Artifactual power curves in forgetting](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03211315), [Toward an explanation of the power law artifact](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198418), [The power law as an emergent property](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03195767), [Why aggregated learning follows the power law of practice when individual learning does not](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.28.2.392)

- **Subcategory 1.4: What the most careful re-analysis selected instead**
  - **Source:** Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using hierarchical Bayesian modelling across 17 data sets, one of the two exponential laws was selected by WAIC in 15 of the 17, with only two data sets preferring a power law. *(Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote, 2018, *Psychological Review* 125(4):592–605, p. 601, DOI 10.1037/rev0000105.)* `T2`
      - A function including a delay parameter — permitting a slower-faster-slower learning rate rather than monotonically decelerating gains — was selected in 13 of the 17 data sets. *(Evans et al., 2018, p. 601.)* `T2`
      - The delayed exponential specifically was the selected law in 11 of the 17 data sets, drawn from every task type studied. *(Evans et al., 2018, p. 601.)* `T2`
      - The authors fit hierarchical models specifically because pooling information over participants provides enhanced measurement without the distortions associated with fitting to averaged data. *(Evans et al., 2018, p. 598.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The most methodologically careful re-analysis to date does not restore the power law, but it does not leave the plain exponential standing either. In a clear majority of paradigms the best-supported form carries an initial delay, which means the assumption shared by the power and exponential laws alike — that improvement always proceeds at a steadily decreasing rate — is wrong for most tasks tested. Any single-parameter rate extracted under a no-delay assumption is therefore misspecified in the majority of paradigms studied, and the misspecification sits in the early trials, which is where a short assessment would be looking.
    - **Link to source:** [Refining the law of practice](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000105)

- **Subcategory 1.5: Individual curves as segments joined at strategy shifts**
  - **Source:** Donner & Hardy (2015); Delaney, Reder, Staszewski & Ritter (1998); Rickard (1997)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The analysis covered 25,280 individual learning curves, each comprising 500 measurements of cognitive performance across four cognitive tasks. *(Donner & Hardy, 2015, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 22(5):1308–1319, p. 1308, DOI 10.3758/s13423-015-0811-x.)* `T2`
      - A piecewise power-law model explained the individual learning curves significantly better than a single power law after controlling for model complexity. *(Donner & Hardy, 2015, p. 1308.)* `T2`
      - At transition points, performance briefly dropped before the later piece surpassed the earlier one. *(Donner & Hardy, 2015, p. 1308.)* `T2`
      - The transition rate was negatively associated with age even after controlling for overall performance. *(Donner & Hardy, 2015, p. 1308.)* `T2`
      - Improvement followed a power function when learning curves were separated by the strategy the participant was using, while aggregating across strategies within a task distorted the observed function. *(Delaney, Reder, Staszewski & Ritter, 1998, *Psychological Science* 9(1):1–7, p. 1, DOI 10.1111/1467-9280.00001.)* `T2`
      - A competing account attributes departures from a smooth practice function to discrete shifts between algorithmic computation and direct memory retrieval. *(Rickard, 1997, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 126(3):288–311, DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.126.3.288.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** With 500 observations per learner, individual curves resolve into a sequence of locally smooth segments joined at discrete strategy shifts rather than one continuous function, so a single global rate parameter is averaging across qualitatively distinct regimes inside one person — the same operation that distorts group curves, applied within an individual. The strategy work adds a further condition: a practice function may only be well defined within a strategy, so an individual rate is interpretable only if the learner used one strategy throughout, which is generally unverified. That structure was visible only at 500 measurements per individual, which is itself the useful number.
    - **Link to source:** [Piecewise power laws in individual learning curves](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0811-x), [The strategy-specific nature of improvement](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00001), [Bending the power law: a CMPL theory of strategy shifts](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.126.3.288)

- **Subcategory 1.6: Two processes on different timescales, and the case that rate is the wrong parameter**
  - **Source:** Kumar, Benjamin, Heathcote & Steyvers (2022); Gallistel, Fairhurst & Balsam (2004); Wixted & Ebbesen (1997)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The analysis drew on 41,006,715 gameplays from a starting pool of 194,695 cognitive-training users, restricted for modelling to 19,463 and 19,694 users who had more than 50 sessions in each of two games. *(Kumar, Benjamin, Heathcote & Steyvers, 2022, *npj Science of Learning* 7(1), Methods, DOI 10.1038/s41539-022-00142-x.)* `T2`
      - Only a model containing both a slow skill-learning process and a fast within-session task-set process, interacting, gave a general account of performance across the full range of naturally occurring inter-session intervals. *(Kumar et al., 2022, Results.)* `T2`
      - The gradually increasing, negatively accelerated learning curve was an artifact of group averaging in six paradigms: pigeon autoshaping, delay and trace eyeblink conditioning in rabbit and rat, autoshaped hopper entry in rat, plus-maze performance in rat, and water-maze performance in mouse. *(Gallistel, Fairhurst & Balsam, 2004, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 101(36):13124–13131, p. 13124, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0404965101.)* `T2` `[animal conditioning data — pigeon, rabbit, rat, mouse — not human learners]`
      - Individual subjects' learning curves showed an abrupt, often step-like increase from untrained to well-trained responding, at least as abrupt as psychometric functions in stimulus-detection experiments. *(Gallistel et al., 2004, p. 13124.)* `T2` `[animal conditioning data, not human learners]`
      - The authors concluded that rate of learning cannot be estimated from the group-average curve, and that the best measure is latency to onset of responding determined for each subject individually. *(Gallistel et al., 2004, p. 13124.)* `T2` `[animal conditioning data, not human learners]`
      - Analysing forgetting functions at the level of the individual subject rather than the group produced genuine power-shaped curves that were not attributable to averaging artifact. *(Wixted & Ebbesen, 1997, *Memory & Cognition* 25(5):731–739, p. 731, DOI 10.3758/BF03211316.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** In self-scheduled real-world practice, observed improvement is the sum of at least two processes running on different timescales plus forgetting between sessions, so what a short burst of practice measures is dominated by the fast task-set component rather than by durable skill. The conditioning data push the objection further: there the individual function is a near-step, the smooth group curve describes the distribution of onset times across subjects rather than the shape of anyone's learning, and the authors conclude that rate is not the estimable quantity at all — but that is animal conditioning, and the paper does not establish the same for human practice. Against both, individual-level power curves do genuinely occur in forgetting, so the artifact argument does not dissolve every observed power law.
    - **Link to source:** [Comparing models of learning and relearning in large-scale cognitive training data sets](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-022-00142-x), [The learning curve: implications of a quantitative analysis](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0404965101), [Genuine power curves in forgetting](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03211316)

### Category 2: Reliability of an individually estimated rate

*General idea: A rate is a slope, a slope is a difference, and a difference carries the error variance of both measurements it is built from while typically having far less true between-person variance than either. That arithmetic produced a two-decade prohibition on individual change scores, a rebuttal holding that the prohibition confuses low reliability with an absence of true individual differences, and a design literature converting the argument into a calculation with three inputs. What the empirical record adds is uncomfortable in a specific way: where fitted learning-rate parameters achieve usable retest reliability, that reliability is substantially produced by the estimator — hierarchical fitting that lets one person's estimate borrow strength from the group and from the other session — and where reliability is low, several large studies locate the cause in small between-person variance rather than in noisy measurement, which is the one diagnosis a better estimator cannot repair. This category also records what the located literature does not say: no source assembled here states a minimum number of trials required to estimate an individual rate, only observation budgets from studies that succeeded at something else.*

- **Subcategory 2.1: The difference-score prohibition, and its rebuttal**
  - **Source:** Cronbach & Furby (1970); Rogosa, Brandt & Zimowski (1982); Rogosa & Willett (1983)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Raw change scores were judged rarely useful for measuring individual change, with investigators advised to reframe their questions so that difference scores are not required. *(Cronbach & Furby, 1970, *Psychological Bulletin* 74(1):68–80, DOI 10.1037/h0029382.)* `T2`
      - The standard indictment of the difference score confuses low reliability with the absence of individual differences in true change, and the difference score is reliable when true change genuinely varies between people. *(Rogosa, Brandt & Zimowski, 1982, *Psychological Bulletin* 92(3):726–748, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.92.3.726; Rogosa & Willett, 1983, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 20(4):335–343, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1983.tb00211.x.)* `T2`
      - Two-wave difference scores should be replaced by individual growth curves fitted to multiwave data as the proper basis for studying change. *(Rogosa, Brandt & Zimowski, 1982.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** For two decades the difference score was effectively prohibited on the arithmetic that a difference between two fallible measures accumulates the error variance of both while its own true-score variance is often small, and the practical legacy is residualised gain, which removes dependence on pre-test level but does not create true-change variance where none exists. The rebuttal is not a hedge: an unreliable difference score is a diagnosis of the *sample* — everyone is changing at about the same rate — rather than a defect in the statistic, and the prescription is more measurement occasions plus an explicit growth model rather than abandonment. Whether a given rate score is measurable therefore stops being a methodological question and becomes an empirical one about between-person variance in true rate, answerable only with data.
    - **Link to source:** [How we should measure "change": Or should we?](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0029382), [A growth curve approach to the measurement of change](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.92.3.726), [Demonstrating the reliability of the difference score in the measurement of change](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1983.tb00211.x)

- **Subcategory 2.2: What determines the reliability of measured change, and the design remedy**
  - **Source:** Willett (1989); Jacobson & Truax (1991)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The reliability of individually measured change depends on exactly three quantities: the magnitude of inter-individual heterogeneity in true growth, the size of the measurement-error variance, and the number of waves of data collected. *(Willett, 1989, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 49(3):587–602, p. 587, DOI 10.1177/001316448904900309.)* `T2`
      - Dramatic increases in the reliability of change measurement can be achieved by collecting relatively few additional waves beyond the traditional two-wave design. *(Willett, 1989, p. 587.)* `T2`
      - Growth-rate reliability "confounds the unrelated influences of group heterogeneity in growth-rate and measurement precision" and must not be read as a property of the measuring instrument. *(Willett, 1989, p. 595.)* `T2`
      - The Reliable Change Index is an observed pre-post difference divided by the standard error of that difference, where the standard error is derived from the test's reliability and change is treated as reliable when the index exceeds roughly 1.96. *(Jacobson & Truax, 1991, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 59(1):12–19, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Willett converts "how many observations does a rate need?" from a rule of thumb into a calculation, because the occasion count enters through the sum of squared deviations of the measurement times, and two waves is the worst case rather than the natural design. The caveat is load-bearing: a growth-rate reliability near zero can mean a noisy instrument *or* a population in which everyone truly improves at the same rate, and the index cannot distinguish them, so a low number is not by itself an instruction to improve the instrument. The Reliable Change Index gives the individual-level form of the same dependency, since the threshold for believable change scales inversely with the square root of the instrument's reliability, and it only ever classifies a change as real or not rather than rehabilitating a rate for ranking.
    - **Link to source:** [Some results on reliability for the longitudinal measurement of change](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448904900309), [Clinical significance: a statistical approach to defining meaningful change](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12)

- **Subcategory 2.3: The observation budget, and what the located literature does not state**
  - **Source:** Willett (1989); Donner & Hardy (2015); Kumar, Benjamin, Heathcote & Steyvers (2022); Mkrtchian, Valton & Roiser (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Targeted searching across the practice-curve, psychometric-change, and computational-reliability literatures assembled here located no source stating a minimum number of trials, practice opportunities, or sessions required to estimate an individual learning rate to a specified precision. *(No such statement located in Willett, 1989; Donner & Hardy, 2015; Kumar et al., 2022; Mkrtchian et al., 2023; or any other source assembled in this document.)* `[an absence located by targeted search across the sources assembled here — not a published null result, and not a claim that no such source exists anywhere]`
      - The piecewise structure of individual learning curves was resolved on records of 500 measurements of cognitive performance per learner. *(Donner & Hardy, 2015, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 22(5):1308–1319, p. 1308, DOI 10.3758/s13423-015-0811-x.)* `T2`
      - Separating the slow skill-learning process from the fast within-session process required restricting the modelling sample to users with more than 50 sessions in a given game. *(Kumar et al., 2022, Methods, DOI 10.1038/s41539-022-00142-x.)* `T2`
      - The number of waves of data collected is one of the three determinants of change reliability, and the design gain from adding waves is largest immediately above the two-wave case. *(Willett, 1989, p. 587, DOI 10.1177/001316448904900309.)* `T2`
      - The most favourable published retest reliabilities for individually estimated learning-rate parameters were obtained on a restless four-armed bandit administered twice to fewer than 50 healthy adults. *(Mkrtchian, Valton & Roiser, 2023, *Computational Psychiatry* 7(1):30, Methods, DOI 10.5334/cpsy.86.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is no published trial count to point at, and the honest move is to record that as an absence rather than to fill it with a plausible number. What the literature supplies instead are observation budgets from studies that succeeded at something specific — roughly 500 measurements per learner before piecewise structure became visible, more than 50 sessions per learner before the slow and fast processes could be separated, and a design result saying the marginal return on added occasions is highest just above two waves. Every one of those budgets was set by what the analysis needed, not by what an individual rate estimate requires for a stated precision, so none of them transfers as a requirement.
    - **Link to source:** [Some results on reliability for the longitudinal measurement of change](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448904900309), [Piecewise power laws in individual learning curves](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0811-x), [Comparing models of learning and relearning in large-scale cognitive training data sets](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-022-00142-x), [Reliability of decision-making and reinforcement learning computational parameters](https://doi.org/10.5334/cpsy.86)

- **Subcategory 2.4: Latent-variable remedies, and what they cannot manufacture**
  - **Source:** McArdle (2009); Kievit, Brandmaier, Ziegler et al. (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Latent change score models represent change as a latent variable estimated from multiple indicators rather than as a manifest difference between two observed scores. *(McArdle, 2009, *Annual Review of Psychology* 60:577–605, DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.60.110707.163612; Kievit, Brandmaier, Ziegler et al., 2018, *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience* 33:99–117, DOI 10.1016/j.dcn.2017.11.007.)* `T2`
      - The approach separates true change from occasion-specific measurement error instead of propagating the error of both occasions into the change estimate. *(McArdle, 2009; Kievit et al., 2018.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the principal modern remedy for difference-score unreliability and it genuinely solves the error-propagation half of the problem by modelling measurement error explicitly rather than absorbing it. What it cannot do is manufacture between-person variance in true change: if everyone improves at nearly the same rate, the latent slope variance is near zero and the model faithfully returns that fact rather than a usable individual score. It also requires multiple indicators at every occasion, which is a heavier measurement burden than a single repeated task.
    - **Link to source:** [Latent variable modeling of differences and changes with longitudinal data](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.60.110707.163612), [Developmental cognitive neuroscience using latent change score models](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2017.11.007)

- **Subcategory 2.5: The reliability paradox — where the low numbers actually come from**
  - **Source:** Hedge, Powell & Sumner (2018); Enkavi, Eisenberg, Bissett, Mazza, MacKinnon, Marsch & Poldrack (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across three studies assessing test-retest reliability of seven classic cognitive tasks — Eriksen flanker, Stroop, stop-signal, go/no-go, Posner cueing, Navon, and SNARC — reliabilities ranged from 0 to .82. *(Hedge, Powell & Sumner, 2018, *Behavior Research Methods* 50(3):1166–1186, p. 1166, DOI 10.3758/s13428-017-0935-1.)* `T2`
      - Those low reliabilities arose from low variance between individuals rather than from high measurement variance. *(Hedge et al., 2018, p. 1166.)* `T2`
      - Taking these reliability estimates into account has the potential to qualitatively change published theoretical conclusions. *(Hedge et al., 2018, p. 1166.)* `T2`
      - Across an extensive self-regulation battery, dependent variables derived from self-report surveys showed high test-retest reliability while those derived from behavioural tasks did not, a pattern that held in both the literature review and the authors' new sample. *(Enkavi, Eisenberg, Bissett, Mazza, MacKinnon, Marsch & Poldrack, 2019, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 116(12):5472–5477, p. 5472, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1818430116.)* `T2`
      - That difference was confirmed to be attributable to differences in between-subject variability. *(Enkavi et al., 2019, p. 5472.)* `T2`
      - Comparing types of task-derived measures, certain model parameters were found to be as stable as raw dependent variables. *(Enkavi et al., 2019, p. 5472.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The paradox is that the property making an experimental effect robust and replicable — small between-person variability — is the same property that destroys its usefulness as an individual-differences measure, so a task can be excellent for demonstrating that learning occurs and useless for ranking who learns faster. The diagnosis matters more than the coefficients: in both bodies of data the low reliability traced to small between-person variance rather than to noisy measurement, which supports the uncomfortable reading that a better estimator will not fix it. The mild counterweight is that fitted model parameters were no worse than raw dependent variables, so the problem is not that fitted parameters are inherently noisier than the observations they come from.
    - **Link to source:** [The reliability paradox: why robust cognitive tasks do not produce reliable individual differences](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-017-0935-1), [Large-scale analysis of test–retest reliabilities of self-regulation measures](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818430116)

- **Subcategory 2.6: Measured retest reliability of fitted learning-rate parameters, and the estimator behind each number**
  - **Source:** Mkrtchian, Valton & Roiser (2023); Waltmann, Schlagenhauf & Deserno (2022); Schaaf, Weidinger, Molleman & van den Bos (2024); Moutoussis, Bullmore, Goodyer, Fonagy, Jones, Dolan & Dayan (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Healthy participants (N = 50; N = 47 for the winning model) completed a restless four-armed bandit twice, two weeks apart. *(Mkrtchian, Valton & Roiser, 2023, *Computational Psychiatry* 7(1):30, Methods, DOI 10.5334/cpsy.86.)* `T2`
      - With parameters estimated separately per session, the reward learning rate achieved ICC(A,1) = 0.60 (95% CI 0.38–0.75) and the punishment learning rate ICC(A,1) = 0.63 (95% CI 0.42–0.77). *(Mkrtchian et al., 2023, Table 1.)* `T2`
      - Estimating the correlation within a joint hierarchical Bayesian model raised those two figures to r = 0.71 (0.53–0.84) and r = 0.85 (0.69–0.95) respectively. *(Mkrtchian et al., 2023, Table 1.)* `T2`
      - The lapse parameter from the same model in the same participants achieved ICC(A,1) = 0.01 (−0.08 to 0.14), and its poor recoverability placed an upper limit on its potential reliability. *(Mkrtchian et al., 2023, Table 1.)* `T2`
      - Excellent reliability for computationally derived indices from a probabilistic reversal learning task taken twice by 40 healthy participants was obtained only when using hierarchical estimation with empirical priors *and* including data from both sessions in the fit. *(Waltmann, Schlagenhauf & Deserno, 2022, *Behavior Research Methods* 54(6):2993–3014, p. 2993, DOI 10.3758/s13428-021-01739-7.)* `T2`
      - Reliability in that study depended heavily on whether sessions were modelled separately or jointly, on the estimation method, and on which combination of parameters the model contained. *(Waltmann et al., 2022, p. 2993.)* `T2`
      - Two independent cohorts (N = 69 for a two-armed bandit, N = 47 for a reversal learning task) were tested twice online with a between-test interval of five weeks. *(Schaaf, Weidinger, Molleman & van den Bos, 2024, *Behavior Research Methods* 56(5):4582–4599, p. 4582, DOI 10.3758/s13428-023-02203-4.)* `T2`
      - ICCs for the reinforcement-learning model parameter estimates ranged from .02 to .52 for the bandit task and from .01 to .71 for the reversal learning task. *(Schaaf et al., 2024, p. 4582.)* `T2`
      - In the same participants at the same sittings, personality and cognitive measures achieved ICCs ranging from .67 to .93. *(Schaaf et al., 2024, p. 4582.)* `T2`
      - Because the procedures were shown capable of detecting high test-retest reliability, the authors ascribed a significant proportion of the variability to the participants themselves and showed that stress and happiness partly explain within-participant variability. *(Schaaf et al., 2024, p. 4582.)* `T2`
      - In a longitudinal sample using a widely used Go/No-Go task, model-derived Pavlovian effects had weak temporal stability while model fit itself was more stable. *(Moutoussis, Bullmore, Goodyer, Fonagy, Jones, Dolan & Dayan, 2018, *PLOS Computational Biology* 14(12):e1006679, p. 1, DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1006679.)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that although the computational construct correlated with important aspects of development, it does not meet conventional requirements for tracking individual development. *(Moutoussis et al., 2018, p. 1.)* `T2`
      - An independent group characterised that study as having found poor reliability of both reward and punishment learning rates over a six-month interval in adolescents, in contrast to their own two-week adult findings. *(Mkrtchian et al., 2023, Discussion.)* `T2` `[a characterisation of one study read through a second study's discussion section, not from the first study's own text]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The most favourable published figures for an individually estimated learning rate are ICCs of roughly 0.60–0.63 at two weeks in adults — "good" by conventional benchmarks, and still leaving around 40% of observed variance outside stable individual difference — while the higher figures of 0.71 to 0.85 come from joint hierarchical estimation, in which a person's session-one estimate has been informed by their session-two data. That caveat travels with the number rather than beside it: a second study obtained excellent reliability *only* under hierarchical estimation with empirical priors fit jointly across both sessions, a configuration that is legitimate for characterising stable individual differences and unavailable to anyone who must score a single sitting prospectively. Elsewhere the figures collapse — .01 to .02 at five weeks on comparable tasks, insufficient stability to track individuals over six months in adolescents, and within one model and one set of participants a parameter at 0.63 sitting alongside another at 0.01. Reliability is therefore a property of the specific parameter, the interval, the population, and the estimator, and one study locates a significant share of the instability in genuine within-participant fluctuation including mood, which no estimator improvement can remove.
    - **Link to source:** [Reliability of decision-making and reinforcement learning computational parameters](https://doi.org/10.5334/cpsy.86), [Sufficient reliability of the behavioral and computational readouts of a probabilistic reversal learning task](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-021-01739-7), [Test–retest reliability of reinforcement learning parameters](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-023-02203-4), [Change, stability, and instability in the Pavlovian guidance of behaviour](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1006679)

### Category 3: Whether learning rate is a stable individual difference at all

*General idea: The previous categories concern whether a rate can be defined and estimated. This one concerns whether there is anything there to estimate — whether people differ enough in true rate for a rate score to carry information. Three bodies of evidence bear on that and they do not agree. A large observational literature, now independently replicated, reports that once initial knowledge is modelled, students' rates of improvement per practice opportunity are nearly identical: the interquartile spread in rate is an order of magnitude smaller than the spread in starting point, and the slower half needs roughly one extra practice opportunity, not ten. Against it stand test–retest studies on implicit learning tasks that do find medium rank-order stability across a week — while also finding those stable differences uncorrelated with IQ and working memory — and a five-decade dynamic-testing tradition that does add small-to-moderate unique variance over static ability, mostly for young children on skills close to the taught content, and whose own foundational review called its central index an unsolved measurement problem. The strongest published challenge to the near-constant-rate result is methodological, and it lands exactly where an assessment would sit: capping practice sequences at the handful of opportunities a testing session could collect inflates the estimated spread in rate by 75% at ten opportunities and 205% at five.*

- **Subcategory 3.1: The eighty-year-old null, and its modern re-examination**
  - **Source:** Woodrow (1946); Voelkle, Wittmann & Ackerman (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Woodrow fitted learning curves to individual records, treated the slope parameter as the best expression of ability to improve, and concluded that "the ability to learn cannot be identified with the ability known as intelligence." *(Woodrow, 1946, *Psychological Review* 53(3):147–158, p. 148, DOI 10.1037/h0053639, as quoted in Voelkle, Wittmann & Ackerman, 2006, p. 303.)* `T2`
      - Woodrow's conclusion that no general learning-ability factor emerged from intercorrelations among practice gains ran so counter to prevailing belief that it was either ignored or vehemently criticised. *(Voelkle, Wittmann & Ackerman, 2006, *Learning and Individual Differences* 16(4):303–319, p. 303, DOI 10.1016/j.lindif.2006.01.001.)* `T2`
      - Re-examining the question with latent growth curve modelling, Voelkle and colleagues argued that abilities and skill acquisition are much more strongly related than commonly assumed once variance is decomposed appropriately. *(Voelkle et al., 2006, p. 303.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The claim that individually measured learning rates behave badly as a trait is eighty years old and was originally established on curve-slope parameters rather than on crude gain scores, and it has been treated as settled for most of that period. The modern latent-variable re-analysis argues that the null was partly a measurement failure rather than a substantive absence. That is precisely the live question for any rate score: whether there is nothing to find, or whether the instrument that went looking was inadequate.
    - **Link to source:** [The ability to learn](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0053639), [Abilities and skill acquisition: a latent growth curve approach](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2006.01.001)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The near-constant-rate finding at scale, and its independent replication**
  - **Source:** Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin (2023); Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A mixed-effects growth model was fit to 1.3 million observations across 27 datasets of real students in elementary, secondary and college math, science and language courses. *(Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin, 2023, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2221311120.)* `T2`
      - Despite prior lectures and readings, the median initial performance across datasets was 0.638 log odds, or 65.42% correct, at the start of practice. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - The median overall learning rate was 0.09 log odds per practice opportunity, converting to a 2.5 percentage-point gain per opportunity from a 65% starting point. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - The median number of practice opportunities a typical student needed to reach 80% mastery of a typical knowledge component was 7.24. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - The median standard deviation of student initial-knowledge estimates was 0.651 log odds with a median interquartile range of 0.830, versus a median standard deviation of 0.015 and interquartile range of 0.018 log odds for student learning rate. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - Expressed in percentage terms, the interquartile range of student learning rate spanned only 1.70% to 2.56% gain per opportunity, while the interquartile range of initial knowledge spanned 55.21% to 75.17% correct. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - A typical student in the bottom half of learning rate needed a median 7.89 opportunities to reach mastery versus 6.94 for the top half, whereas the bottom half of initial knowledge needed 13.13 opportunities versus 3.66 for the top half. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - Learning-rate variation across knowledge components was orders of magnitude larger than across students, with a median standard deviation of 0.102 and an interquartile range of 0.132 log odds, seven times the student figure. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - Disaggregated by domain, the median interquartile range of student learning rate was 0.045 log odds in the six language datasets versus 0.017 in the 16 math and 0.008 in the five science datasets, and by grade level it was 0.015 for upper elementary, 0.018 for middle and high school, and 0.018 for college. *(Koedinger et al., 2023.)* `T2`
      - The finding was independently replicated on 821,890 observations from more than 15,000 students across six math topics in an intelligent tutoring system during the 2022–23 school year, in a live "business as usual" context with a student population broadly representative of US K-12. *(Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali, 2024, *Proceedings of the 17th International Conference on Educational Data Mining*, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.12729850.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** On the largest observational evidence base available, differences in learning rate are roughly an order of magnitude smaller than differences in starting point, and the gap they produce is about one extra practice opportunity against the ten-opportunity gap produced by prior knowledge. Rate varies far more across *what is being learned* than across *who is learning it* — seven times more on the same statistic — which relocates the interesting variance from the child to the material. It is not a single-lab artifact: the result replicates on a much larger, more representative, live tutoring corpus.
    - **Link to source:** [An astonishing regularity in student learning rate](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2221311120), [Independent replication of the student learning-rate regularity on a live tutoring corpus](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.12729850)

- **Subcategory 3.3: The truncation sensitivity that challenges the estimate**
  - **Source:** Lee, Lichand, Barnard, Klotz, Thille, Kim & Domingue (2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Refitting the same model on 26 of the original 27 datasets while truncating practice sequences showed that capping at the first ten practice opportunities per student–skill pair inflated the median estimated interquartile range of student learning rates by 75%. *(Lee, Lichand, Barnard, Klotz, Thille, Kim & Domingue, 2026, *Proceedings of the Thirteenth ACM Conference on Learning @ Scale*, DOI 10.1145/3774398.3811619.)* `T2`
      - Capping at the first five opportunities inflated the same quantity by 205%, with individual datasets ranging from negligible to seventeen-fold. *(Lee et al., 2026.)* `T2`
      - The authors state explicitly that their results do not establish that students learn at substantially different rates, only that the published estimate of small slope variation warrants checking against which observations enter the fit. *(Lee et al., 2026.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The strongest published challenge to the near-constant-rate result is methodological rather than substantive, and it lands exactly where an assessment would sit: short practice sequences manufacture apparent rate differences, inflating the rate-variance estimate by 75% at ten opportunities and 205% at five. That is the observation budget a testing session could realistically collect, so the regime in which a rate would actually be measured is the regime most contaminated by this artifact, and the bias runs toward *seeing* differences that longer sequences do not sustain. The authors decline to claim that rates do differ, which keeps this a caution about estimation rather than a competing substantive finding.
    - **Link to source:** [Truncation sensitivity of the estimated variance in student learning rates](https://doi.org/10.1145/3774398.3811619)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Where stable rate differences do appear, and what they are not correlated with**
  - **Source:** Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn (2019); Stark-Inbar, Raza, Taylor & Ivry (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a test–retest study in which healthy young adults performed four implicit learning tasks twice about a week apart, three of the four — probabilistic classification, serial response, and implicit category learning — showed medium test–retest reliability by Cohen's guidelines, while artificial grammar learning did not. *(Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn, 2019, *Cognition*, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2019.05.007.)* `T2`
      - Implicit learning on all four tasks was uncorrelated with independent measures of IQ, working memory, and explicit learning ability. *(Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn, 2019.)* `T2`
      - Participants tested twice showed learning that was reliable at the individual level in visuomotor adaptation and in the alternating reaction time task, but not in the serial reaction time task. *(Stark-Inbar, Raza, Taylor & Ivry, 2017, *Journal of Neurophysiology* 117(1):412–428, p. 412, DOI 10.1152/jn.01141.2015.)* `T2`
      - Individual learning measures did not correlate between sensorimotor adaptation and sequence learning. *(Stark-Inbar et al., 2017, p. 412.)* `T2`
      - Faster adaptation was associated with lower performance variability, whereas greater sequence learning was associated with higher variability and slower reaction times. *(Stark-Inbar et al., 2017, p. 412.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Stable individual differences in learning do exist, but they have been demonstrated on implicit tasks across roughly a week rather than on academic practice across a term, and where they are stable they are uncorrelated with IQ, working memory, and explicit learning ability. Reliably measured learning rates in two implicit motor domains also failed to correlate with each other, and the sign of the variability–learning relationship reversed between them, which argues against a single underlying learning-speed capacity. The disagreement with the near-constant-rate literature may therefore be about task type and timescale rather than about the phenomenon, and demonstrating individual-level reliability was treated by these authors as a prerequisite to be shown before any individual-differences claim rather than assumed.
    - **Link to source:** [Evidence of stable individual differences in implicit learning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2019.05.007), [Individual differences in implicit motor learning: task specificity in sensorimotor adaptation and sequence learning](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01141.2015)

- **Subcategory 3.5: What the dynamic-testing tradition delivers over static ability**
  - **Source:** Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs (2008); Dixon, Oxley, Gellert & Nash (2023); Dixon, Oxley, Nash & Gellert (2023); Wood, Biggs & Molnar (2024); Grigorenko & Sternberg (1998); Fuchs, Fuchs, Compton, Hollenbeck, Craddock & Hamlett (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A mixed-methods review of 24 dynamic-assessment studies, with correlational analysis on 15 of them, found dynamic assessment showed superior predictive validity to traditional testing when feedback was not contingent on student response, when applied to students with disabilities rather than at-risk or typically achieving students, and when the criterion was an independent dynamic or criterion-referenced test rather than a norm-referenced test or teacher judgment. *(Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs, 2008, *The Journal of Special Education*, DOI 10.1177/0022466907310366.)* `T2`
      - A systematic review of 17 peer-reviewed articles reporting 18 studies published 1992–2020 found that, after static predictors were accounted for, dynamic measures of phonological awareness and decoding explained 1–21% of variance in the growth of word-reading accuracy and typically 1–9% in word-reading fluency. *(Dixon, Oxley, Gellert & Nash, 2023, *Reading and Writing*, DOI 10.1007/s11145-022-10312-3.)* `T2`
      - In that same review, dynamic measures of morphological awareness accounted for 4–33.4% of variance in reading-comprehension outcomes, while one dynamic decoding assessment accounted for 1%. *(Dixon, Oxley, Gellert & Nash, 2023.)* `T2`
      - A random-effects meta-analysis of 35 studies yielding 192 effect sizes found an overall pooled effect of g = 0.49 between dynamic assessments of word reading and word-reading outcome measures in children aged 4–10. *(Wood, Biggs & Molnar, 2024, *Perspectives of the ASHA Special Interest Groups*, DOI 10.1044/2024_persp-23-00262.)* `T2`
      - That meta-analysis found dynamic assessment significantly better suited to children aged 4–5 and 6–7 than to children aged 8–9 (p = .0001), with validity otherwise consistent across typically developing versus at-risk and bilingual versus monolingual participants. *(Wood, Biggs & Molnar, 2024.)* `T2`
      - A companion systematic review of 14 articles found the predictive power of dynamic assessment of decoding and phonological awareness appeared to wane with increasing age as static measures explained more variance in outcomes. *(Dixon, Oxley, Nash & Gellert, 2023, *Journal of Learning Disabilities*, DOI 10.1177/00222194221117510.)* `T2`
      - The canonical review of dynamic testing concluded that the field had no consensus on the psychometrics of measuring change and treated the pretest-to-posttest difference score as its central unresolved measurement problem. *(Grigorenko & Sternberg, 1998, *Psychological Bulletin*, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.124.1.75.)* `T2` `[a publisher correction is on record at doi:10.1037/0033-2909.124.3.443 — a correction, not a retraction]`
      - In a study of 122 third graders assessed in the fall and randomly assigned to 16 weeks of validated versus conventional word-problem instruction, structural equation models showed the dynamic assessment measured a dimension of pretreatment ability distinct from language, nonverbal reasoning, attentive behavior, calculation skill and word-problem skill. *(Fuchs, Fuchs, Compton, Hollenbeck, Craddock & Hamlett, 2008, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, DOI 10.1037/a0012657.)* `T2`
      - In that study, instruction condition alone accounted for the year-end word-problem outcome proximal to instruction, whereas language, pretreatment math skill and the dynamic assessment were all needed to forecast the outcome more distal to instruction. *(Fuchs et al., 2008.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Dynamic testing does add something over static ability, but the increment is small-to-moderate, narrow, and conditional — largest for the youngest children, for skills close to what was taught, and for far-transfer criteria that static pretests underdetermine — and it shrinks with age as static measures catch up, which is the wrong direction for a selection instrument most needed on older applicants and untaught content. Its own foundational reviewers flagged that the tradition's headline index is a change score whose psychometrics were never settled, the same unresolved problem the rest of this document circles. The measured advantage also varies with how feedback is delivered and which criterion is chosen, two degrees of freedom that can inflate apparent validity independently of anything the child did.
    - **Link to source:** [Predictive validity of dynamic assessment: a review](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022466907310366), [Dynamic assessment as a predictor of reading development: a systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11145-022-10312-3), [Dynamic assessment of decoding and phonological awareness with increasing age](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222194221117510), [Meta-analysis of dynamic assessment of word reading](https://doi.org/10.1044/2024_persp-23-00262), [Dynamic testing (Psychological Bulletin review)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.124.1.75), [Dynamic assessment of pretreatment ability in third-grade word-problem instruction](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012657)

### Category 4: Practice, retest and contamination

*General idea: Suppose a rate is well defined, estimable, and genuinely varies between children. A score still has to establish that the observed climb is the thing it claims to be, and the retest literature says a substantial share of it is not. Taking a cognitive ability test a second time with no instruction in between buys about a third of a standard deviation, rising to half by the third sitting before plateauing, and the size of that free gain depends on item format, form equivalence, and retest interval — the very parameters a rate-scoring instrument would set for itself. Those gains are not construct-neutral: in one high-stakes sample the retest score was less g-loaded, more memory-loaded, and no longer predicted the criterion at all, and in large applicant samples they accrued unequally across demographic groups and unequally across item formats. Running underneath all of it is a separate finding from the motor- and verbal-learning literatures: performance observed during acquisition is an established poor proxy for durable learning, and some instructional manipulations move the two in opposite directions.*

- **Subcategory 4.1: The size of the free gain from mere repetition**
  - **Source:** Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018); Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A longitudinal multilevel meta-analysis of 174 samples from 122 studies, yielding 786 test outcomes and a total sample of 153,185, found a standardized mean change of 0.33 from the first to the second administration of a cognitive ability test with no intervention in between. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence*, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T2`
      - That meta-analysis found gains of 0.50 from first to third administration and 0.17 from second to third, with no further score gains after the third administration. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018.)* `T2`
      - Retest gains from first to second administration differed significantly by test content, at 0.18 for figural, 0.33 for verbal and 0.41 for tests mixing several contents, with equivalence of test forms, retest interval and participant age also significant moderators. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018.)* `T2`
      - A meta-analysis of 50 studies covering 107 samples and 134,436 participants found an adjusted overall retest effect of d = .26 for cognitive ability tests in selection settings, larger when practice was accompanied by coaching and when identical rather than alternate forms were used. *(Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard, 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Simply taking a cognitive test a second time buys about a third of a standard deviation with no learning intervention at all, rising to half a standard deviation by the third sitting and then flattening. The size of that free gain depends on item format, form equivalence, and interval — exactly the design parameters a rate-scoring instrument controls — so the instrument's own construction partly determines how much improvement it will observe. Any rate measured across two occasions therefore begins with a gain the retest literature can produce without any learning having occurred.
    - **Link to source:** [Retest effects in cognitive ability tests: a longitudinal meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003), [Retesting in selection: a meta-analysis of practice effects](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373)

- **Subcategory 4.2: What the retest score turns out to measure instead**
  - **Source:** Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a latent-variable analysis of 941 candidates taking a cognitive ability test in a high-stakes context, no measurement bias was present at initial testing, but retesting introduced both measurement and predictive bias. *(Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad, 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672.)* `T2`
      - The retest factor was less saturated with g and more associated with memory. *(Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad, 2007.)* `T2`
      - Those changes eliminated the test's criterion-related validity. *(Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad, 2007.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The gain from a second administration is not construct-neutral, and in this sample it was not even construct-preserving. The retest score was less g-loaded, more memory-loaded, and no longer predicted the criterion at all, which inverts the intuition that observing a candidate twice yields better measurement than observing them once. A larger observed climb can therefore mean less valid measurement rather than more learning.
    - **Link to source:** [Measurement and predictive invariance under high-stakes retesting](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672)

- **Subcategory 4.3: Unequal retest gains across groups and formats**
  - **Source:** Schleicher, Van Iddekinge, Morgeson & Campion (2010)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across large applicant samples taking three written tests (N = 7,031) and five performance tests (N = 2,060), White applicants showed larger retest score improvements than Black or Hispanic applicants on several of the assessments. *(Schleicher, Van Iddekinge, Morgeson & Campion, 2010, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, DOI 10.1037/a0018920.)* `T2`
      - That differential was larger on written tests than on performance tests. *(Schleicher et al., 2010.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Retest improvement accrued unequally by demographic group in large real applicant samples, and unequally by test format within the same programme. A score built out of improvement therefore encodes differential test-wiseness alongside whatever learning signal it carries, and does so in the component a rate score treats as the entire signal. Because the differential was format-dependent, the choice of item type is itself a fairness decision rather than a neutral design detail.
    - **Link to source:** [Race, retesting, and score improvement in applicant samples](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018920)

- **Subcategory 4.4: Performance during acquisition versus durable learning**
  - **Source:** Soderstrom & Bjork (2015); Kumar, Benjamin, Heathcote & Steyvers (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An integrative review of the motor- and verbal-learning literatures concluded that performance observed during acquisition is often an unreliable index of long-term learning and that improvements in performance can fail to yield durable learning. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, DOI 10.1177/1745691615569000.)* `T2`
      - That review found that certain instructional manipulations have opposite effects on performance and on learning. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Learners themselves routinely misinterpret their performance during acquisition as a reliable guide to what they have durably learned. *(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015.)* `T2`
      - Within a single session, cognitive-training learners gained 50–70% of asymptotic performance, while gains across sessions were slow but steady. *(Kumar, Benjamin, Heathcote & Steyvers, 2022, Results, DOI 10.1038/s41539-022-00142-x.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A climb observed inside a single session is not evidence of durable learning, and under some instructional manipulations the two move in opposite directions, so a steeper observed curve can indicate less learning rather than more. Most of a single sitting's improvement is the fast within-session component, which decays between sessions rather than accumulating into skill. A single-session climb and a term of learning may therefore be different constructs rather than the same construct measured over different lengths — and the learners themselves are systematically wrong about which one they are experiencing.
    - **Link to source:** [Learning versus performance: an integrative review](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000), [Comparing models of learning and relearning in large-scale cognitive training data sets](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-022-00142-x)

### Category 5: The decision-grade bar, and the ladder of weaker claims

*General idea: The published testing standards and the psychometric literature treat "this score correlates with something" and "this score may help decide a child's placement" as two different claims carrying two different evidentiary burdens. The second requires classification evidence — decision consistency and decision accuracy — that cannot be read off a reliability coefficient, plus error reported at the cut, subgroup fairness evidence at the cut, and a demonstrated increment over information already in hand. The standards impose these obligations on a measure that merely contributes to a decision as well: growth and difference scores used for individual students must carry their own validity, reliability, and fairness evidence, and nothing exempts a non-decisive input. Where an absolute individual rate cannot meet that bar, the literature supplies a descending ladder of weaker uses — rank, coarse band, tie-breaker, follow-up flag, research-only signal — but each rung has its own preconditions, and two of them are hostile to intuition: partial pooling can preserve a child's rank while collapsing the spread that made ranking meaningful, and the tie-breaker rung concentrates a weak measure exactly where its error rate is highest.*

- **Subcategory 5.1: What a consequential score use must clear**
  - **Source:** Kane (2013); AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Livingston & Lewis (1995); Hunsley & Meyer (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - It is the proposed score interpretations and uses that are validated, not the test or the test scores. *(Kane, 2013, *Journal of Educational Measurement*, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12000.)* `T2`
      - Validating the score interpretation on which a score use is based does not validate the score use. *(Kane, 2013.)* `T2`
      - More-ambitious claims require more support than less-ambitious claims. *(Kane, 2013.)* `T2`
      - Evaluating a score use requires evaluating the consequences of that use, and negative consequences can render a score use unacceptable. *(Kane, 2013.)* `T2`
      - Rejecting a score use does not necessarily invalidate the prior, underlying score interpretation. *(Kane, 2013.)* `T2`
      - When a test or combination of measures is used to make classification decisions, estimates should be provided of the percentage of test takers who would be classified the same way on two replications of the procedure. *(AERA, APA & NCME, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, 2014, Standard 2.16.)* `T2`
      - The standard error of measurement cannot be translated into an expected percentage of consistent or accurate decisions without strong assumptions about the distributions of measurement errors and true scores. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 2.16 comment.)* `T2`
      - Decision accuracy and decision consistency are separately estimated quantities, and the published method for estimating them applies to composite scores produced by any scoring method, including a weighted composite. *(Livingston & Lewis, 1995, *Journal of Educational Measurement*, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x.)* `T2`
      - Where cut scores are specified for selection or classification, standard errors of measurement should be reported in the vicinity of each cut score. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 2.14.)* `T2`
      - When there is credible evidence that conditional standard errors of measurement will differ substantially across subgroups, the extent and impact of those differences should be investigated and reported. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 2.15.)* `T2`
      - A predictor may show no significant differential item functioning yet still show group differences in predictive relations, so measurement bias and predictive bias must be investigated independently. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, ch. 3.)* `T2`
      - Correlation coefficients provide inadequate evidence for or against a differential-prediction hypothesis when groups differ in means and variances on the test and the criterion. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 3.7.)* `T2`
      - When test scores are used in conjunction with other variables to predict an outcome, analyses should include those additional variables, and it is often informative to estimate the increment in predictive accuracy as each variable is introduced in addition to all others available. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 1.19.)* `T2`
      - Empirically derived weights for combining predictors can capitalize on chance factors in a given sample, so multi-predictor analyses should be verified by cross-validation or equivalent analysis. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 1.19 comment.)* `T2`
      - There has been insufficient effort in most areas of applied psychology to evaluate incremental validity at all. *(Hunsley & Meyer, 2003, *Psychological Assessment*, DOI 10.1037/1040-3590.15.4.446.)* `T2`
      - Applying group-level (nomothetic) incremental-validity findings to an individual case is itself an identified methodological challenge rather than a solved step. *(Hunsley & Meyer, 2003.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The bar is not a reliability number; it is a use-specific argument that must carry classification evidence a reliability coefficient cannot supply. A measure entering a consequential decision owes decision consistency and decision accuracy estimates, error reported at the cut rather than on average, subgroup fairness evidence treated as separate from measurement bias, and a demonstrated increment over what is already measured — an increment the literature reports is seldom evaluated at all. Kane's separation of interpretation from use is the structural point, and it runs in both directions: a rate interpretation can stay defensible for research while the gating use built on it is rejected, and rejecting the use does not retroactively invalidate the interpretation.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (2014)](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf), [Validating the interpretations and uses of test scores](https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12000), [Estimating the consistency and accuracy of classifications based on test scores](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x), [The incremental validity of psychological testing and assessment](https://doi.org/10.1037/1040-3590.15.4.446)

- **Subcategory 5.2: Why a merely contributing signal does not escape the requirements**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A decision or characterization that will have major impact on a student should take into consideration not just scores from a single test but other relevant information, and those additional data sources should themselves demonstrate information relevant to the intended construct. *(AERA, APA & NCME, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, 2014, Standard 12.10.)* `T2`
      - When difference or growth scores are used for individual students, such scores should be clearly defined, and evidence of their validity, reliability/precision, and fairness should be reported. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 12.11.)* `T2`
      - Where complex statistical models are used to predict scores for individual students, the method for constructing the models should be made explicit and justified, with supporting technical and interpretive information provided to score users. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 12.11.)* `T2`
      - In testing individuals for diagnostic or special-program placement purposes, test scores should not be the sole indicators of an individual's functioning or competence. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 3.18.)* `T2`
      - It may be neither advisable nor lawful to admit students automatically on a single measured threshold without considering additional relevant information about their performance. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 12.10 comment.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The multiple-measures requirement is routinely read as licence to add a weak signal, but the standards attach validity, reliability, and fairness obligations to individual growth and difference scores directly, and require any supplementary information used in a major-impact decision to be construct-relevant in its own right. A "contributing but not decisive" rate score therefore inherits the requirements rather than escaping them, and the fairness obligation is explicit rather than implied. The same provisions also block the opposite move — automatic admission on a single measured threshold — so neither sole reliance nor unexamined supplementation is available as a way out.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (2014)](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)

- **Subcategory 5.3: Rank and band — the spread requirement, and what makes a reference distribution defensible**
  - **Source:** Willett (1989); AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Kanaya, Scullin & Ceci (2003); Kane & Staiger (2002)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Because inter-individual heterogeneity in true growth is one of its inputs, growth-rate reliability confounds group heterogeneity in growth rate with measurement precision and is not an index of the instrument's reliability. *(Willett, 1989, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 49(3):587–602, DOI 10.1177/001316448904900309.)* `T2`
      - Reports of norming studies should include precise specification of the population sampled, sampling procedures and participation rates, any weighting of the sample, the dates of testing, and descriptive statistics, and technical documentation should indicate the precision of the norms themselves. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 5.9.)* `T2`
      - The information provided about norms should be sufficient to let users judge whether the norms are appropriate for interpreting the scores of local examinees. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 5.9 comment.)* `T2`
      - A test publisher is responsible for renorming with sufficient frequency to permit continued accurate and appropriate score interpretation, and users must either have up-to-date norms or evidence that older norms remain appropriate. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 5.11.)* `T2`
      - In longitudinal records from nine U.S. sites, students in the borderline and mild mental-retardation range lost an average of 5.6 IQ points when retested on a renormed test and were more likely to be classified as mentally retarded than peers retested on the same test. *(Kanaya, Scullin & Ceci, 2003, *American Psychologist*, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.58.10.778.)* `T2`
      - Children of the same cognitive ability could be classified differently depending on which test norms were in use in the year they were tested. *(Kanaya, Scullin & Ceci, 2003.)* `T2`
      - Because the median U.S. elementary school contained only about 69 children per grade level, school-level mean test-score measures are quite imprecise, and rewards or sanctions keyed to either extreme of the distribution primarily affect small units. *(Kane & Staiger, 2002, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, DOI 10.1257/089533002320950993.)* `T2`
      - When an individual student's scores from different tests are compared, the reliability and standard error of the difference score are affected by the relationship between the constructs measured as well as by each test's standard error, and overlapping constructs can render difference-score reliability low. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 12.12.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Ranking is not a free downgrade from point estimation: the same quantity that makes an individual growth rate estimable — genuine between-child heterogeneity in true growth — is what a rank needs in order to carry information, and Willett's decomposition makes that dependence explicit alongside error variance and occasion count. A coarse band additionally needs a reference distribution whose sampled population, participation, weighting, testing dates, and precision are documented and current, and the renorming evidence shows what happens when that condition lapses, with a 5.6-point average shift changing who fell on which side of a classification line. Noise in small units is worst at the extremes of a distribution, which is where band and rank cutoffs usually sit.
    - **Link to source:** [Some results on reliability for the longitudinal measurement of change](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448904900309), [Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (2014)](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf), [Rising IQ norms and mental-retardation classification](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.58.10.778), [Imprecision in school-level test-score measures](https://doi.org/10.1257/089533002320950993)

- **Subcategory 5.4: Shrinkage against rank**
  - **Source:** Shen & Louis (1998); Lockwood, Louis & McCaffrey (2002)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In the two-stage compound sampling model the posterior means are optimal under squared-error loss but can perform poorly in estimating the histogram of the parameters or in ranking them. *(Shen & Louis, 1998, *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B* 60(2):455–471, DOI 10.1111/1467-9868.00135.)* `T2`
      - No single set of estimates can simultaneously optimize unit-specific estimation, estimation of the parameter histogram, and estimation of the ranks. *(Shen & Louis, 1998.)* `T2`
      - Ranks derived from posterior means are themselves shrunk toward the mid-rank of the set and are generally not integers. *(Shen & Louis, 1998.)* `T2`
      - An estimator constructed to optimize the empirical distribution function and the ranks outperformed both posterior means and constrained Bayes estimates on those goals, at the cost of some squared-error optimality on the individual estimates. *(Shen & Louis, 1998.)* `T2`
      - The mean squared error of percentile (rank) estimates depends on the signal-to-noise ratio — the ratio of the unit-level variance component to the variance of the unit-specific estimator — and only moderately on the number of units being ranked. *(Lockwood, Louis & McCaffrey, 2002, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 27(3):255–270, DOI 10.3102/10769986027003255.)* `T2`
      - Mean squared error of percentile estimates is large for commonly encountered variance ratios, and an unrealistically large ratio is required for ideal ranking performance. *(Lockwood, Louis & McCaffrey, 2002.)* `T2`
      - Percentile-specific mean squared error interacts with the variance ratio especially at extreme percentiles, degrading decision rules that key on identifying the extremes. *(Lockwood, Louis & McCaffrey, 2002.)* `T2`
      - Even optimal percentile estimators must be assessed for whether they perform well enough to be used in evaluative decisions at all. *(Lockwood, Louis & McCaffrey, 2002.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Partial pooling does not protect a ranking use, it distorts it in a specific direction: posterior means shrink, the ranks built from them shrink toward the mid-rank, and no estimator optimizes individual estimates, the spread, and the ordering at once. A purpose-built estimator does recover ranks better than posterior means, so the problem is tractable in principle — but rank accuracy is governed by a signal-to-noise ratio described as unfavourable at realistic values and worst at the extreme percentiles, which are exactly the ones a selection rule reads. These two results share an author, which makes the pairing a constraint asserted from inside the research programme that built the estimators rather than an objection from outside it.
    - **Link to source:** [Triple-goal estimates in two-stage hierarchical models](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9868.00135), [Uncertainty in rank estimation: implications for value-added modeling accountability systems](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986027003255)

- **Subcategory 5.5: The tie-breaker applied at the margin, and an absence in the literature**
  - **Source:** Lathrop (1986); Jølstad & Gustavsson (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - For most examinees the unreliability of a well-designed assessment is not sufficient to cause misclassification, but for the significant portion of examinees with scores near the cutting score the risk of misclassification is substantial even for instruments with relatively high retest reliability. *(Lathrop, 1986, *The Journal of Educational Research*, DOI 10.1080/00220671.1986.10885683.)* `T2`
      - A tie-breaker treated as a secondary consideration has, by construction, either infinitesimal value or value lexically subordinate to the primary criteria. *(Jølstad & Gustavsson, 2023, *Journal of Medical Ethics*, DOI 10.1136/jme-2023-108972.)* `T2`
      - The "second tie-breaker problem" asks whether a factor that is significant only when there are no relevant differences among options on primary criteria can plausibly be significant enough to overcome the reasons for preferring a lottery. *(Jølstad & Gustavsson, 2023.)* `T2`
      - The instances where a tie-breaker seems necessary are precisely the situations where there are strong reasons for preferring a lottery, and factors judged genuinely valuable should be included among the primary considerations rather than held back as tie-breakers. *(Jølstad & Gustavsson, 2023.)* `T2`
      - Targeted searching located this tie-breaker argument in the medical-ethics priority-setting literature and did not locate an equivalent developed treatment in the educational-measurement literature on multiple-criteria identification, which reports combination-rule effects on classification without addressing whether marginal-only application of a weak measure is defensible. *(Jølstad & Gustavsson, 2023; Lathrop, 1986.)* `T2` `[an absence located by targeted search, not a published null result]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Misclassification risk concentrates near the cut even for instruments with relatively high retest reliability, so the tie-breaker rung applies a weak measure precisely to the examinees whose error rate is highest — the opposite of the low-stakes hedge it is usually presented as. The normative argument that a marginal-only tie-breaker is self-undermining, being either too small to matter or large enough that it belonged among the primary criteria, is developed in medical-ethics priority setting rather than in educational measurement. That absence is itself the finding: the tie-breaker rung has no measurement-side defence to lean on.
    - **Link to source:** [Reliability of cutting-score decisions near the cut](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671.1986.10885683), [Tie-breakers, lotteries, and the second tie-breaker problem](https://doi.org/10.1136/jme-2023-108972)

- **Subcategory 5.6: Follow-up flag, research-only signal, and what a weak input does inside a combination rule**
  - **Source:** Glover & Albers (2007); McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014); AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Universal screening is framed as a prerequisite to providing early prevention and intervention services rather than as a placement determination. *(Glover & Albers, 2007, *Journal of School Psychology*, DOI 10.1016/j.jsp.2006.05.005.)* `T2`
      - A screener must be evaluated for appropriateness to the specific context and purpose, technical adequacy, and usability, and criteria for making those judgements were not readily available to those working in educational settings. *(Glover & Albers, 2007.)* `T2`
      - Choice of combination rule — conjunctive ("and"), disjunctive or complementary ("or"), or compensatory ("mean") — changes the size of the identified population, the ability heterogeneity of the identified students, and the psychometric performance of a multiple-criteria identification system. *(McBee, Peters & Waterman, 2014, *Gifted Child Quarterly*, DOI 10.1177/0016986213513794.)* `T2`
      - That combination-rule analysis was conducted by statistical simulation of an operational, mandated, multiple-criteria state identification system. *(McBee, Peters & Waterman, 2014.)* `T2`
      - Where tests are selected for evaluation or accountability settings, the intended uses and the consequences they are expected to promote should be clearly described, together with cautions against inappropriate uses. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 13.7.)* `T2`
      - Claims that a testing use will improve outcomes should be made explicit when the system is adopted, and evidence supporting their validity should be provided when available. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 13.7 comment.)* `T2`
      - Multiple sources of information should be used for diagnostic and placement purposes, alternative explanations for test performance considered, and the professional judgement of someone familiar with the test brought to bear. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 3.18.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The two lowest rungs are the only ones whose evidentiary preconditions are already articulated as ordinary practice: a screening flag is judged on fit to purpose, technical adequacy, and usability rather than on fitness to place a child, and a research-only signal is governed by an explicit statement of intended use with cautions attached. The rung immediately above them is where the ladder metaphor leaks, because a measure described as one input among several is still combined by some rule, and rule choice alone changes who is identified and how psychometrically sound the whole system is. Nothing in the standards treats "one input among several" as a lighter evidentiary category, so descending the ladder buys less relief than it appears to.
    - **Link to source:** [Considerations for evaluating universal screening assessments](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2006.05.005), [Combination rules and the composition of the identified gifted population](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986213513794), [Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (2014)](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)
