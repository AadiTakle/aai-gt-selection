# Candidate Tensions — Scoring a Learning-Rate Measure

Raw material for the owner's DOK 3 work. Every line records a place where two sources in the
research base disagree, and names both. Nothing here is a conclusion, a resolution, or a
recommendation — a tension becomes an insight only once the owner has interrogated it.

---

## 1. The functional form of the practice curve

- Newell & Rosenbloom report the power function fitting better than the exponential in all 15 averaged data sets, while Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort report the exponential fitting better than the power function in all of the unaveraged data sets.
- Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort report the exponential beating the power function in every unaveraged data set, while Wixted & Ebbesen report genuine, non-artifactual power curves in individual-subject forgetting functions.
- Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote find a *delayed* exponential — slower-faster-slower — selected in the majority of paradigms, contradicting the assumption shared by Newell & Rosenbloom's power law and Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort's plain exponential that improvement always decelerates monotonically.
- Donner & Hardy find individual curves to be piecewise *power* laws joined at strategy transitions, whereas Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort and Evans et al. find exponential-family forms at the individual level.
- Gallistel, Fairhurst & Balsam find individual learning to be abrupt and step-like, so that rate is not estimable and onset latency is the correct measure, whereas the practice-curve literature represented by Heathcote et al. and Evans et al. treats individual learning as a smooth continuous function with an estimable rate — though the two rest on animal conditioning and human skill data respectively.
- Delaney, Reder, Staszewski & Ritter locate the well-defined practice function at the level of the *strategy*, while Rickard attributes the same departures to discrete shifts between algorithmic computation and direct memory retrieval.
- Kumar, Benjamin, Heathcote & Steyvers require two interacting processes on different timescales to account for real-world practice, while the single-parameter rate assumed across the classical form literature treats improvement as one process.

## 2. The averaging artifact, and its limits

- Anderson & Tweney report the averaging artifact is small under normally or rectangularly distributed component slopes with noise-free measurement, while the artifact is invoked by Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort and by Haider & Frensch as a general explanation for observed power laws.
- Anderson shows in simulation that averaging drives aggregates toward power form irrespective of component type, while Newell & Rosenbloom's original inference read an aggregate power law as evidence about individual form.
- Myung, Kim & Pitt locate the artifact in nonlinearity plus individual differences generally, while Anderson & Tweney's originating account ties its magnitude specifically to intercomponent slope heterogeneity, noise-distribution shape, and range restriction.
- Estes establishes that group curves need not share individual form as a general result, while Wixted & Ebbesen show individual-level power curves do occur — so an observed power law is neither diagnostic nor automatically artifactual.

## 3. Whether a difference or rate score can be measured at all

- Cronbach & Furby hold that individual change scores are rarely usable, while Rogosa, Brandt & Zimowski and Rogosa & Willett hold that low difference-score reliability diagnoses an absence of true between-person variation in change rather than a defect in the statistic.
- Woodrow concludes from curve-slope parameters that no general learning ability exists, while Voelkle, Wittmann & Ackerman argue via latent growth curve modelling that abilities and skill acquisition are far more strongly related than the classical null implies.
- Willett shows growth-rate reliability rises sharply with a few added waves, while Hedge, Powell & Sumner and Enkavi et al. locate the binding constraint in low between-person variance, which additional waves do not increase.
- Willett's design result treats added occasions as the remedy, while Willett's own caution states that growth-rate reliability confounds group heterogeneity with measurement precision, so a higher coefficient need not mean a better instrument.
- McArdle and Kievit et al. offer latent change score models as the remedy for error propagation, while Hedge, Powell & Sumner and Enkavi et al. locate the problem in an absence of true between-person variance that no latent model can supply.
- Jacobson & Truax define reliable change against the instrument's own reliability, while Rogosa, Brandt & Zimowski argue the relevant question is between-person variance in true change rather than instrument precision.

## 4. Measured reliability of fitted rate parameters

- Mkrtchian, Valton & Roiser obtain ICCs of 0.60 and 0.63 for reward and punishment learning rates at two weeks in adults, while Moutoussis et al. find learning-related parameters insufficiently stable to track individuals over six months in adolescents, and Schaaf et al. obtain ICCs as low as .01–.02 at five weeks.
- Waltmann, Schlagenhauf & Deserno achieve excellent reliability through hierarchical estimation with empirical priors fit jointly over both sessions, while Schaaf et al. locate a significant share of the instability in genuine within-participant fluctuation including mood, which estimator improvements cannot remove.
- Mkrtchian, Valton & Roiser report ICC = 0.63 for one parameter and ICC = 0.01 for another inside the same model and the same participants, while the field's common practice treats retest reliability as a property of the task.
- Enkavi et al. find model-derived parameters as stable as raw dependent variables, while Mkrtchian, Valton & Roiser report a fitted parameter whose poor recoverability caps its achievable reliability.
- Stark-Inbar, Raza, Taylor & Ivry establish individual-level reliability on two of three implicit motor tasks, while Schaaf et al. and Moutoussis et al. report reinforcement-learning parameters failing that same prerequisite.

## 5. Whether learning rate varies between people

- Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin report the interquartile range of student learning rate spanning only 1.70%–2.56% gain per opportunity, while Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn report medium rank-order stability of individual differences in implicit learning across a one-week retest.
- Koedinger et al.'s regularity replicates on 15,000+ students in Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali, while Lee et al.'s refit of 26 of the same 27 datasets shows the rate-variance estimate inflating 75%–205% when practice sequences are truncated to the five-to-ten observations a testing session could realistically collect.
- Lee et al. demonstrate that the small-variance estimate is sensitive to which observations enter the fit, while Lee et al. also state explicitly that their results do not establish that students learn at substantially different rates.
- Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn find stable individual differences in implicit learning uncorrelated with IQ and working memory, while Voelkle, Wittmann & Ackerman argue abilities and skill acquisition are strongly related once variance is decomposed.
- Stark-Inbar, Raza, Taylor & Ivry find reliably measured learning rates in two implicit motor tasks that do not correlate with each other, while a single "learning rate" construct is presupposed wherever one score is proposed.
- Koedinger et al. find learning-rate variation across knowledge components seven times larger than across students, while selection framing treats the rate as a property of the child.

## 6. What dynamic testing adds, and to whom

- Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs, Dixon et al. and Wood, Biggs & Molnar report dynamic assessment adding unique variance over static ability, while Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin report almost no between-student variance in rate for it to be adding.
- Wood, Biggs & Molnar find dynamic assessment significantly better suited to children aged 4–7 than 8–9 and Dixon et al. find its predictive power waning with age, while a selection instrument is most needed for older applicants and untaught content.
- Grigorenko & Sternberg call the pretest-to-posttest change score the field's central unresolved measurement problem, while the meta-analytic literature that followed — Caffrey et al., Dixon et al., Wood et al. — reports validity coefficients built on that same index without resolving it.
- Fuchs et al. find dynamic assessment necessary to forecast the outcome *distal* to instruction but unnecessary for the proximal one, while Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs report its superiority strongest against criterion-referenced and dynamic criteria rather than norm-referenced ones.
- Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs find dynamic assessment's advantage conditional on feedback contingency and criterion choice, while Wood, Biggs & Molnar report a single pooled effect of g = 0.49 across heterogeneous designs.

## 7. Retest, practice, and what an observed climb contains

- Scharfen, Peters & Holling report a standardized mean change of 0.33 from first to second administration with no intervention, and Hausknecht et al. an adjusted d = .26 in selection settings, while any two-occasion "learning rate" attributes that same movement to learning.
- Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad find retest scores less g-loaded, more memory-loaded, and stripped of criterion validity, while the retest-gain literature represented by Scharfen et al. and Hausknecht et al. quantifies the gain without addressing what construct it belongs to.
- Schleicher, Van Iddekinge, Morgeson & Campion find retest improvements larger for White than for Black or Hispanic applicants and larger on written than performance formats, while Scharfen, Peters & Holling report content moderators without a demographic breakdown.
- Soderstrom & Bjork establish that performance change during acquisition is a poor proxy for durable learning, while Kumar, Benjamin, Heathcote & Steyvers show 50–70% of asymptotic performance is reached inside a single session.
- Scharfen, Peters & Holling report retest gains ceasing after the third administration, while Willett's design result recommends adding occasions to raise change reliability.

## 8. The decision-grade bar and multiple measures

- AERA, APA & NCME Standards 3.18 and 12.10 require multiple sources of information for a placement decision, while Standard 12.11 requires every individual growth or difference score used to carry its own validity, reliability and fairness evidence.
- AERA, APA & NCME Standard 1.19 requires incremental validity to be estimated before a predictor is added, while Hunsley & Meyer report that incremental validity is seldom evaluated at all in applied psychology.
- AERA, APA & NCME chapter 3 and Standard 3.7 require differential item functioning and differential prediction to be investigated separately, so clearing one provides no assurance about the other.
- Kane holds that consequences can invalidate a score use even where the underlying score interpretation stands, while Livingston & Lewis supply classification statistics that speak to the use without addressing consequence.
- AERA, APA & NCME Standard 2.16 requires decision-consistency estimates, while its own comment states the standard error of measurement cannot be converted into them without strong distributional assumptions.
- Hunsley & Meyer identify the application of nomothetic incremental-validity findings to an individual case as unsolved, while AERA, APA & NCME Standard 1.19 frames incremental validity as the evidence that licenses adding a predictor.
- Glover & Albers frame a screening flag as consequence-light and judged on fit-to-purpose, while AERA, APA & NCME Standard 13.7 requires stated intended uses and cautions because a logged signal becomes available to future decision rules.

## 9. Rank, band, and shrinkage

- Shen & Louis show a purpose-built estimator recovering good ranks and a good parameter histogram, while Lockwood, Louis & McCaffrey show percentile mean squared error large at commonly encountered variance ratios and worst at the extreme percentiles.
- Shen & Louis report posterior means optimal under squared-error loss, while the same analysis reports the ranks derived from those means shrunk toward the mid-rank — the opposite of what a ranking use needs.
- Willett shows growth-rate reliability rising with between-child heterogeneity, so the same statistic looks better precisely in cohorts more unequal at intake, while Hedge, Powell & Sumner treat low between-person variance as the property that makes a measure useless for individual differences.
- AERA, APA & NCME Standards 5.9 and 5.11 require a current, population-matched reference distribution for band boundaries, while Kanaya, Scullin & Ceci show that the renorming event itself shifted classification by an average of 5.6 IQ points.
- Kane & Staiger show small-unit noise worst at the distribution's extremes, while Lockwood, Louis & McCaffrey show percentile estimation worst at the same extremes — which is where selection cutoffs are normally placed.
- AERA, APA & NCME Standard 12.12 warns that overlapping constructs render difference-score reliability low, while multiple-measure schemes routinely combine correlated instruments.

## 10. Tie-breaker, lottery, and the bottom of the ladder

- Lathrop reports misclassification risk substantial near the cutting score even for instruments with relatively high retest reliability, while the tie-breaker rung is defended as a low-stakes hedge and applies the weak measure to exactly those examinees.
- Jølstad & Gustavsson argue a marginal-only tie-breaker is either infinitesimal or lexically subordinate and weaker than a lottery, while Lathrop and the educational-measurement literature report combination-rule and cut-score effects without addressing whether marginal-only application is defensible.
- Jølstad & Gustavsson hold that a factor judged genuinely valuable belongs among the primary criteria, while multiple-criteria identification systems of the kind McBee, Peters & Waterman simulate hold weak measures back as secondary inputs.
- Jølstad & Gustavsson argue a lottery is impartial at a genuine tie, while AERA, APA & NCME Standard 12.10 requires that additional relevant information about the student's performance be considered.
- McBee, Peters & Waterman show combination-rule choice alone changes who is identified and how psychometrically sound the system is, while a measure described as "one input among several" is treated as evidentially lighter than a decisive one.
- Kane's separation of score interpretation from score use permits a research-only rate interpretation to stand while the gating use is rejected, while AERA, APA & NCME Standard 13.7 records that any logged signal requires an explicit use boundary because it remains available to later rules.
