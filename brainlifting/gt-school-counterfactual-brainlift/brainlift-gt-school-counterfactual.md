## Purpose

### Purpose
This BrainLift synthesizes research on separating GT School's **program effect** from its **selection effect**: causal-identification designs (regression discontinuity, admission lotteries, matched comparisons), what actually predicts achievement growth in high-ability students, the fairness and access consequences of aptitude screening, and the selected-cohort critique of GT School / Alpha. It is a standalone, source-traceable map of what the evidence does and does not support.

### In Scope
- Why a single CogAT-plus-tuition screen can be too restrictive — false negatives, rank instability, gatekeeping, and missed spatial talent — as a *selection* problem.
- Causal-identification designs (RD, lottery ITT, matched comparisons) and outcome-validity constraints for separating program effect from selection.
- What predicts achievement growth in high-ability students (prior ability, ability breadth, conscientiousness) and why broad "motivation" constructs are weaker predictors.
- The equity and access disparities that aptitude screening encodes, and how they compound the selection problem.
- GT School / Alpha and comparable programs as **case-study context** and clearly labeled company statements where relevant.

### Out of Scope
- Designing or implementing an admissions product, selecting vendors, or setting live admissions policy.
- Specifying operational workflows, software architecture, implementation roadmaps, or data systems.
- Treating predictive validity, access change, or selected-cohort growth as proof of program impact without an identified counterfactual design.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

**SPOV 1: A capability-gated admission lottery is a clean and feasible method of collecting causal evidence that can separate GT's program effect from its selection effect.**

- **Elaboration:** The core problem GT faces is that the current selection effect methodology of a high CogAT cutoff and private-school tuition rate results in a confirmation bias that renders it impossible to adequately measure program effect (Insight 1, 3.6). However, the immediate fix of just lowering GT's CogAT cutoff reduces the programs effectiveness as a result (Insight 5). Thus, any solution that intends to separate program effect from selection effect needs to broaden program access without lowering the bar of admission. One way to do this is to identify a separate set of capable but underserved applicants who are gifted in metrics not covered by the CogAT (spatial reasoning, conscientiousness, demonstrated achievement, etc.) (Insight 2). By our existing knowledge of giftedness, these kids are classified as gifted and likely just as capable of succeeding in GT's program as any accepted GT applicant, yet they are denied by the current system. 

Since GT's program doesn't currently serve this group of applicants, it makes them prime candidates for demonstrating the program's effectiveness (Insight 3, 3.7). By separating and tracking the group of capable but underserved applicants who are offered a seat vs. not offered a seat into GT's next cohort, we can analyze their growth over the academic year (via MAP scores or another trusted metric) to determine if GT's program disproportionately benefits capable gifted students over traditional schooling. The selection of students into offered and not offered groups should be done using a capability-gated lottery since research has shown these systems to boost accepted applicant performance and allow us to account for income/financial disparities between applicants gracefully (Insight 6, 3.9). Given GT's small size, this method will likely need to prove its worth over several years worth of cohorts to result in a counterfactual that is precise enough to truly be bulletproof (5.5, 3.9).

**SPOV 2: The same capability-gated admission lottery can help provide the program accessibility needed to begin bridging the "excellence gap."**

- **Elaboration:** The current CogAT cutoff and high tuition combination that resulted in confirmation bias also excludes capable Black, Hispanic, low-income, and ELL students (Insight 4, 6). This means that the same methodology used to solve the confirmation bias as part of SPOV 1 will also help to bridge the excellence gap observed as a side effect.

**SPOV 3: If the lottery-offered students show a positive program effect and demonstrate capabilities closely resembling the students admitted above the CogAT cutoff, then there is enough supporting evidence to expand the GT admitted cohort and capability-based lottery to slowly work towards the 10K MIT ready kids by 8th grade goal.**

- **Elaboration:** For expansion to be a viable next step, the lottery-admitted students need to go beyond outpacing their non-offered peers (SPOV 1) and actually match the growth of their CogAT cutoff admitted peers in GT school (3.8). This will demonstrate that the lottery admissions can be accelerated at the same rate as the current GT admitted students and are not falling behind their peers in terms of acceleration. Additionally, before expansion of the GT program can begin, a set of guardrails need to be met for the cohort lottery expansion: current student scores cannot fall, the GT environment of high-velocity acceleration cannot be diluted or otherwised modified, and the MAP/knowledge gains achieved by the end of the academic year need to be retained through to the next academic year (3.8, 5.7). If these conditions are met, then the expansion of the GT school's admitted cohort and the lottery admission system will "broaden access without sacrificing the bar" (Insight 5). Luckily, GT's use of individualized learning and acceleration tools makes expansion of the program very viable at the moment. However, there are still a few aspects of GT school that would need to expand with the cohort size (Guides, workshops, admins, etc.) (Insight 5, 5.6). Still, expansion allows GT to work towards the 10K MIT ready kids by 8th grade marker through volume without compromising on program performance.

## DOK 3: Insights

**Insight 1 - GT's selection process directly hinders measuring program effect**
- GT school admits kids in the top percentile of CogAT scores (5.3) and are willing to pay their $25K tuition fee (5.3, 6.3). Critics argue that this barrier makes measuring the program effect unreliable because naturally gifted students will likely excel, therefore skewing the data toward high-achieving scores. In turn, the results will have a confirmation bias (5.4, 3.6) — the same limitation the anchor SMPY literature concedes about its own design (2.4). For example, if a student were to spend the same amount on tutoring services, it's possible their MAP score may outperform a GT School student.
**Insight 2 - CogAT's batteries are too narrow to identify the breadth of a gifted student's talents.**
- While the CogAT assesses verbal, nonverbal, and quantitative reasoning well, it lacks in other areas such as spatial reasoning (1.6), which may be key identifiers of giftedness. Thus, for qualities such as intrinsic motivation and conscientiousness that cannot be measured with the CogAT (4.5), GT School's loses a substantial portion of admittable students from the selection effect.
**Insight 3 - The students right beneath the CogAT's minimum bar are the exact people who can help prove GT School's program effect.**
 - Studies support that individualized tutoring helps those falling behind the most (3.7, 4.3, 4.4), but GT itself doesn't have the necessary control group (5.2, 5.4) to substantiate that claim GT says it helps capable but underserved students, but their statistics are supplemented by outside sourcing. They have not tested transfers to their gifted cohort (4.4), and the promise of GT's complete package (5.1) is hindered by screening out capable students through CogAT and tuition (5.3).
 **Insight 4 - GT’s extreme exclusivity is the driving force behind the “excellence gap” seen in so many G&T programs.**
- Studies show that tests like CogAT disproportionately select kids on race and class lines. Children’s test scores are closely and positively correlated to a family’s income starting as early as 2 years old (6.3) and even “culture-fair” tests fail to account for ELL/subgroup kids (6.2). Additionally, black and hispanic students have been selected less frequently by other GT programs despite demonstrating equivalent achievements and talents (6.1). As a result, the extremely restrictive CogAT cutoff and high tuition fee of GT school is likely to be driving the race/class gap come to be known as the “excellence gap" (5.4).
 **Insight 5 - Instead of diluting GT's edge by lowering the CogAT cutoff, GT School should utilize a capability-gated lottery system.**
 - While GT and Alpha School utilize the same timeback model (5.1), GT's uniqueness banks on its selective cohort of gifted students (5.6). Lowering the cutoff as suggested through local-norm/lower-threshold remedies (6.4) risks the marginal-admit pattern Bui found (3.7). A lottery bypasses this issue, broadening access without sacrificing the bar (5.1, 5.3).
**Insight 6 - A capability-based lottery will provide the necessary data to demonstrate GT's program effect separately from its selection effect.**
- By randomizing offers to a program, studies have shown that applicants balance both ability and motivation (3.9). On average, students perform ~0.35 SD/yr better, largest for those behind (3.9, 3.7). Critiques on the small cohorts can be solved through pooling data over the years, fitting the voucher ramp (5.5). This plans around ~0.10-0.20 SD effects (4.4, 3.7), with noninferiority as a separate test (3.8). The school's current "2.6x growth" claim recalculates to ~1.69x math/1.54x reading in comparison to public matched completers with no counterfactual (5.2).
**Insight 7 - SSP's 2026 lottery systems demonstrates the design is possible and why research consent must be prioritized.**
- Currently, SSP's selection process goes through a contextual review, qualified pool, randomized offers, and waitlist comparison (3.10). However, conditions lottery exploit research consent (ex: if students decline, they lose their spot on the program waitlist), which Jenkins (2023) calls coercive (3.10). The new proposed design must keep the two structurally separate, meaning refusing research does not affect the student's admission inputs/outputs.

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

### Overall Summary

Selected-cohort outcomes cannot separate ability, family resources, applicant motivation, and program effect. SMPY establishes long-run predictive validity, not a program counterfactual; RD and lottery evidence shows selective programs can have null, positive, or heterogeneous effects depending on population, curriculum, comparison, and outcome. Card & Giuliano's positive achievement-rank RD alongside an essentially null IQ-threshold RD further shows that predicting high standing is not the same as identifying who benefits. A future capability-gated lottery may be a strong design only under genuine scarcity, independent oversight, preregistration, high-ceiling outcomes, adequate follow-up/power, and a firewall that keeps research choice from changing admission rights.

### Category 1: Why GT School's Ability Screen May Be Too Strict — How One Test Cutoff Misses Able Children

- **Subcategory 1.1: Two tests that agree closely still name different children as "the best"**
  - **Source:** Lohman (2005); Lohman & Gambrell (2012)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The CogAT — the Cognitive Abilities Test — is the reasoning test GT School uses to screen applicants. It has a verbal section, a quantitative (number) section, and a nonverbal section built from pictures and shapes, plus a Composite score that combines all three. The ITBS — the Iowa Tests of Basic Skills — is a different instrument, measuring what a child has actually learned in school rather than raw reasoning.
      - A correlation, written *r*, describes how closely two sets of scores move together, from 0 (no relationship) to 1 (perfect agreement). A "false negative" is an able child the screen wrongly turns away.
      - Lohman, D. F. (2005), "An Aptitude Perspective on Talent: Implications for Identification of Academically Gifted Minority Students," *Journal for the Education of the Gifted*, 28(3/4), 333–360 — using the joint ITBS/CogAT national standardization (~14,000 students/grade, grades 3–6): of students in the **top 3% on ITBS Reading**, the **CogAT Composite identifies only 32%**, the CogAT Verbal battery 35%, and the **CogAT Nonverbal battery only 18%** (even the ITBS Composite catches just 54%).
      - Lohman (2005) states: "correlations generally imply far less agreement between scores than most people think, especially for extreme scores." The two tests do track each other closely overall — CogAT Composite against ITBS Reading r = .79, CogAT Nonverbal against Reading r = .62 — which is precisely what makes their disagreement at the top so striking.
      - Lohman, D. F., & Gambrell, J. L. (2012), "Using Nonverbal Tests to Help Identify Academically Talented Children," *Journal of Psychoeducational Assessment*, 30(1), 25–44 — "large discrepancies between even highly correlated abilities become increasingly common at the extremes." The CogAT Nonverbal battery's standard error of measurement is about **3.7** points, meaning that is the typical distance between the score a child happens to get on one sitting and the score reflecting their real standing. The scale's population spread — one standard deviation, the ordinary distance between an average child and a clearly above-average one — is **16** points. A child's plausible true score is therefore a band rather than a point, and that band straddles any single cutoff line.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A single reasoning test does not identify "the" able students. It identifies the subset that one particular test happens to rank highly, and at the extreme top even tests that agree strongly overall disagree sharply about who belongs there. On these numbers, a CogAT Composite cutoff at the 90th percentile would pass over the majority of children who are genuinely top performers on a school-achievement measure, and the ordinary wobble in any score makes a single pass/fail line partly arbitrary. Why a formal comparison group is needed before any of this can support a claim about GT School's program is set out in 3.6; which children the screen excludes, by race and income, is Category 6.
    - **Link to source:** [https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746059.pdf](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746059.pdf) | [https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2013/04/Lohman-Gambrell-2012.pdf](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2013/04/Lohman-Gambrell-2012.pdf)
- **Subcategory 1.2: A one-time childhood score is a moving target — where a child ranks at eight says little about where they rank at thirteen**
  - **Source:** Lohman & Korb (2006) / Martin (1985); Ramsden et al. (2011); Kelley's regression-to-the-mean (Maassen 2000)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Regression to the mean" is the tendency of an unusually high or low score to land closer to average the next time it is measured, because part of any extreme score reflects a good or bad day rather than lasting ability.
      - Lohman, D. F., & Korb, K. A. (2006), "Gifted Today but Not Tomorrow? Longitudinal Changes in Ability and Achievement During Elementary School," *Journal for the Education of the Gifted*, 29(4), 451–484 — approximately **half** of students in the top 3% in one year do not remain there the next year; only **~35–40%** remain top-3% from grade 3 to grade 8 (about 60–65% fall out over that interval).
      - Ramsden, S., et al. (2011), "Verbal and non-verbal intelligence changes in the teenage brain," *Nature*, 479, 113–116 — individual IQ shifted by **−20 to +23 points (verbal)** and −18 to +21 (full-scale) over ~3.5 years; ~33% of teens moved further than their 90% confidence interval, the band inside which a stable score would be expected to stay; 21% shifted verbal IQ ≥15 points, a full standard deviation. Those changes tracked measurable structural changes in the brain, so they were real development rather than testing noise.
      - The statistical engine behind that fall-out is regression to the mean. Because no test reproduces the same score perfectly, a group selected for extreme scores drifts back toward average on retest by an amount proportional to (1 − reliability), where reliability is how consistently the test repeats itself. The formula is Kelley's; the verified source anchoring it is Maassen, G. H. (2000), *Psychometrika*, 65(2), 187–197.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The rank a child holds on a one-time test in early childhood is a weak guide to the rank they will hold years later — both because ability itself changes through adolescence and because extreme early scores partly reflect luck that washes out on retest. An admission screen applied once, at entry, locks in a snapshot that a large fraction of eventual high achievers would fail at that moment even if they would clear it later.
    - **Link to source:** [https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746292.pdf](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746292.pdf) | [https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10514](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10514) | [https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02294373](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02294373)
- **Subcategory 1.3: The screening step that happens before the test throws away able children all by itself**
  - **Source:** McBee, Peters & Miller (2016); McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014); Peters et al. (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "nomination gate" is a first filter — usually a teacher or parent recommendation — that decides who is even allowed to sit the test. A "combination rule" is how a program merges several scores into one admit-or-reject decision.
      - McBee, M. T., Peters, S. J., & Miller, E. M. (2016), "The Impact of the Nomination Stage on Gifted Program Identification," *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 60(4), 258–278 — a simulation shows that requiring a screening/nomination gate before testing means "a large proportion of gifted students being missed"; under commonly implemented conditions the share of gifted children wrongly screened out **can easily exceed 60%**. A screening stage can only lose students, never recover them.
      - McBee, M. T., Peters, S. J., & Waterman, C. (2014), "Combining Scores in Multiple-Criteria Assessment Systems: The Impact of Combination Rule," *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 58(1), 69–89 — the combination rule drives who qualifies. An "and" rule, which requires a child to clear every bar separately, produces the smallest admitted pool and the most missed students; averaging the scores, so a strength can offset a weakness, is the most accurate.
      - Peters, S. J., et al. (2019), "Effect of Local Norms on Racial and Ethnic Representation in Gifted Education," *AERA Open*, 5(2) — switching from a single national cutoff to local norms, which rank each child against others in their own school rather than against the whole country, changes which students qualify.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Even holding the test itself fixed, the *structure* of a selection process — a hard gate before testing, a rule that a child must clear one particular bar, a single national cutoff — is itself a large source of missed children, independent of any child's true ability. GT School's single-CogAT-cutoff gate is precisely the configuration this literature identifies as maximizing missed students.
    - **Link to source:** [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0016986216656256](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0016986216656256) | [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0016986213513794](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0016986213513794) | [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2332858419848446](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2332858419848446)
- **Subcategory 1.4: Able low-income children fall out of the top tier years before any admissions test is given**
  - **Source:** Wyner, Bridgeland & DiIulio (2007), *Achievement Trap*; Plucker et al. (excellence gap)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "quartile" is one quarter of the ranked population, so the top quartile is the highest-scoring 25% of children. The "excellence gap" is the difference between demographic groups in how many children reach the very top of the achievement range, as opposed to the more familiar gap in average scores.
      - Wyner, J. S., Bridgeland, J. M., & DiIulio, J. J. (2007), *Achievement Trap: How America Is Failing Millions of High-Achieving Students from Lower-Income Families*, Jack Kent Cooke Foundation & Civic Enterprises — "more than 3.4 million K-12 students achieving in the top quartile academically come from families earning less than the median income."
      - *Achievement Trap*: of lower-income students in the top reading quartile as first graders, only 56% remained in it by fifth grade, versus 69% of higher-income students — about 44% fell out, against about 31%.
      - *Achievement Trap*: among students *not* in the top quartile in first grade, only ~7% of lower-income students rose into it by fifth grade in math and reading, versus 16–17% of higher-income students. Lower-income high-potential students rarely rise in, and the report attributes the loss to low-expectation school environments rather than to lack of ability.
      - Excellence-gap data (Plucker et al.): on the fourth-grade math section of the National Assessment of Educational Progress — the test given to a nationally representative sample of U.S. students — roughly 3% of children receiving lunch assistance reached the "Advanced" tier versus ~14% of everyone else, a gap that has persisted across administrations.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Before GT School ever applies its screen, the population of children who have "already demonstrated" high performance has been thinned by years of unequal environments. Able students, disproportionately lower-income, fall out of the measured top tier or never enter it, and the report ties this to opportunity rather than ability. A screen that selects on demonstrated standing therefore inherits that prior loss, systematically excluding exactly the students whose ceiling was never fairly tested.
    - **Link to source:** [https://www.jkcf.org/research/achievement-trap-how-america-is-failing-millions-of-high-achieving-students-from-lower-income-families/](https://www.jkcf.org/research/achievement-trap-how-america-is-failing-millions-of-high-achieving-students-from-lower-income-families/) | [https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/achievement-trap-how-america-failing-millions-high-achieving-students-lower](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/achievement-trap-how-america-failing-millions-high-achieving-students-lower)
- **Subcategory 1.5: The strongest honest case FOR testing — ability tests predict well, and testing everyone beats waiting for referrals**
  - **Source:** Deary et al. (2007); Card & Giuliano (2016); Grissom & Redding (2016); Lohman (2005); Makel et al. (2016); Hobart (in Segan / Reason 2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Deary, I. J., Strand, S., Smith, P., & Fernandes, C. (2007), "Intelligence and educational achievement," *Intelligence*, 35(1), 13–21 — in a study that tested children first and tracked them forward, covering more than 70,000 children, a cognitive test (the CogAT) at age 11 correlated **r ≈ .69 (observed; .81 latent)** with GCSE results at 16, the national exams English students sit at that age. The higher "latent" figure is what the relationship looks like after correcting for the imperfection of both measures. General cognitive ability, written *g*, accounted for about 59% of the differences between students in mathematics. Cognitive ability is among the strongest single early predictors of later achievement.
      - Card & Giuliano (2016, published in *PNAS*, the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*): replacing teacher referral with **universal** cognitive screening — testing every child instead of only those someone put forward — raised gifted identification of disadvantaged students **+174%**, Hispanic students **+118%**, and Black students **+74%**. An objective test, applied to everyone, surfaced far *more* underrepresented talent than human referral did. The same study reappears in 3.3 and Category 6.
      - Grissom, J. A., & Redding, C. (2016), "Discretion and Disproportionality," *AERA Open*, 2(1) — at identical achievement, Black students were assigned to gifted programs about *half* as often as White peers; a test-based rule removes some of that discretionary human bias.
      - Lohman (2005) is a *proponent* of aptitude testing done well — using local norms and multiple measures — not an opponent of testing as such.
      - Makel, M. C., Kell, H. J., Lubinski, D., Putallaz, M., & Benbow, C. P. (2016), "When Lightning Strikes Twice: Profoundly Gifted, Profoundly Accomplished," *Psychological Science*, 27(7), 1004–1018 — giving 12-year-olds a test written for much older students predicted exceptional attainment (doctorates, tenure, patents) by around age 40, which supports identifying ability early.
      - GT School's documented position, stated by Pamela Hobart in Segan, *Reason*, 2026: "the way you actually find very high-ability learners falling through the cracks is you just standardized-test everyone." She argues that moving to "multiple measures" risks omitting high-IQ students who do not display leadership or are not nominated. No documented statement from Joe Liemandt or MacKenzie Price crediting the CogAT specifically with a positive impact was located.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The honest counterweight to 1.1–1.4 is that cognitive-ability testing is among the best-validated single predictors of achievement, and that a standardized test given to *everyone* is more equitable than the subjective referral it replaces — the Card & Giuliano result shows the test *widened* access rather than narrowing it. Notably, the defensible pro-testing positions on record (Lohman; Hobart) do not defend a single fixed cutoff; they defend testing everyone and then looking at several measures. That locates the real disagreement at "one fixed cutoff versus test-everyone-plus-multiple-measures," not at "test versus no test."
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2006.02.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2006.02.001) | [https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113) | [https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858415622175](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858415622175) | [https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616644735](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616644735)
- **Subcategory 1.6: The screen is blind to an entire kind of talent — spatial reasoning**
  - **Source:** Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2009); Kell & Lubinski (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Spatial ability" is the capacity to picture, rotate, and fold objects in your head — the reasoning behind engineering, architecture, surgery, and design. STEM is the usual shorthand for science, technology, engineering, and mathematics.
      - Wai, J., Lubinski, D., & Benbow, C. P. (2009), "Spatial Ability for STEM Domains: Aligning Over 50 Years of Cumulative Psychological Knowledge Solidifies Its Importance," *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 101(4), 817–835 — using Project Talent (**N > 400,000** students, tracked for 11 years): spatial ability reliably predicts achievement and attainment in science and engineering *over and above* math and verbal ability.
      - Talent searches restricted to math and verbal measures **miss roughly 50% of the top 1% in spatial ability**; approximately **70% of the spatial top 1% do not fall in the math or verbal top 1%**. These are analyses by Lubinski and Benbow, corroborated in the top-0.01% sample of the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth described in Category 2.
      - How high a child's ability is predicts *how much* they achieve; the *shape* of their abilities — a tilt toward spatial over verbal, combined with math — predicts *which field* they achieve it in.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Even granting the case for cognitive testing (1.5), a composite weighted toward verbal and number reasoning systematically overlooks an entire, well-evidenced kind of ability: spatial reasoning. Because roughly half of the most spatially able students are invisible to math and verbal talent searches, a CogAT-style cutoff misses a large pool of exactly the profile that most predicts success in science and engineering. This is a miss about the *breadth* of what gets measured, not about motivation, and a screen that added a spatial measure would recover it.
    - **Link to source:** [https://my.vanderbilt.edu/smpy/](https://my.vanderbilt.edu/smpy/) | [https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2014-kell.pdf](https://gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2014-kell.pdf)



### Category 2: The 50-Year Study Anchoring the Case for Early Testing — the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth

- **Subcategory 2.1: How the study picked its students — and why matching them afterwards is not the same as randomizing**
  - **Source:** Lubinski & Benbow (2006); Swiatek & Benbow (1991)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, usually shortened to SMPY, is a 50-year study that identified gifted 12- and 13-year-olds and then followed the same people for decades. A study that tracks the same individuals over many years is called longitudinal.
      - It selected students by "above-level" testing: giving 12- and 13-year-olds the SAT, a college-entrance exam written for 17- and 18-year-olds, so that the questions are hard enough to separate, in the founders' words, "the able from the exceptionally able." SMPY was founded by Julian C. Stanley on Sept. 1, 1971, at Johns Hopkins.
      - Five cohorts, >5,000 participants, identified 1972–1997; the design plans follow-ups at ages 18, 23, 33, 50, and 65. It is now directed by David Lubinski & Camilla Benbow at Vanderbilt. Each cohort was defined by how rare its members' scores were by age 13, ranging from roughly the top 3% down to the top 0.01%; one cohort was made up of graduate students in top-15 science and engineering programs, who were not selected by SAT score at all.
      - "Acceleration" means moving a student through school faster than usual — skipping a grade, or taking a subject years early.
      - Swiatek, M. A., & Benbow, C. P. (1991), "A ten-year longitudinal follow-up of ability-matched accelerated and unaccelerated gifted students," *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 83, 528–538 — compared accelerated students to non-accelerated students **matched on measured ability**. Nobody was assigned by chance, so the comparison is constructed after the fact rather than experimental.
      - Lubinski & Benbow (2006) report that more ability produces more achievement even *inside* the top 1%: splitting participants into the top versus bottom quarter on the SAT math section yields clearly different outcomes. The reported gradient: about 30% earned a doctorate among those scoring ≥500 on SAT math at 13, versus about 50% among those scoring ≥700.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** SMPY's power comes from a sharp, quantified selection rule — one hard test at 12 or 13 — applied to children who volunteered for talent searches. It tracks a *selected* pool forward in time, and although the selection rule is completely transparent, **participants first chose to enter a talent search**, so the sample is not a random draw of high-ability youth. Nor did SMPY ever assign anyone by chance to "gifted" status or to acceleration; its comparisons rest on *matching* students of equal measured ability. Matching holds measured ability constant but leaves motivation and family circumstances uncontrolled — the same limitation any matched GT School comparison would face, and the reason a lottery is the stronger design.
    - **Link to source:** [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00019.x](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00019.x) | [https://my.vanderbilt.edu/smpy/](https://my.vanderbilt.edu/smpy/)
- **Subcategory 2.3: What the study found — a test taken at 13 still forecasts achievement at 38 and beyond**
  - **Source:** Kell, Lubinski & Benbow (2013); Bernstein, Lubinski & Benbow (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "base rate" is how common something is in the general population, which is what any impressive-sounding percentage has to be compared against.
      - Kell, H. J., Lubinski, D., & Benbow, C. P. (2013), "Who Rises to the Top? Early Indicators," *Psychological Science*, 24(5), 648–659 — N=320 people identified before age 13 as being in the top 0.01%, assessed by age 38: 44% held doctorates against a U.S. base rate of about 2%, about 15% held at least one patent against a base rate of about 1%, and more than 11% held university tenure.
      - Bernstein, B. O., Lubinski, D., & Benbow, C. P. (2019), "Psychological Constellations Assessed at Age 13 Predict Distinct Forms of Eminence 35 Years Later," *Psychological Science*, 30(3), 444–454 — the *pattern* of a 13-year-old's abilities and interests, not just their overall level, predicted which kind of distinction they reached by around age 50.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** SMPY is the strongest evidence that one ability measure taken young really does forecast achievement decades later, and that differences keep mattering even among the already-extraordinary. This is the empirical case *against* the argument this BrainLift makes — it suggests that admitting on ability really does pick out who succeeds — and any comparison-group design must meet it head-on rather than wish it away.
    - **Link to source:** [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23531483/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23531483/) | [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6419263/](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6419263/)
- **Subcategory 2.4: The authors state in print that their design cannot separate the program from the students it selected**
  - **Source:** Lubinski & Benbow (2006), authors' own caveats
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An "observational" study watches what people do and records the results; it does not assign anyone to a treatment, so it cannot rule out that the people who chose the treatment were different to begin with.
      - Discussing a finding about Advanced Placement participation, the authors write that the effect could operate "through self-selection or something intrinsic to the AP program itself" — an explicit statement that they cannot tell the program apart from the kind of student who signs up for it.
      - Identification rests on a single SAT sitting at 12 or 13, with no assignment by chance either to gifted status or to acceleration.
      - The cohorts lean toward particular places (Maryland, the mid-Atlantic, the Midwest) and a particular era (before the SAT was rescaled in the 1990s), which limits how far the results carry to other populations.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The authors of the anchor study concede its central limitation in print: a study that selects on ability and then lets participants choose their own programs cannot cleanly credit outcomes to the program. That admission is the opening a comparison-group argument can work through — a credible design should do the one thing SMPY could not. The studies that *do* separate program growth from selection — Card & Giuliano; Booij — are in 3.7, and they make exactly the move SMPY's structure rules out.
    - **Link to source:** [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00019.x](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00019.x)



### Category 3: Study Designs That Can Separate a Program's Effect from the Students It Admits — Cutoffs, Lotteries, and Matching

- **Subcategory 3.1: Comparing children just above and just below an admission cutoff — selective schools mostly show no test-score gain**
  - **Source:** Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist & Pathak (2014); Dobbie & Fryer (2014); Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A **regression discontinuity** study compares the children who scored just above an admission cutoff with the children who scored just barely below it. Those two groups are alike in essentially everything — ability, family, ambition — except that one group got in and the other did not, so any later difference between them can be credited to attending. A **"fuzzy"** version handles the realistic case where some children above the line do not enroll and some below the line get in anyway; it measures the effect of *being offered the chance* rather than of attending.
      - Effects are reported in **standard deviations (SD)**, the typical spread of scores across the population. Roughly, 0.1 SD is a small nudge, 0.3 SD is a substantial gain, and 1.0 SD is enormous. "pp" means percentage points.
      - Abdulkadiroğlu, A., Angrist, J., & Pathak, P. (2014), "The Elite Illusion: Achievement Effects at Boston and New York Exam Schools," *Econometrica*, 82(1), 137–196 — a fuzzy above-and-below-the-cutoff comparison at selective exam-school admission lines. Even though crossing the line placed a student among dramatically stronger classmates, that jump had "little causal effect on test scores or college quality."
      - Dobbie, W., & Fryer, R. (2014), "The Impact of Attending a School with High-Achieving Peers: Evidence from the New York City Exam Schools," *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 6(3), 58–75 — applicants who just qualified got classmates scoring 0.17–0.36 SD higher and 6.4–9.5 pp less likely to be Black or Hispanic, yet saw little impact on achievement or college outcomes.
      - Bui, S., Craig, S., & Imberman, S. (2014), "Is Gifted Education a Bright Idea? Assessing the Impact of Gifted and Talented Programs on Students," *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 6(3), 30–62 — the published article combines two designs: an above-and-below comparison at a gifted-eligibility boundary, plus a separate randomized offer of a magnet-school place. Eligibility could be earned by two different routes combining achievement scores, the Naglieri Nonverbal Ability Test, grades, teacher recommendations, and points for circumstances, so the authors had to reconstruct each student's distance to a three-dimensional qualifying surface rather than rank everyone on one total.
      - Bui's comparison was not merely "better classmates, same lessons." Crossing the eligibility boundary raised classmates' achievement by about 0.27–0.32 SD, added approximately 1.25 advanced Vanguard classes, and put students into what the district described as a deeper, project-based curriculum. After about 1.5 years the preferred estimates were −0.037 SD math, +0.049 reading, −0.015 language, +0.003 social studies, and −0.025 science, with margins of error around 0.07–0.08. The resulting ranges ruled out positive effects larger than roughly 0.11–0.18 SD by subject, while still leaving room for small benefits or small harms.
      - Slicing the data by how much classmates improved, how many advanced classes students took, how concentrated the gifted classes were, what type of school students attended, and how they had scored before produced no consistent positive pattern. These slices are suggestive rather than conclusive, because which school a child attends can itself change with eligibility.
      - Bui's magnet lottery asked a different question: 542 already-eligible applicants competed for two premier magnet schools; 394 were offered a place and 148 were not, and those who lost generally kept access to gifted services at their neighborhood school. The lottery applicants scored roughly 1 SD above the average gifted student and were less economically disadvantaged, which shows that testing everyone does not stop families from sorting themselves into an optional elite program. The preferred estimates of attending were near zero in four subjects and +0.281 SD in science, but once the study accounted for the fact that some students dropped out of the data at different rates, the science range included zero.
      - Crossing the ordinary gifted-eligibility boundary raised the chance a student stayed in the district by about 4.9 percentage points from a 76% baseline. That is a genuine causal effect on something other than test scores, not evidence of learning growth.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The strongest causal studies refuse to equate selectivity with value added, and Bui sharpens the lesson: even a real move into stronger classmates, more advanced classes, and a reportedly richer curriculum produced no broad short-run gain on standardized tests for borderline-eligible students. The magnet lottery separately shows that testing every child can coexist with heavy self-selection into an optional program, and that comparing an elite program against ordinary gifted services measures *how much more intensive* it is, not gifted education versus nothing. These are local, short-run results; they do not establish that gifted services never work. What they establish is that any causal claim must name its comparison group, the full package being delivered, who was still in the data at follow-up, and which outcome was measured.
    - **Link to source:** [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.3982/ECTA10266](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.3982/ECTA10266) | [https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.6.3.58](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.6.3.58) | [https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.6.3.30](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.6.3.30)
- **Subcategory 3.2: A cutoff comparison only answers a question about the borderline applicant**
  - **Source:** Imbens & Lemieux (2008); Lee & Lemieux (2010)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Imbens, G., & Lemieux, T. (2008), "Regression discontinuity designs: A guide to practice," *Journal of Econometrics*, 142(2), 615–635.
      - Lee, D., & Lemieux, T. (2010), "Regression Discontinuity Designs in Economics," *Journal of Economic Literature*, 48(2), 281–355.
      - Comparing children on either side of a cutoff estimates what economists call a Local Average Treatment Effect: the effect *for the students sitting at the cutoff*. It says nothing about the students who cleared the bar comfortably.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A cutoff comparison around GT School's CogAT line would answer "does GT School help the student who barely got in?" — not "does it help its typical top student?" That distinction is decisive: finding no effect at the borderline, as the exam-school studies do, does not disprove an effect for students well above the line, and finding an effect at the borderline does not extend upward either. Whether this is even the right main design here depends on which group of students matters most.
    - **Link to source:** [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2007.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2007.05.001) | [https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.48.2.281](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.48.2.281)
- **Subcategory 3.3: Testing every child, instead of waiting for referrals, found far more gifted students without lowering the bar**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Universal screening" means every child in a grade takes the test automatically, rather than only the children a teacher or parent puts forward.
      - Card, D., & Giuliano, L. (2016), "Universal screening increases the representation of low-income and minority students in gifted education," *PNAS*, 113(48), 13678–13683.
      - A large Florida district switched from referral-based to universal second-grade screening using the Naglieri Nonverbal Ability Test. The chance of being identified as gifted rose ~45% overall, with increases of ~+118% for Hispanic students, +74% for Black students, and +174% for disadvantaged students under a lower IQ ≥ 116 threshold — all with **no change in eligibility standards**.
      - Before the program, 13 schools had zero gifted third-graders; afterward, all 140 had at least one.
      - Among disadvantaged students newly identified because of universal screening, **20% had IQ ≥ 130**, so the newly surfaced group was not made up only of borderline scorers. Gifted enrollment also spread out across the district: the share of schools containing half of all gifted students rose from **18% before screening to 57% after**.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** When the *filter* changed but the *bar* did not, the system found large numbers of qualified gifted students it had previously missed — disproportionately poor and minority. This is direct evidence that conventional gifted selection is systematically too narrow, and it supports the premise that GT School's ability screen may be excluding students who meet its own standard and would succeed if let in.
    - **Link to source:** [https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113)
- **Subcategory 3.4: Matching students on paper can only balance what you actually measured**
  - **Source:** Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983); Steenbergen-Hu, Makel & Olszewski-Kubilius (2016); Cheung & Slavin (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Matching" builds a comparison group by pairing each participating student with a non-participating student who looks the same on the characteristics you recorded. A "propensity score" condenses all those characteristics into a single number — each child's estimated likelihood of participating — so children can be paired on one figure instead of dozens.
      - Rosenbaum, P., & Rubin, D. (1983), "The Central Role of the Propensity Score in Observational Studies for Causal Effects," *Biometrika*, 70(1), 41–55 — matching on the propensity score removes bias from the characteristics you *observed*. It works only if nothing you failed to measure also drives both participation and the outcome, an assumption that cannot be checked from the data.
      - Steenbergen-Hu, S., Makel, M., & Olszewski-Kubilius, P. (2016), "What One Hundred Years of Research Says About the Effects of Ability Grouping and Acceleration...," *Review of Educational Research*, 86(4), 849–899 — accelerated students beat same-age classmates by about 0.70 SD, but beat the older students they had skipped up to join by only about 0.09 SD, a difference small enough to be indistinguishable from zero.
      - Across **645 education evaluations**, Cheung & Slavin (2016) found average effects of **+0.23 SD in studies that built a comparison group after the fact versus +0.16 SD in studies that assigned students by chance**. That gap does not prove every after-the-fact result is inflated, but it puts a number on the risk built into the design.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Matching can build a comparison group of similar measured ability, but it balances only what was *measured*. The influences we most worry about here — motivation, parenting, family investment — go unmeasured and therefore uncontrolled, which is exactly the "smart kids were already smart" residual. The acceleration review also shows that changing the comparison group, from same-age classmates to the older students the child joined, swings the answer from large to nothing, which underscores how much the choice of comparison drives the result.
    - **Link to source:** [https://academic.oup.com/biomet/article-abstract/70/1/41/240879](https://academic.oup.com/biomet/article-abstract/70/1/41/240879) | [https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316675417](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316675417) | [https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X16656615](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X16656615)
- **Subcategory 3.6: Why a comparison group is required at all — you can never see what the same child would have done elsewhere**
  - **Source:** Holland (1986); Rubin (1974); Heckman (1979); Campbell & Stanley (1963)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "counterfactual" is what would have happened to the same child under the other option — the GT School student's alternative life at an ordinary school. It is the thing an evaluation has to estimate, because it can never be observed.
      - Holland, P. W. (1986), "Statistics and Causal Inference," *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 81(396), 945–960 — states what he calls the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference: for any one person you can see the outcome with the treatment *or* without it, never both. The paper also coined the terms "Rubin causal model" and "no causation without manipulation."
      - Rubin, D. B. (1974), "Estimating Causal Effects of Treatments in Randomized and Nonrandomized Studies," *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 66(5), 688–701 — defines a causal effect as the difference between the two outcomes a person could have had.
      - Heckman, J. J. (1979), "Sample Selection Bias as a Specification Error," *Econometrica*, 47(1), 153–161 — when people end up in a group for non-random reasons, whatever caused them to be there is a missing variable in the analysis, and leaving it out biases the result.
      - Campbell, D. T., & Stanley, J. C. (1963), *Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Research* — names drift toward the average and non-random selection as standing threats to whether a study's conclusion holds internally, and singles out the design that simply tests one group before and after as especially vulnerable.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the formal reason "smart kids were already smart" is a design requirement rather than a rhetorical jab. Nobody ever observes what a GT School student *would have* achieved without GT School, so any result built from that one group blends the program together with whatever made those students selectable in the first place. Every design in this category — cutoff comparisons, lotteries, matching — is an attempt to construct that missing alternative credibly. Without one, whatever went unmeasured pushes the estimate in a direction nobody can determine.
    - **Link to source:** [https://www.jstor.org/stable/2289064](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2289064) | [https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ118470](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ118470) | [https://www.jstor.org/stable/1912352](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1912352)
- **Subcategory 3.7: When a gifted program really does raise achievement — and when it doesn't**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2016); Booij, Haan & Plug (2016, 2017); reconciled against the null results in 3.1
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "complier" is a student who actually takes up the opportunity when offered it and would not have taken it otherwise — the group these designs can genuinely speak about.
      - **Positive results:**
      - Card, D., & Giuliano, L. (2016), "Can Tracking Raise the Test Scores of High-Ability Minority Students?", *American Economic Review*, 106(10), 2783–2816 (district records on 70,058 third-graders across 140 schools; main above-and-below-cutoff sample n=4,144) — students entered a separate high-achiever classroom based on their *prior achievement* rank. Full sample **≈+0.27 SD combined (+0.29 reading / +0.34 math)**; Black and Hispanic students who took up the offer gained **≈+0.4–0.5 SD**, and more in additional analyses; White and advantaged students gained about **0**. Math gains persisted into fifth grade, science also improved, and there was no harm to the students left behind.
      - In the same paper, the district's separate IQ-based cutoff produced **essentially no effect**. The contrast matters but is specific to this district: the boundary drawn on past achievement marked out a group that benefited, while the formal IQ boundary did not. How high a child's measured ability is and how much a program helps them are therefore two different empirical questions, not two names for the same thing.
      - Booij, A. S., Haan, F., & Plug, E. (2016), "Enriching Students Pays Off…" (IZA discussion paper 9757) — an above-and-below-cutoff comparison at a gifted-program entrance line (N=3,127, one selective Dutch school): grade point average rose **+0.38 SD math / +0.30 language / +0.44 other**, and the effect carried through to which subject students chose at university. A companion paper (2017, IZA discussion paper 10836; ~2,400 students) combined the cutoff comparison with a before-and-after comparison against an untreated group, finding roughly **+0.2 SD** near the cutoff, with gains *growing* for students further above it.
      - Both papers credit the gains to a genuinely **richer curriculum and higher expectations** for underserved high achievers, rather than to better classmates alone.
      - **Evidence limitations:**
      - Card & Giuliano measures an effect **at the borderline of an *achievement* cutoff**, not at an IQ or CogAT line like GT School's; the treatment is a **separate tracked classroom**, not an individualized software-and-mastery model; the effect is **about zero for White and advantaged students**, so it does not extend to all high-ability children; and the district is anonymized, though widely identified as Broward County, FL.
      - Booij (2016/2017) are **IZA working papers** rather than confirmed peer-reviewed journal articles at that stage, so the final publication should be checked before either is relied on heavily. The 2016 result comes from a **single already-selective school**, which limits how far it travels. Cutoff comparisons also depend on how wide a window around the cutoff the analyst chooses and on how sharply crossing the line actually changed enrollment, and the 2017 paper's per-subject margins of error were not independently verified.
      - Both are cutoff-based comparisons rather than experiments with random assignment; both measure grades and test scores, **not** "elite readiness"; and **neither tests an intensive artificial-intelligence tutoring or two-hour model**. They establish that a program effect is *possible* for high-ability students, not that GT School's particular model produces one.
      - **Counter-evidence:**
      - The cutoff studies in 3.1 — Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014, gifted magnet ≈ 0), Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist & Pathak (2014, "Elite Illusion" ≈ 0), Dobbie & Fryer (2014, NYC exam schools ≈ 0) — find that **selective admission on its own raises nothing**, despite placing students among far stronger classmates.
      - *Issues with the counter-evidence:* each measures only the effect **on the student at the admission borderline** and says nothing about students well above the line. The exam-school studies and Bui's magnet lottery largely test how much more intensive a program is rather than whether gifted education works, but Bui's ordinary-services comparison also changed advanced-course exposure and reportedly deepened the curriculum, so its null result cannot be waved away as a "same curriculum" test. These studies show that selection, stronger classmates, and modest curriculum change do not guarantee broad short-run achievement gains; they do not show that every enriched program fails.
      - *An upper bound built on weak evidence:* the acceleration figure of about 0.70 SD (Steenbergen-Hu et al. 2016, in 3.4) comes only from matched, after-the-fact comparisons. It is inflated by the same selection problem, and structurally just as vulnerable to "these children were already gifted" as SMPY is. Treat it as a ceiling, not as an estimate of what a program does.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Card and Giuliano and Booij do the one thing SMPY cannot: credit achievement growth to a gifted program after accounting for who was selected into it. But Bui shows that stronger classmates, more advanced courses, and modest curriculum enrichment can still produce a tightly bounded short-run nothing at the eligibility borderline. Program effects are therefore possible but not universal, and cannot be assumed from the label "gifted," from who the classmates are, or from curriculum enrichment alone. The population, the instructional targeting, the comparison condition, how much of the program students actually received, and the outcome measured all matter. Neither the positive 0.3–0.5 SD findings nor Bui's null transfers automatically to another program.
    - **Link to source:** [https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20150484](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20150484) | [https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/9757](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/9757) | [https://ideas.repec.org/p/iza/izadps/dp10836.html](https://ideas.repec.org/p/iza/izadps/dp10836.html)
- **Subcategory 3.8: The checklist a credible evaluation must clear — and why even a perfect design proves something only about the test you measured**
  - **Source:** Angrist, Imbens & Rubin (1996); McCrary (2008); Oster (2019); VanderWeele & Ding (2017); Chetty, Friedman & Rockoff (2014) w/ Rothstein (2017); Betebenner (2009); Assouline & Lupkowski-Shoplik (2012); Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014); Kulik & Fletcher (2016); Bailey et al. (2017); Bastani et al. (2025); WWC Handbook v5.0 (2022); Nosek et al. (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Intent-to-treat" means analyzing every student according to the group they were assigned to, whether or not they actually took up the offer. It is the honest headline number, because dropping the students who declined reintroduces exactly the self-selection the design was built to remove.
      - **How much different designs can be trusted, strongest first:** assigning students by chance balances both the traits you measured and the ones you did not, at least on average across the randomized group (Angrist, Imbens & Rubin 1996, in the *Journal of the American Statistical Association*). A cutoff comparison can identify a credible local effect when the cutoff is protected and nobody can nudge themselves across it (McCrary 2008). Before-and-after comparisons against a control group require that the two groups were on the same trajectory beforehand. Matching on recorded characteristics balances only what was recorded, so motivation remains tangled up in the result.
      - **When assigning by chance is impossible, report how fragile the result is:** state how strong an unmeasured influence such as "motivation" would have to be to overturn the finding. Three standard ways of doing this are Rosenbaum's Γ bound, Oster's (2019) δ measure, where a value of 1 or above means the result is robust, and the E-value of VanderWeele & Ding (2017).
      - **Choosing the outcome measure:** account for where each student started. One approach compares actual to expected scores given prior achievement (Chetty, Friedman & Rockoff 2014, disputed by Rothstein 2017); another reports Student Growth Percentiles (Betebenner 2009), which describe growth but do not establish cause. Growth should be measured against the student's **own multi-year record, not the national median** (MDRC). But one school on its own yields a blurry measurement: the smallest difference such a study could reliably detect is roughly **0.57–0.80 SD**, far larger than the effects worth caring about.
      - **Checking the test has room at the top, which matters specifically for gifted students:** a test written for a child's own grade can run out of hard questions, hiding real growth. Precision must therefore be checked at the top of the observed score range (Assouline & Lupkowski-Shoplik 2012; the same reasoning behind SMPY). Bui et al. did this properly, examining the actual score distributions and documenting real headroom: cutoff-sample averages sat at 58%–77% of the maximum score, and even the stronger lottery sample stayed below 90% by subject, rather than assuming the Stanford test was good enough. **A high-ceiling adaptive test such as NWEA MAP** needs the same checks: how much scores still vary at the top, how often students hit the maximum, how precise the test is for a given student at that level, and whether it was administered the same way for everyone in the evaluated group.
      - **A precise outcome can still be the wrong outcome:** Kulik & Fletcher's review of 50 evaluations of computer tutoring systems reported a median effect of 0.66 SD, but average effects were about 0.73 on tests written by the program's own developers versus 0.13 on independent standardized tests. Bastani et al.'s randomized high-school math study found that unrestricted access to GPT raised grades on practice problems by 48% while lowering later grades **without** the tool by 17%; a hint-based tutor reduced that unaided harm. Bailey et al. document that early gains often fade, and identify three ways an effect can last: building a foundational skill the child would not otherwise develop, opening a timely opportunity, and being sustained by later environments. So proving a program caused MAP growth proves it caused MAP growth. Whether the knowledge is retained later, transfers to unfamiliar tasks, or survives when the software is taken away are separate outcomes and separate claims.
      - **The standards the design must meet:** the What Works Clearinghouse Handbook v5.0, the U.S. Department of Education's rulebook for evaluating education research, says only a randomized trial that keeps most of its participants "meets standards without reservations." Studies without random assignment must show the groups started out equivalent — a starting gap of ≤0.05 SD needs no adjustment, 0.05–0.25 SD must be statistically adjusted, and anything >0.25 SD fails outright. The analysis must follow intent-to-treat, the main outcome and analysis plan must be **registered publicly in advance** (Nosek et al. 2018), and the study should run checks on outcomes the program could not plausibly have affected, to expose any leftover selection.
      - **"No significant difference" is not the same as "no worse":** any claim that one group or admission route performs no worse than another must be planned in advance as an explicit equivalence test, measured against a stated margin for the smallest difference that would matter (Lakens, Scheel & Isager 2018), and sized for that test *separately* from the main comparison. Simply failing to find a statistically significant difference does **not** demonstrate the two are equivalent; it may only mean the study was too small to tell.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Isolating a program effect requires two things at once: a design that can attribute cause, and an outcome measure worth attributing it to. That means (1) random assignment or a valid local cutoff comparison; (2) a common outcome with room to grow at the top; (3) growth measured against both the student's own baseline and a comparison group; (4) intent-to-treat analysis, advance registration, control of who drops out of the data, and claims kept within what was tested; and (5) an explicit statement of how fragile the result is when random assignment was not possible. Even a flawless comparison answers only for the outcome measured. A positive intent-to-treat result on MAP would establish a MAP effect for the students in the study; broader claims about durable knowledge, transfer, unaided performance, or educational quality need their own evidence.

- **Subcategory 3.9: Admission lotteries are the cleanest comparison available — but one small school does not generate enough of them**
  - **Source:** Cullen, Jacob & Levitt (2006); Angrist et al. (KIPP, 2012); Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist, Narita & Pathak (2017); Weiland et al. (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An admission lottery draws names at random among applicants who all met the same bar. Because the winners and losers are alike on average in every respect, including ambition and family drive, any later difference between them is attributable to the school. "Statistical power" is a study's ability to detect a real effect if one exists; it rises with the number of students and with the size of the effect being looked for.
      - **Feasible and internally sound:** Cullen, Jacob & Levitt (2006), *Econometrica* — 194 randomized lotteries across 19 oversubscribed Chicago high schools, analyzed using records that already existed.
      - **The KIPP study's design and sample:** Angrist, Dynarski, Kane, Pathak & Walters (2012), "Who Benefits from KIPP?", pooled four lotteries at KIPP Academy Lynn, part of a charter network serving mostly low-income urban students, from 2005 to 2008. The published article reports 629 raw applicants and a 446-student matched randomized analysis sample; its preferred models use 833 stacked student-test observations, which are repeated measurements rather than 833 separate students. About 67.9% were offered a place and 52.5% attended.
      - **What KIPP actually measured:** winning the lottery increased time spent at KIPP by the test date by about 1.22 years. The headline intent-to-treat effect — comparing everyone offered a place against everyone not offered one — was about **+0.430 SD in math and +0.164 in English language arts**. A second calculation, estimating the effect per year actually attended, gave **+0.352 SD math and +0.133 English language arts**. That second figure rests on extra assumptions, describes only the students whose attendance the lottery actually changed, and is neither the primary result nor an average for the KIPP network as a whole.
      - **Who benefited, and how far it travels:** effects were larger for applicants who started out further behind, for students still learning English, and for students receiving special-education services. The relationship with starting score was −0.111 in math and −0.167 in English language arts, meaning the lower a student started, the more they gained, and the English gains concentrated in the lowest-scoring quarter of applicants. KIPP studied a voluntary, low-income urban applicant pool receiving a complete "No Excuses" school package, not a gifted-school population and not software on its own. It supports the design, and the hypothesis that benefits vary by starting point, but it does not supply an effect size another program can borrow.
      - **Following students up:** because the outcome came from statewide testing that happens anyway, the study still had scores for about 85% of the students who were not offered places. The small difference in follow-up between groups could not explain away the large math effect. Studies that have to collect comparison-group outcomes themselves face a much harder problem keeping people in the data.
      - **Other lottery results:** Denver charter estimates were about 0.35–0.42 SD in math and 0.18–0.22 in writing. These are specific to their setting and should not be plugged into another program's planning as if they were generic.
      - **The size problem, and the fix:** a lottery at a single small school usually cannot detect a real effect. Most KIPP schools did not have enough applicants, and their null results reflected being too small rather than the program doing nothing. The remedy is to pool many lotteries and years together using a method that accounts for each applicant's differing odds — the deferred-acceptance approach — which grew Denver's usable sample about fivefold, from 462 students to roughly 2,300. Accumulating one small lottery across several years is a legitimate route to an adequately sized study.
      - A federal charter-school lottery evaluation (Gleason et al., 2010) needed **2,330 applicants across 36 oversubscribed middle schools** to produce publishable achievement estimates, and only a minority of schools had enough excess demand to qualify for the design at all. That is evidence that pooled lotteries are workable, and a warning that having genuinely more applicants than seats is a prerequisite no program can assume.
      - **Planning must use independent numbers:** a study is easier to run the larger the effect it is looking for, but a company's advertised growth multiplier is not an empirical effect size. Planning should stick to the independently grounded **~0.10–0.20 SD** range for broad outcomes and report the resulting imprecision honestly, rather than raising the assumed effect to make a small enrollment look sufficient.
      - **The ceiling on what a lottery can tell you:** even a valid lottery describes only the *applicants* whose behavior the offer changed, and those families are systematically more advantaged than the general population. Weiland et al. (2024) found that the neighborhood income of the families whose enrollment actually shifted far exceeded the applicant-pool average. Results speak to the applicant pool, not to the families who never applied.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A lottery among equally eligible applicants can separate the effect of a school package from selection on traits both measured and unmeasured. KIPP shows why the offer effect and the per-year-attended effect must be reported as different numbers, why an effect for a whole school package cannot pin down which ingredient did the work, and why an effect measured in one population cannot become another program's assumed effect size. Pooling comparable years can raise precision, but only if the weighting and the quantity being estimated are fixed in advance. Students dropping out of the data, students crossing between groups, outcomes that are not comparable, precision at the top of the score range, and the program itself changing between years all remain explicit limitations.

- **Subcategory 3.10: A real program already runs this lottery design — and shows how it can go wrong on consent**
  - **Source:** SSP International official evaluation/application materials (2026); Abt Global study description via SSP; Jenkins (2023); *The Opportunity Cost of Compulsory Research Participation* (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - SSP is the Summer Science Program, a selective residential science program for high-school students. Its official 2026 process runs: a free application; a traditional review of the whole application in context, identifying students "who have the most to gain from and contribute to" the program; parental permission plus the student's own agreement; then a lottery assigning consenting qualified applicants either to an invitation or to a waitlist. The waitlist serves as the comparison group, and some waitlisted students may later receive offers.
      - SSP reported **720 participants in 2026 across 13 campuses and 20 sections**. Its FY2025 annual report separately reports **3,739 applications, a 16% admission rate, and 588 enrolled students**. How many applicants entered the 2026 lottery pool has not been published; the often-repeated figures of ~10,000 applicants and ~1,000 qualified remain unverified and should not be treated as evidence.
      - Abt Global conducts the evaluation under Abt's own ethics board, the body that reviews research involving human participants. Offered and waitlisted students take the same 60-minute surveys before and after, covering scientific thinking and social-emotional skills; students are paid for their survey time and may withdraw once the study has started without losing a program seat.
      - Before the lottery is drawn, however, taking part in the research is a condition of admission. SSP states that applicants who decline the study are excluded from both the program and the waitlist, while simultaneously describing participation as "completely your choice." No public advance registration, protocol, calculation of the study's size, lottery-pool size, or comparison-group size was located.
      - Jenkins (2023), writing earlier and without naming SSP, argues that making lottery entry the gateway to and incentive for research participation undermines genuinely free consent, because applicants do not know their odds and the arrangement exploits people's difficulty reasoning about small probabilities. A 2020 analysis of compulsory research participant pools likewise treats a costly refusal option as coercive in practice, whatever it is called.
      - SSP's ability to expand and to fund an outside evaluation followed a roughly $200 million bequest in 2023 and genuine scarcity of seats. That documents what is feasible under SSP's conditions; it does not establish feasibility for a differently resourced program.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** SSP is a live precedent for the sequence "review applications in context, form a qualified pool, randomly assign the scarce offers, and track the comparison group." It is simultaneously a warning against copying the implementation. SSP makes research consent a condition of entering the admissions lottery, does not publish the information needed to judge whether the study is large enough or was planned in advance, and relies on a waitlist made up only of families who agreed to be studied. An ethically sound version keeps eligibility and ordinary admission rights entirely independent of whether a family agrees to the research, and treats SSP's funding and its excess demand as circumstances that do not transfer.

### Category 4: The Motivation Hypothesis — Can Admission Select on Drive Instead of Prior Achievement?

- **Subcategory 4.1: The case for admitting on grit — perseverance predicts success beyond test scores**
  - **Source:** Duckworth et al. (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Grit," as Duckworth defines it, is sustained perseverance and passion toward long-term goals, measured by a self-report questionnaire.
      - Duckworth, A., Peterson, C., Matthews, M., & Kelly, D. (2007), "Grit: Perseverance and Passion for Long-Term Goals," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 92(6), 1087–1101.
      - Grit accounted for about 4% of the differences between people in success outcomes. It predicted who survived West Point's "Beast Barracks" summer training — a one standard deviation increase in grit raised the odds of staying by roughly 1.62 times — and predicted Spelling Bee performance, in both cases beyond what IQ or SAT scores explained.
      - Grit was slightly *negatively* correlated with SAT scores (r ≈ −.20): higher-scoring students were, marginally, less gritty.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Duckworth's case is that a trait having nothing to do with reasoning ability predicts high-stakes achievement on its own — and that the very highest testers are not the grittiest people in the room. If that holds, motivation is a legitimate admissions signal that is genuinely independent of ability, which is the foundation this BrainLift's argument needs.
    - **Link to source:** [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17547490/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17547490/)
- **Subcategory 4.2: The case against — grit predicts weakly and mostly renames an existing personality trait**
  - **Source:** Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "meta-analysis" pools the results of many separate studies into one overall estimate, so it carries far more weight than any single study. "Conscientiousness" is one of the five standard personality traits: being organized, dependable, and self-disciplined.
      - Credé, M., Tynan, M., & Harms, P. (2017), "Much Ado About Grit: A Meta-Analytic Synthesis of the Grit Literature," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 113(3), 492–511, covering 584 effect sizes across 88 samples and roughly 66,807 individuals.
      - The relationship between grit and academic performance was weak, about .18. The relationship between grit and conscientiousness was about .84 — so close that grit is largely conscientiousness under a new name.
      - Only the *perseverance-of-effort* half of the grit scale predicted performance, at about .26; the *consistency-of-interest* half, at about .10, added little. Programs designed to increase grit likely have only weak effects.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The largest synthesis finds that grit predicts weakly, is barely distinguishable from a personality trait psychology already had, and carries whatever signal it has almost entirely in its effort component. For an admissions rule built on motivation, this is the load-bearing warning: a self-reported grit score would be too noisy and too close to conscientiousness to defend. Effort a student has actually demonstrated may be the only defensible version.
    - **Link to source:** [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27845531/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27845531/)
- **Subcategory 4.3: Growth mindset helps the students who are behind, and almost nobody else**
  - **Source:** Dweck / Blackwell et al. (2007); Sisk et al. (2018); Yeager et al. (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A "growth mindset" is the belief that ability can be developed through effort rather than being fixed at birth. Effects here are reported as *d* or *r*, both standardized measures of how large a difference is; *d* of about 0.1 is small and 0.3 is moderate for a short classroom intervention.
      - Blackwell, L., Trzesniewski, K., & Dweck, C. (2007), "Implicit Theories of Intelligence Predict Achievement Across an Adolescent Transition," *Child Development*, 78(1), 246–263 — holding a growth belief predicted rising math grades, and a short intervention reversed the grade decline seen in the comparison group.
      - Sisk, V., et al. (2018), "To What Extent and Under Which Circumstances Are Growth Mind-Sets Important to Academic Achievement? Two Meta-Analyses," *Psychological Science*, 29(4), 549–571 — the overall link between mindset and achievement was r ≈ .10 and the average intervention effect d ≈ 0.08, both very small. But for students from low-income households the intervention effect was d ≈ 0.34, and for academically at-risk students d ≈ 0.19.
      - Yeager, D., et al. (2019), "A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement," *Nature*, 573, 364–369 (N=12,490 students across 65 schools) — lower-achieving students gained +0.10 grade points, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.04, 0.16], meaning the true effect is very likely inside that range. Higher achievers gained about nothing, and enrollment in advanced math rose about 3 percentage points.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Even the skeptical meta-analysis and the large national trial converge on a pattern that cuts *in favor* of this BrainLift's argument: motivation-focused interventions do little for high achievers but meaningfully help lower-achieving and disadvantaged students. The group where the effect is real is precisely the group GT School's ability screen turns away.

- **Subcategory 4.4: Ability predicts strongly — yet intensive individual teaching helps the weakest students most**
  - **Source:** Schmidt & Hunter (1998); Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022); Robertson et al. (2010); Bloom (1984); Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Mastery learning" means a student does not move on to the next topic until they have demonstrated they have learned the current one. A "randomized controlled trial" assigns participants to treatment or comparison by chance, which is the strongest evidence a single study can produce.
      - Schmidt, F., & Hunter, J. (1998), *Psychological Bulletin*, 124(2), 262–274 — general mental ability is among the strongest predictors of performance, with a validity of about **.51** on a scale where 1.0 would be perfect prediction.
      - **A disputed correction:** Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022) argue that the statistical adjustments behind the familiar .51 figure systematically overcorrect for the fact that studies typically only observe people who were already selected. Their revised figure for cognitive ability is nearer **.31**. The two numbers answer questions corrected in different ways and should be reported side by side rather than treating .51 as settled.
      - Robertson, K., Smeets, S., Lubinski, D., & Benbow, C. (2010), "Beyond the Threshold Hypothesis," *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 19(6), 346–351 — ability *and* motivation are each independently necessary, and ability does not stop mattering once a student clears some threshold.
      - Bloom, B. (1984), "The 2 Sigma Problem," *Educational Researcher*, 13(6), 4–16 — one-to-one tutoring combined with mastery learning produced results about 2 standard deviations above conventional classroom instruction, and group mastery learning alone about 1 standard deviation. **An important caveat:** Bloom's two-standard-deviation figure has **never been reproduced**. It rests on small dissertation studies. Tutoring effects from properly designed studies are around **0.30–0.42 SD** (Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan 2024, a meta-analysis of 96 randomized trials; Cohen, Kulik & Kulik 1982 found about 0.33 SD). Treat "2 sigma" as an aspiration, not an expectation.
      - Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), "Effectiveness of Mastery Learning Programs: A Meta-Analysis," *Review of Educational Research*, 60(2), 265–299 — mastery learning raised exam scores by about 0.52 SD on average, with *stronger* effects for weaker students. These studies were mostly matched comparisons rather than randomized trials, so the randomized tutoring figure above is the more trustworthy benchmark.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two facts sit in tension. Ability is a genuinely strong predictor, so betting on motivation alone is risky. Yet intensive individualized instruction — the very mechanism GT School uses — produces its largest gains for weaker students. **Well-targeted, intensive tutoring evidence generally lands around 0.3–0.4 SD**, not Bloom's unreproduced 2 SD, and effects on independent broad standardized tests are typically smaller still: **~0.10–0.20 SD is the appropriate range for planning an evaluation** of those outcomes. Most of the randomized evidence comes from *struggling* students, so applying either figure to high-ability learners is a hypothesis to be tested, not a result GT can claim. The design question is whether GT School's program is the *kind* of teaching for which the behind-but-motivated student is the highest-return group — and only a purpose-built evaluation can answer it.

- **Subcategory 4.5: If not "motivation," what does predict growth? Prior ability, plus conscientiousness**
  - **Source:** Guez et al. (2018); McManus et al. (2013); Poropat (2009); Mammadov (2021); Duckworth & Seligman (2005); Meyer et al. (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Prior ability keeps predicting, with no ceiling at the top.** Guez, Peyre, Le Cam, Gauvrit & Ramus (2018), *Intelligence* — in a representative French sample of **30,489** students, the strength of the link running from IQ in grade 6 to achievement in grade 9 barely changes when the analysis is narrowed to the top 2% (β=.11) or top 5% (β=.15) of students. The relationship is essentially a straight line, with no point at which extra ability stops mattering. McManus et al. (2013), "The Academic Backbone," *BMC Medicine* — what a student achieved before is the single dominant predictor of what they achieve next, with year-to-year correlations within a school of about 0.73–0.75. Compare SMPY's finding in 2.3 that differences persist even inside the top 1%.
      - **The one measurable lever that is genuinely separate from ability is conscientiousness, or self-discipline — not grit and not mindset.** Poropat (2009), *Psychological Bulletin*, pooling 138 studies covering 70,926 people: conscientiousness is the strongest of the five main personality traits as a predictor of achievement, at a corrected r=.22, rising to .24 once intelligence is held constant, and it still adds prediction (.17) even *after* accounting for a student's prior grades. Mammadov (2021), across 267 samples and 413,074 people, puts conscientiousness at a corrected .27. Duckworth & Seligman (2005), *Psychological Science*, found that self-discipline accounted for **more than twice as much** of the difference in adolescents' grades as IQ did, and did so on top of prior grades, achievement tests, and IQ.
      - **Conscientiousness appears to work *with* ability rather than substituting for it at the high end.** Meyer et al. (2024), *European Journal of Personality*, with 18,637 participants, found a small effect in which conscientiousness *amplifies* the link from ability to achievement — although the wider literature is mixed, with other studies finding that one compensates for the other instead.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Prior ability and prior achievement remain the dominant predictors, and they do not level off at the top. Conscientiousness, or self-discipline, is the best-evidenced predictor in this literature that is genuinely independent of ability, while "grit" is largely a noisier stand-in for it. One caution: the conscientiousness evidence comes from general or mixed-ability samples. Whether it still adds anything within a group already drawn from the top few percent — where the range of both ability and conscientiousness is compressed — remains an open question.

  - **Source:** Alpha School / 2 Hour Learning marketing (company claims); Reason (Segan, 2026, independent reporting)

- **Subcategory 5.6: Why the Curated Cohort Plausibly Matters — the Pace Mechanism (and where GT School's self-paced model already neutralizes it)**
  - **Source:** Reis et al. (1998); Kulik & Kulik (1992); Steenbergen-Hu et al. (2016); Duflo, Dupas & Kremer (2011); the elite-illusion/self-paced findings
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Gifted students waste large amounts of time on already-mastered content:** Reis, Westberg, Kulikowich & Purcell (1998), *Gifted Child Quarterly* — teachers eliminated **40–50% of the regular curriculum** for 336 high-ability students in mixed-ability classes with **no loss** in achievement (out-of-level tested to avoid ceilings).
      - **The gain comes from pace/level, not peer sorting:** between-class grouping with no curriculum change ≈ null (g≈0.04–0.06), but **acceleration ≈ g=0.70** vs. same-age peers (Steenbergen-Hu et al. 2016, in 3.4); Kulik & Kulik (1992) — effects scale with the amount of curriculum/pace adjustment (accelerated gifted ≈ ~1 grade-level gain).
      - **Cleanest causal support for the compromise-pace problem:** Duflo, Dupas & Kremer (2011), *AER*, randomized across **121 schools** — mixed-ability classes force a compromise pace; tracking by readiness let instruction run at the group's level and helped **both** ends (~+0.14–0.18 SD).
      - **But GT School's self-paced software largely neutralizes this at the academic core:** because adaptive/mastery software paces each student individually, a fast student's rate is largely insulated from peers (the elite-illusion nulls show near-zero peer-composition effect on achievement — 3.1). The pace mechanism therefore bites mainly in the **shared components** — group workshops (Duflo) and finite guide attention — not the software.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Mixed-ability group instruction can force a compromise pace, while acceleration and curriculum compacting can benefit high-readiness students. GT School's self-paced software may reduce this mechanism in the academic core, but shared workshops and finite adult attention remain possible channels through which cohort composition could matter. The cited studies do not directly test that mechanism inside GT School.

- **Subcategory 5.7: Efficiency Is Not Yet Durable Learning — Transfer, Retention, and Independent Performance**
  - **Source:** Kulik & Fletcher (2016); Bailey et al. (2017); Bastani et al. (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kulik & Fletcher's meta-analysis of 50 controlled intelligent-tutoring evaluations reported a median effect of 0.66 SD, with average effects around 0.73 on locally developed tests and 0.13 on standardized tests. Outcome alignment materially changes the apparent effect.
      - Bastani et al. randomized nearly 1,000 high-school math students to textbook control, unrestricted GPT Base, or a teacher-informed hint-based GPT Tutor. GPT Base raised assisted-practice grades 48% but lowered later unaided grades 17% relative to control; GPT Tutor raised assisted performance 127% and largely mitigated the unaided harm. Tool performance and independent learning can move in opposite directions.
      - Bailey, Duncan, Odgers & Yu (2017) review widespread intervention fadeout and identify three routes to persistence: building malleable/fundamental skills that would not otherwise develop, opening a timely opportunity, and sustaining the gain through later environments. An immediate effect does not establish persistence.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A credible counterfactual solves only the attribution problem; it does not decide whether the measured outcome is sufficient evidence of learning. A positive MAP ITT would support the bounded claim that the complete package caused MAP growth for the randomized population. It would not by itself establish durable retention, far transfer, unaided performance after support disappears, broad educational quality, or an AI-specific mechanism.

### Category 6: The Equity Blind Spot — How Aptitude Screening Encodes Cultural, Racial, and Class Disparity

*Where Category 1 asks "does the screen miss able kids?", this category asks "which kids, systematically, and why?" It connects to 1.5 and 3.3 (universal screening as a partial remedy). The tension is deliberate: the same objective test that is more equitable than biased referral (1.5) is also correlated with the very advantages it claims to look past.*

- **Subcategory 6.1: Racial/ethnic underrepresentation in ability-identified gifted programs**
  - **Source:** NAGC (federal OCR/CRDC data); Grissom & Redding (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - NAGC, citing 2017–18 federal Office for Civil Rights data: Black and Hispanic students are represented in gifted programs at only ~55% and ~77% of their share of the K–12 population, while Asian American and White students are at ~196% and ~108%; historically underrepresented groups are underserved "by at least 50%."
      - Education Next analysis of 2017–18 CRDC: ~6.9% of students are in gifted programs; 27.3% of gifted students are Black or Hispanic vs. 47.7% of overall enrollment.
      - Grissom, J. A., & Redding, C. (2016), "Discretion and Disproportionality," *AERA Open*, 2(1) — using ECLS-K (>10,000 students): at identical math/reading achievement, Black students were assigned to gifted programs about *half* as often as White peers; overall, Black students were 66% and Hispanic 47% less likely to be identified; Black students were ~3× more likely to be identified when taught by a Black teacher.
      - Access differs before identification as well: about **90% of White students versus 83% of Black students** attended a school offering gifted services in the same national dataset.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Gifted programs identified through ability/achievement screening substantially under-enroll Black, Hispanic, and Native students relative to their population share, and the gap persists even among students with *identical* measured achievement. That residual indicates the disparity is not merely a difference in measured ability but in who gets identified and served.
    - **Link to source:** [https://www.nagc.org/identification](https://www.nagc.org/identification) | [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858415622175](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858415622175)
- **Subcategory 6.2: Cultural loading and the "culture-fair test" myth**
  - **Source:** Lohman (2005, *GCQ*); Lohman, Korb & Lakin (2008); historical origins of aptitude testing
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Lohman, D. F. (2005), "The Role of Nonverbal Ability Tests in Identifying Academically Gifted Students," *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 49(2), 111–138 — nonverbal ("culture-fair") tests are "neither culture free nor culture fair"; selecting on them alone would exclude the majority of the most academically accomplished students in every ethnic group.
      - Lohman, D. F., Korb, K. A., & Lakin, J. M. (2008), *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 52(4), 275–296 — across the Raven, NNAT, and CogAT (1,198 children, ~40% ELL), ELL children scored ~0.5–0.67 SD (8–10 standard-score points) lower on all three *nonverbal* tests, and none predicted ELL achievement well — the "culture-fair" remedy did not close the gap.
      - Historical framing (contested): U.S. mass aptitude testing traces to the WWI Army tests and to figures (Terman 1916; Brigham 1923, who then developed the SAT) who explicitly tied testing to racial hierarchy. Brigham later repudiated that premise and the College Board has disavowed the legacy; critics nonetheless argue the instruments still encode dominant-culture norms.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Even the tests marketed as bias-reducing leave large gaps for English-language learners and culturally diverse students, and the field's own leading psychometrician holds that no single test is culture-fair. The historical record shows aptitude testing was, at inception, entangled with claims of racial hierarchy — a contested but relevant backdrop to using such tests as gatekeepers.
    - **Link to source:** [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/001698620504900203](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/001698620504900203) | [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0016986208321808](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0016986208321808)
- **Subcategory 6.3: Class/socioeconomic disparity — and the compounding of test + tuition**
  - **Source:** SES–cognition literature (von Stumm et al.); Card & Giuliano (2016); reasoned synthesis
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - SES is among the most robust predictors of children's cognitive-test performance: highest- vs. lowest-SES children differ by ~6 IQ points at age 2, a gap that nearly triples by age 16 (von Stumm et al.); vocabulary shows the strongest SES association of any cognitive domain.
      - Card & Giuliano (2016): under the traditional referral system, only 28% of gifted 3rd-graders were Black or Hispanic vs. 60% of district enrollment; low-income and ELL students were systematically under-referred.
      - **Correction / honesty note:** the "test prep compounds the advantage" mechanism is **weaker than commonly assumed for SAT-style commercial coaching**: causally identified estimates are about **20 SAT points** after controlling for selection and ~**+9 percentile** in a federal review, and **no CogAT-specific coaching trial was located**. That does not make cognitive tests practice-proof. A meta-analysis of **174 samples / 153,185 people** found significant retest gains across cognitive tests, with form equivalence, interval, age, and prior exposures as moderators; identical forms produced larger gains. Raven-style matrices also improved across repeated untrained sittings through strategy learning. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003; Bors & Vigneau, 2003.)* The SES→score link is still chiefly developmental, while retest/form exposure is a separate validity and security threat.
      - Drawing the preceding threads together rather than citing a single statistic: Even setting coaching aside, when a program charges tuition (GT School ≈ $25k) or gates scholarships on the same aptitude test, the aptitude filter and the ability-to-pay filter — both correlated with SES — compound.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Cognitive-test scores are strongly correlated with socioeconomic status from before school entry, so an aptitude cutoff functions partly as a proxy for family advantage. That association is chiefly developmental rather than explained by SAT-style commercial coaching, but cognitive tests are not practice-proof: retesting, form reuse, and learned strategies are separate score-validity and security threats. A program that also charges substantial tuition applies a second, SES-correlated filter, concentrating access among the already-advantaged unless the design deliberately counteracts it.

- **Subcategory 6.4: Frameworks and remedies**
  - **Source:** NAGC equity position; Card & Giuliano (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - NAGC equity position ("Identifying and Serving Culturally and Linguistically Diverse Students"): "giftedness … is found among all racial, ethnic, and income groups"; "one test at a specific point in time should not dictate whether someone is identified"; recommends universal screening, multiple measures, local norms, and front-loading/talent development.
      - Card & Giuliano (2016) is the empirical proof-of-concept that a design change (universal screening) can materially reduce the disparity without lowering the eligibility bar.
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The equity literature converges on a prescription that mirrors the core identification-design question: single-point, single-test gatekeeping is the problem, and universal screening plus multiple measures and local norms is the evidence-based remedy. The disparity critique is therefore not an argument against selection but an argument for redesigning it.
