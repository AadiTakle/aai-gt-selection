# Project options

Eight one-paragraph options, ordered by how much we think each is worth to GT rather than by how easy it
is to argue for.

Every option works without changing anything about Timeback, without replacing the CogAT, and without
GT inserting itself into Riverside's testing process.

Options 2, 3 and 5 are components of option 1. They can ship inside it or stand alone as smaller
projects, and they are written both ways so you can choose.

---

## 1. Model the admissions filter

Joe wants 100,000 children who reach MIT-level work by fourteen. What decides whether GT finds them is
not the CogAT itself, it is the filter GT has built around the CogAT, and nobody has ever modelled that
filter. We would build the model and hand Joe the controls. Every part of the current rule becomes a
dial: where the cut sits, how much error the test carries at that point, whether the reading requirement
sits before the CogAT or beside it, what share of applicants arrive already practised, and how big the
applicant pool is. The model reports what the filter does with those settings. How many children it
admits who will struggle, how many it turns away who would have succeeded, and how both numbers move
when any dial moves. Joe set the number 95, so the meeting where he drags it himself and watches the
consequences is a different meeting from one where we show him a slide about reliability.

The finding it exists to demonstrate is that **GT's filter gets less accurate as GT gets bigger.** How
many children a cutoff misclassifies depends on how many applicants sit close to it. GT's applicants are
self-selected, they are applying to a school that publishes a 95th-percentile requirement, and a growing
share of them arrive practised, so the pool piles up just above the line, which is the one place the test
has the least to say. Today Crystal absorbs that by hand and offers a six-month retest to anyone "sitting
on the edge." At 100,000 applicants she cannot, and an error that a person was quietly correcting becomes
a policy nobody wrote.

The model also prices the gates, which is the part GT could act on for free. In the research on gifted
screening, placing any gate in front of the test drops the share of gifted children found from 84% to
28%, and requiring a child to clear two separate bars caps it near half. If GT's reading requirement
sits in front of the CogAT rather than beside it, moving it costs nothing and the model says what it is
currently worth, in children per 100,000 applicants.

What it deliberately will not do is tell Joe to move his cutoff. It reports which assumption the answer
is most sensitive to, and names the one measurement GT could take to replace that assumption with a
fact. That is the same posture as our placement prototype, which reported a break-even instead of
declaring a winner.

**Addresses:** whether the cut is in the right place, what the gate ordering costs, score inflation from
practice, and a borderline policy that will not survive scale. **Needs from GT:** nothing to build it.
To calibrate it, the CogAT's precision near the cut, and eventually the outcomes of admitted children,
which would turn its central assumption into a measurement.

## 2. Own the prep, so the score means the same thing for every child

Crystal already knows this is happening: "CogAT is one of the few things that parents, especially hustler
parents, will make their kids practice." The research puts a size on it. Across 153,000 people, simply
sitting the same kind of reasoning test a second time raises the score by about a third of a standard
deviation, and by half by the third sitting, with nobody teaching anything. The effect is larger in
younger children, which is the band Crystal says is hardest to read. And the inflated score predicts real
performance worse than the first one did. So a strict cut at the 95th percentile hands free points to the
families who knew to prepare, and those points mean less than the ones they displaced. We give every
applicant the same preparation so the cut compares like with like. This closes the same fairness exposure
that made Joe ban an in-house test, without touching the test.

**Addresses:** score inflation that currently favours prepared families, and the fairness risk Joe is
already worried about. **Needs from GT:** a slot in the applicant experience to deliver it.

## 3. Stop rejecting children because they cannot read yet

Crystal raised this twice without being asked: "we absolutely have to make sure that we can somehow
separate their ability to read, both comprehension and decode, versus the content itself." She has
children with grade-8 vocabulary and grade-4 decoding, and children who could handle grade-4 or 5 math
but cannot read the math, so it "is going to look like they're only" low. Putting a reading requirement
in front of a reasoning measure is costly in a specific and measurable way, which is the 84% to 28% drop
above. We build a short reasoning check where the identical question is either read aloud to the child or
has to be read by them, and report the difference between the two. Where a child's reasoning outruns
their reading, Crystal sees it before the application is declined rather than afterward.

**Addresses:** the reading confound Crystal raised twice, the K-2 band that cannot read yet, and
avoidable rejections. **Needs from GT:** confirmation of whether the reading requirement sits before the
CogAT or beside it. The value swings hard on that answer.

## 4. Measure how much repetition a child needs

This is Crystal's own criterion in her own words. An Alpha student may need to see material repeatedly,
use flashcards, and take a test "six times, seven times, eight times" to reach 90% mastery, and "we can't
have them needing that type of repetition for it to sink in the same way." That is a measurable thing,
and it happens to be the one learning-speed measure with solid research behind it. Counting how many
attempts a child needs to reach a fixed standard holds up across a three-year gap and tracks ability,
unlike the "how fast do they learn" scores that sound similar and fall apart under scrutiny. We build a
short block where the child learns something genuinely new inside the assessment, and report how many
tries it took. It is the closest thing to what Crystal means by wishing she could measure drive that has
real evidence under it, and it measures learning rather than cognitive ability, so it sits outside Joe's
constraint.

**Addresses:** repetition tolerance as a stated admissions criterion, and part of the drive question.
**Needs from GT:** agreement on what a child should be asked to learn, since the content has to be new to
every applicant.

## 5. Give the borderline band a written rule

Today a child sitting on the line is handled by judgment. Crystal would consider a six-month retest "if
someone's sitting on the edge," which works at 350 applicants and breaks at 100,000, and leaves GT with
no written answer when a family asks why their child was declined. Every test score is a range rather
than a point, and at a high cutoff the errors run in opposite directions: a child landing exactly on the
line is more likely to have been lucky, while a genuinely gifted child slips just under it easily.
Reliability is at its worst in young children, which is when GT screens. We build the rule: for each
applicant, the probability their true score clears the cut, a flagged borderline band with a defined next
step, and a record of the reasoning behind every decision. It reads the CogAT result and changes nothing
about the test.

**Addresses:** undocumented borderline decisions, and the defensibility of a rejection at scale.
**Needs from GT:** whether Riverside publishes the test's precision at the top of the scale. Without
that, this has to run on a modelled estimate instead of a published one.

## 6. Fix the K-2 band

Crystal calls this her hardest problem: "our K through two band is still the most difficult to get an
accurate reading from a CogAT alone," and a kindergarten 95th percentile is "almost good for like two to
three years." Three separate things account for most of that and nobody has put them together. Test
scores are least reliable in young children. At a high cutoff, luck pulls scores back toward the line, so
early high scorers drift down whatever GT does. And practice inflation is largest in the youngest
children, so part of what looks like a child losing their giftedness is a preparation bump wearing off.
We measure how long a K-2 CogAT actually predicts anything using GT's own records, then add a short
reading-free check aimed only at that band, which is the one place the current instrument is weakest and
a supplement has the most room to help.

**Addresses:** the weakest band in the funnel, and the two-to-three-year shelf life Crystal describes.
**Needs from GT:** historical K-2 CogAT scores with later performance attached.

## 7. Make admission provable

Joe's claim is that a child who starts at GT by kindergarten leaves at fourteen with eight APs and a
1550. Right now nobody outside GT has to believe it, because GT admits only children in the top few
percent and children in the top few percent do well in a lot of places. Until GT can separate what its
program does from who it selects, MIT-by-8 is a result GT cannot use to justify going from hundreds of
students to a hundred thousand. We design an admission process that answers it. Among applicants who
clear a capability bar, some places are offered by lottery. The families offered and the families not
offered then form a fair comparison, and inside two years GT has real proof of what the program adds
rather than what its admissions filter adds. This is the only option here that produces that, and it is
also the largest change to how GT admits anyone.

**Addresses:** the fact that GT's headline result is currently unprovable, and the evidence Joe needs for
scale. **Needs from GT:** more applicants than places, and Joe's agreement to offer some places by
lottery. Both are open.

## 8. Turn the application into the pitch

Crystal wants a change in posture: "I would like to flip that narrative that it is a privilege that we
consider you for an elite campus like GT School. You tell me why I should take your child." Today the
application is a form followed by a wait. Many GT families are also wary of technology, and she ends up
explaining by hand that Math Raiders is teaching rather than entertaining. We rebuild the
applicant-facing experience to do both jobs at once: make the standard visible, tell the family plainly
what GT will ask of their child, and ask the family to make their case. On its own this is a presentation
change rather than a capability. It is listed because it is the surface several options above would ship
inside, so it is worth building once deliberately instead of five times by accident.

**Addresses:** the stated positioning shift, and parent scepticism. **Needs from GT:** design authority
over the applicant-facing portal, which is nearly finished already.
