## 1. What changed: Joe's "MIT by 8" mandate

The single largest change. Crystal, opening:

> "his new mandate is this MIT by eight, meaning if a child starts with this by kindergarten, they
> will complete eight AP classes at a level five and get a 1550 on their SATs by the time this child
> is 14 years old and leaving GT school, and this includes both our physical campus and our online
> campus."

What it implies for selection, in her words:

- The criterion is a child who "can not only function on Timeback completely without the need and
  assistance of someone to interfere, but that they can do a minimum of **two grade levels every
  single year** they're in it at an **A level**, so that by the time they're in sixth grade, they're
  completing their AP classes."
- "all the other ideas that have been came up for what quote giftedness is can't necessarily achieve
  this mandate. So everything has to be built around that being the ultimate goal."
- Explicitly excluded: "none of the frou-frouness of like well they did a gifted art project or
  they're an excellent musician — that's wonderful and that is a version of gifted but that does not
  mean they're going to succeed in our program."
- **95th percentile CogAT cutoff, strictly applied.** "he is quite strict on that."
- Joe's stated scale target is **100,000 kids**.
- Joe wants every currently enrolled GT Anywhere student retested — "All 350 of them and counting."

Crystal notes the mandate is recent and not yet universally known: she had not spoken to Arvin and
David about it and was "unsure if they're even fully aware that this has now been mandated."

## 2. No in-house cognitive test

Load-bearing constraint, and the reason is legal rather than technical:

> "part of the reason that he is doing this mandate of absolutely not to change CogAT testing unless
> we have a better third-party testing service for cognitive abilities. **He does not want to do it
> in-house** because we are opening up a discrimination can of worms like there's no tomorrow. Who's
> to say we're not only taking a certain type of person in this program... and it is a PR nightmare."

The internship lead states the consequence directly — "that was a lot harder for their actual project
because they were making an in-house replacement for the cogat" — and Crystal confirms: "Correct."

A replacement instrument is available only via a **better third-party service**, not via GT-built
software. Decision authority is unambiguous: "Joe is the one that makes the ultimate decisions for
GT School."

## 3. Hustlers are no longer the admissions target

The July interview treated "hustler kids" as a group to catch. This call reverses that for
admissions, on time arithmetic rather than on measurement grounds:

> "Even a hustler kid to get to MIT is going to struggle with enough hours in the day... they'd have
> to go back to a six-hour day or an eight-hour day in order to be able to accomplish this level of
> academic rigor. In which case, where's the difference between us and another camp?"

The deeper reason is what MIT-by-8 requires beyond the APs: tier-one university admission also needs
"some sort of unique, creative way of applying those science or original scholarship concepts." If a
hustler spends six to eight hours on APs, there is no time left for the Alpha X-style projects and
ikigai work that produce that. Workshops therefore have to scaffold toward high-school-level project
work by grade 6–8.

**Her observed data, with her own caveat.** Of roughly 25 children at GT School using the apps:
below the 90th percentile is "extraordinarily struggling"; a few at 93 "can almost" manage it. She
volunteers the limitation herself — "I do not have a large sample set to make that proof yet."

Related, on repetition tolerance: an Alpha student may need to see material repeatedly, use
flashcards, and take a test "six times, seven times, eight times" to reach 90% mastery. "We can't
have them needing that type of repetition for it to sink in the same way."

## 4. What Crystal says she needs

Ordered by how hard she pushed, not by feasibility.

### 4.1 Measure drive — her one stated unknown

> "I wish I could measure drive."

The pain is concrete: 99th-percentile CogAT children who lack it. "we definitely have a couple of
kids who are 99 percentile, and they will with the proper coaching and motivation... but good God,
it would have been nice to get a heads up that these kids are just like whatever."

She volunteered the shape of a solution unprompted:

> "I love the idea of doing a drive, whatever that looks like, a drive quiz that doesn't have to be
> like we don't have to pretend it's gonna overtake the CogAT, or that it's different than MAP. It
> can be something a little more fun that they can do on their own... while they're waiting for the
> CogAT. It'll have some prep things on the CogAT, but it also has a way that we can sort of
> data-wise check their motivation and drive to do hard things, and **not unsolvable problems**, but
> things that do kind of push them to think a little weird or be a little outside of the box."

She also notes a parent-report version would not work: "A parent's not going to be the same way at
filling out a drive... It's going to be very kid geared anyway."

### 4.2 The K–2 band, where CogAT is weakest

> "our K through two band is still the most difficult to get an accurate reading from a CogAT alone."

Because "research shows that a child who might get a 95th percentile in a CogAT in kindergarten is
not necessarily reflection of them maintaining rigor past grade three — it's almost good for like
two to three years." She lists possible causes (loss of stimulation, a different learning curve) but
treats them as open.

Her framing of the missing measure is a delay-of-gratification one: "some of that just has to do with
marshmallow style test: **is a kid willing to do the hard things for the long term? And I don't know
how you test that.**"

### 4.3 Separating reading from content — raised twice

> "whenever you're making something that is a reading requirement, we absolutely have to make sure
> that we can somehow separate their ability to read — both comprehension and decode — versus the
> content itself."

Supporting detail:

- "I've got kids who are in grade eight vocabulary and grade four reading... the comprehension is
  great, **except when they have to read to themselves**."
- Oral language routinely exceeds decoding: a child may have extensive knowledge of a topic "but they
  really can't read or articulate it."
- The 85th-percentile MAP reading gate does not mean what it appears to at young ages. "even at the
  85th percent cutoff in reading, kindergarten level is still not actually reading yet. It means
  you're good at decoding. You're not reading fluently yet."
- The cost is misclassification rather than failure: such a child "might also be like a fourth or
  fifth grade math student, but they can't read the math content... so it's going to look like
  they're only [low]." Asked whether these children then struggle on Timeback: "Not necessarily, but
  **it doesn't allow them to show off what they know either**."

### 4.4 True grade level without a full mastery placement

> "it would be cool if there was also an actual... if there was a way that we could test true grade
> levels without doing full mastery placements."

Her example: a grade-7 student who "hit like 99 percentiles on all his MAP screeners" and is "still
struggling in grade three, four coursework." Composites hide gaps — "You could be phenomenal at
algebra, but really terrible at geometry. So it might skew your math score, but you might actually
have huge gaps here."

The interns raise the obvious objection in the call: a real placement test would run six hours, and a
child would not stay motivated through it.

### 4.5 CogAT familiarization in the admissions portal

Wanted, and specified:

> "one of the things that I did think would be valuable at one point was for my admissions portal
> itself... a page that goes through: what is a CogAT, what are you being tested on, here are some
> sample questions that you can use to prepare, here is why we do this test."

The window exists because testing is batched. "CogATs happen once or twice a month, where it's just a
bulk import of people doing this testing, because we have to find a way to do it with proper
proctoring at a larger scale, so the kids will have a week or two weeks or a month sometimes out
while they're waiting." She wants prep as part of "the drip feed nurture campaign."

Her one hesitation about prep, thinking aloud: "if the child is spending all this time working on how
to pass a CogAT test, it's very possible that they're losing the academic [time]."

The internship lead's read is that a static resource hub is too small — the interns "probably do that
in a day."

### 4.6 Learning speed

> "the answer is how fast they learn. Plain and simple would be extremely helpful. How fast they can
> take new information and continue, and CogAT does a decent job of that, but it also can't."

## 5. Operational facts

- **CogAT is coachable, and GT knows it.** "The CogAT actually can be improved upon with time... You're
  not going to be better at it in a month, but in six months you might if you were practicing." It is
  timed after grade 3, so speed matters. "CogAT is one of the few things that parents, especially
  hustler parents, will make their kids practice to be able to get into any gifted program... This is
  not an unusual GT thing. So there's a lot of resources online."
- **No retake within an application cycle.** It has never come up — "I haven't had anybody ask to. But
  the answer is no." Reapplying the following year is allowed, and she would consider a six-month
  bump "if someone's sitting on the edge."
- **Large-scale CogAT delivery is her biggest admissions blocker.** "the testing part is the biggest
  holdup because I don't know how to do a large scale CogAT yet, because the software for Riverside
  is just terrible and old. But we don't have a choice but to use it because it's a proprietary
  testing service" and "it has to be through their secure server."
- **The admissions portal is nearly finished.** "my portal's almost done."
- **GT Anywhere is not currently CogAT testing** except voluntarily, "which is bizarre and going to end."
- Asked whether any other part of admissions needs help: "Not at the moment... it's pretty cut and dry
  to a certain extent. I mean, I wish it wasn't, but it kind of is." She notes other GT departments
  have needs — "we need AP classes properly on Timeback yesterday."

## 6. Brand and positioning constraints

- **No video-game framing.** "we do not want to be known as the school that's using video games to
  track. Do you know what I mean? Elite product." Many GT parents are anti-tech, and she has had to
  explain to parents that Math Raiders is teaching rather than entertaining.
- The bar is "make it elite and streamlined, that it feels fun and entertaining for a kid **without
  cheapening the brand**."
- A Quizlet-style clone was judged unlikely to impress Joe.
- Desired posture shift: "I would like to flip that narrative that it is a privilege that we consider
  you for an elite campus like GT School. You tell me why I should take your child."

## 7. Crystal's methodological caution

Worth recording because it bears on how this project should use AI:

> "AI is fantastic at putting together puzzle pieces that exist before... but we also have to try to
> come up with something that has no frame of reference... Make sure that we're not relying on what's
> being told to us, because there might be a better way that we're all ignorant about... the people
> in the field who are saying 'hey, a problem is happening here' — AI doesn't know that until we're
> all sort of having these thoughts."

She is a heavy AI user herself ("Claude Code is like my best friend... does the job of like 50
people"), so this is a caution about novelty, not about tooling.
