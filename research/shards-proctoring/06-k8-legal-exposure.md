# Proctoring K–8 Children at Home: The Legal Exposure That Attaches Because They Are Children

*Shard compiled 2026-07-30. **This is not legal advice, and its author is not a lawyer.** It is a sourced map of what the statutes, regulations, and decided cases actually say, written so that a non-lawyer can see where the law is settled, where it is contested, and where it is silent. Every case name, holding, statute section, deadline, and damages figure below was checked against a primary or near-primary source, and anything alleged, pending, argued, or merely reported is labelled as such. Verify current law with counsel before relying on any of it.*

**General idea.** Nearly every published account of remote-proctoring law is about adults — university students, bar candidates, professional certification. Move the examinee down to kindergarten through eighth grade and four things change at once. First, a separate federal statute switches on below age 13 and switches off above it, so a K–8 cohort sits on both sides of a bright statutory line, and at least one federal court has held that the *younger* child ends up with *fewer* private remedies than the older one because the federal children's statute preempted her state-law claim. Second, the education-privacy statutes everyone reaches for — FERPA, PPRA — are conditioned on federal education funding, which means they may not reach a private-school operator at all, and FERPA has no private right of action regardless. Third, the real weight sits in state law: well over a hundred state student-data statutes imposing contract, deletion, breach, and parent-rights duties, plus one biometric statute in Illinois that carries a private right of action and liquidated damages, and where the decisive question is whether the software *identifies* a face or merely *detects* one. Fourth, the camera is pointed into a child's home, which brings in recording-consent law, video-voyeurism law, and — if the operator is a covered "provider" — a federal reporting duty triggered by what the camera might accidentally capture. Underneath all of it is a structural gap: the adult whose consent makes the whole thing lawful is the same adult best positioned to help the child cheat, and no located US statute, regulation, or decided case addresses that conflict. The law verifies *who* the parent is. It has nothing to say about what the parent will do.

## 1. The age-13 boundary: what attaches below it, and what happens to a cohort that straddles it

**Source:** Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 6501–6506, and the COPPA Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 312, as amended April 2025; decided federal preemption cases from the Central District of Illinois and the Ninth Circuit; state comprehensive privacy statutes with teen-specific provisions.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- COPPA and its Rule apply to a "child," defined as an individual under the age of 13, and impose no obligations of their own with respect to a 13-year-old ([16 C.F.R. § 312.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2); [FTC, Complying with COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)).
- The Rule reaches operators of services directed to children under 13 *and* operators of general-audience services with actual knowledge that they are collecting personal information from a child under 13 ([FTC, Complying with COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)).
- A photograph, video, or audio file containing a child's image or voice is itself "personal information" under the Rule, independent of any biometric analysis performed on it ([16 C.F.R. § 312.2(8)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2)).
- Verifiable parental consent is required *before* any collection, use, or disclosure of a child's personal information, with no grace period and no incidental-collection carve-out in the text ([16 C.F.R. § 312.5(a)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.5)).
- The 2025 amendments created a stand-alone definition of a "mixed audience website or online service": one that is child-directed but does not target children as its primary audience, and that collects no personal information from any visitor — beyond the limited § 312.5(c) purposes — before collecting age information or otherwise determining whether the visitor is a child ([90 Fed. Reg. 16918](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm); [Latham & Watkins summary](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)).
- Any age screen must be "done in a neutral manner that does not default to a set age or encourage visitors to falsify age information," which practitioner guidance reads as requiring an open date-of-birth question rather than an "are you over 13?" checkbox ([90 Fed. Reg. 16918](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm); [Davis Wright Tremaine analysis](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2025/05/coppa-rule-ftc-amended-childrens-privacy)).
- Acceptable methods of verifiable parental consent are an enumerated list in § 312.5(b)(2) — signed form, monetary transaction, toll-free call to trained personnel, video-conference with trained personnel, government-ID check against a database, knowledge-based authentication, or facial-recognition match of a live capture to a government photo ID — plus an FTC-approval route for new methods ([16 C.F.R. § 312.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.5); [FTC, Verifiable Parental Consent](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/verifiable-parental-consent-childrens-online-privacy-rule)).
- Where an operator uses the facial-recognition consent method, both the parent's government ID and the captured facial image must be promptly deleted after the match is confirmed ([16 C.F.R. § 312.5(b)(2)(vii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.5)).
- COPPA has no private right of action; it is enforced by the FTC and, under 15 U.S.C. § 6504, by state attorneys general, who must notify the FTC before filing ([FTC, Complying with COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)).
- A court may hold an operator liable for civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation, a figure the FTC adjusts annually for inflation ([FTC, Complying with COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)).
- The 2025 amendments took effect 23 June 2025 with a general compliance date of 22 April 2026 ([90 Fed. Reg. 16918](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm)).
- A federal district court dismissed the Illinois biometric-privacy claim of an under-13 plaintiff on the ground that COPPA preempted it, while the claims of the older co-plaintiffs proceeded — the court later described its own ruling as "dismissing only the claims of former plaintiff H.K." *(H.K. v. Google LLC, 595 F. Supp. 3d 702 (C.D. Ill. 31 March 2022), No. 1:21-cv-01122; subsequent order of 21 August 2023 characterising the earlier ruling.)* ([vLex record](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/h-k-v-google-927676835); [later order, CourtListener](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.ilcd.83081/gov.uscourts.ilcd.83081.45.0.pdf))
- A practitioner analysis characterises that holding as "COPPA preempts BIPA claims asserted by plaintiffs under age of 13 because BIPA and COPPA impose different substantive standards," citing 595 F. Supp. 3d at 709–11 ([Covington, Inside Class Actions](https://www.insideclassactions.com/2023/01/16/ninth-circuit-holds-coppa-does-not-preempt-consistent-state-law-claims-premised-on-coppa-violations/)) `[secondary characterisation of a district-court holding; the underlying opinion text was not retrieved directly]`.
- The Ninth Circuit reached the opposite general conclusion, holding that "COPPA's preemption clause does not bar state-law causes of action that are parallel to, or proscribe the same conduct forbidden by, COPPA," and that neither express nor conflict preemption applied *(Jones v. Google LLC, No. 21-16281 (9th Cir., amended opinion filed 13 July 2023), reversing dismissal.)* ([Ninth Circuit opinion PDF](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2023/07/13/21-16281.pdf))
- The Ninth Circuit amended its opinion to note that the FTC, in an amicus brief filed at the court's request, supports the conclusion that COPPA does not preempt consistent state privacy claims ([Orrick InfoBytes, 19 July 2023](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2023-07-19/9th-circuit-denies-en-banc-hearing-coppa-preemption-question/)).
- Above 13, most state comprehensive privacy laws define personal data of a *known child under 13* as sensitive data requiring opt-in consent and COPPA-compliant processing, and many require a documented data protection assessment for such processing ([Legal 500, Minor Privacy Laws in the U.S.](https://www.legal500.com/guides/hot-topic/minor-privacy-laws-in-the-u-s-a-moving-target/)).
- Several states extend protections upward past 13 on different age lines: Delaware bars targeted advertising and sale where the controller knows or wilfully disregards that the consumer is 13 to 17; Connecticut imposes the same bar effective 1 July 2026; Maryland reaches consumers the controller "knows or has reason to know" are under 18; Minnesota's equivalent applies to 13-to-16-year-olds ([Legal 500, Minor Privacy Laws in the U.S.](https://www.legal500.com/guides/hot-topic/minor-privacy-laws-in-the-u-s-a-moving-target/)).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** Below 13, a federal statute imposes a pre-collection consent gate, a closed list of acceptable consent methods, a written security programme, a written retention policy, and per-violation civil penalties — and webcam video of the child is personal information under it whether or not anything biometric is derived. Above 13, none of that federal apparatus applies, and the child is covered instead by a patchwork of state teen provisions drawing lines at 16, 17, or 18. The operational consequence for a K–8 population is that a neutral age screen becomes a load-bearing product feature rather than a formality, because the entire compliance regime forks on its output and the Rule forbids collecting anything before it runs. The preemption picture is genuinely unsettled and cuts counterintuitively: one district court held that COPPA displaced an under-13 child's state biometric claim while her older sibling's survived, and the Ninth Circuit — backed by the FTC as amicus — held the opposite as a general matter, so whether the youngest examinees have *more* or *fewer* private remedies than the oldest currently depends on which court hears the case.

## 2. FERPA, PPRA, and the funding condition that decides whether either applies

**Source:** Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, 20 U.S.C. § 1232g and 34 C.F.R. Part 99; Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment, 20 U.S.C. § 1232h and 34 C.F.R. Part 98; US Department of Education Student Privacy Policy Office guidance; *Gonzaga University v. Doe*, 536 U.S. 273 (2002).

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- FERPA applies to educational agencies and institutions that receive funds under any programme administered by the US Secretary of Education ([34 C.F.R. § 99.1; ED joint FERPA/HIPAA guidance](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/2019%20HIPAA%20FERPA%20Joint%20Guidance%20508.pdf)).
- The Department of Education states plainly that "private and parochial schools at the elementary and secondary level generally do not receive such funding and are, therefore, not subject to FERPA" ([ED Student Privacy Policy Office FAQ](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/which-educational-agencies-or-institutions-does-ferpa-apply)).
- Where a FERPA-covered district places a student in a private school for services under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, that student's records — including those the private school maintains — are subject to FERPA ([ED/HHS joint guidance](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/2019%20HIPAA%20FERPA%20Joint%20Guidance%20508.pdf)).
- An outside contractor may be treated as a "school official" without parental consent only if it performs an institutional service the school would otherwise use employees for, is under the school's direct control as to the use and maintenance of education records, is subject to the redisclosure restrictions of § 99.33(a), and meets the school-official criteria stated in the school's annual FERPA notification ([34 C.F.R. § 99.31(a)(1)(i)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/99.31); [ED, Who is a "school official"?](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/who-school-official-under-ferpa)).
- A school with a policy of disclosing records to outsourced school officials must state, in its annual notification, the criteria for who counts as a school official and what counts as a legitimate educational interest ([34 C.F.R. § 99.7(a)(3)(iii), as described in ED guidance](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/FERPA%20Guidance%20for%20School%20Officials%20on%20Student%20Health%20Records.pdf)).
- The Department's 2008 rulemaking preamble explains "direct control" as requiring schools to make clear in service agreements that the outside party may not use or allow access to record data except as the disclosing school specifies ([ED cloud-computing FAQ, quoting 73 Fed. Reg. 74816](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/FAQ_Cloud_Computing_0.pdf)).
- The Department can impose what it informally calls the "five-year rule," instructing the originating school not to give a violating outside organisation any further access to student record data for at least five years, and that sanction applies whether or not the organisation itself receives Department funds ([ED, FERPA and Volunteer and Partnership Organizations, 2021](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/ferpa-and-community-based-orgs_2021.pdf)).
- The Supreme Court held that FERPA's nondisclosure provisions "create no personal rights to enforce under § 1983," reasoning that they contain no rights-creating language, have an aggregate rather than individual focus, and serve primarily to direct the Secretary's distribution of funds *(Gonzaga University v. Doe, 536 U.S. 273 (2002).)* ([Supreme Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-679.ZO.html))
- PPRA applies to the programmes and activities of a state educational agency, local educational agency, or other recipient of funds under any programme funded by the US Department of Education ([ED, PPRA topic page](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/topic/protection-pupil-rights-amendment-ppra)).
- PPRA requires prior written parental consent before a student is required to submit to a survey, analysis, or evaluation revealing any of eight protected categories, one of which is "mental or psychological problems of the student or the student's family" ([20 U.S.C. § 1232h(b)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A20+section%3A1232h+edition%3Aprelim%29)).
- Where a protected-information survey is not funded by a Department programme, PPRA instead requires notice and an opportunity for the parent to opt the student out ([ED PPRA model notice](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/20-0379.PPRA_508.pdf)).
- PPRA separately requires notice and opt-out for any non-emergency, invasive physical examination or screening required by an LEA as a condition of attendance, and for activities collecting personal information from students for marketing or sale ([ED PPRA model notice](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/20-0379.PPRA_508.pdf)).
- No authority was located applying PPRA's "survey, analysis, or evaluation" language to a cognitive-ability assessment; the nearest of the eight categories is the mental-or-psychological-problems category, and whether an ability test falls inside it appears unaddressed *(negative finding from this search pass.)* `[absence of located authority, not a holding that PPRA does not apply]`
- In Autumn 2024 the Department of Education affirmed an intention to propose amendments to the FERPA regulations, including clarifying "provisions governing non-consensual disclosures of personally identifiable information from education records to third parties," and the FTC expressly declined to finalise its proposed ed-tech COPPA amendments to avoid conflicting with them ([90 Fed. Reg. 16918](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm)).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** FERPA is a funding condition, not a general privacy law, and the Department of Education says in terms that private and parochial K–12 schools generally fall outside it — so an operator whose customer is a private school may find that FERPA imposes nothing directly, while an operator whose customer is a public district must satisfy four conjunctive school-official conditions and accept the school's direct control over how it uses and keeps the data. Even where FERPA applies, *Gonzaga* forecloses a damages suit by a parent; the exposure is administrative, and for a vendor the sharpest form of it is the Department's five-year access ban, which reaches organisations that receive no federal money at all. PPRA is a narrower instrument than its reputation suggests: it is triggered by the same federal-funding hook, it lists eight specific content categories, and nothing located applies it to a cognitive-ability test — the honest answer is that this is an open question, not a settled exemption. The whole federal education-privacy layer is also mid-revision, with the Department having signalled amendments to the third-party disclosure rules and the FTC having held back its own ed-tech provisions to wait for them.

## 3. State student-data-privacy law: the shape of the obligation, and the handful that actually bite

**Source:** Future of Privacy Forum and Public Interest Privacy Center counts of enacted state student-privacy laws; California Bus. & Prof. Code § 22584 (SOPIPA/KOPIPA); Illinois Student Online Personal Protection Act, 105 ILCS 85; New York Education Law § 2-d and 8 NYCRR Part 121; Colorado Student Data Transparency and Security Act, C.R.S. art. 16 of tit. 22; New York State Technology Law § 106-b and the 2023 Commissioner's determination.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- The Future of Privacy Forum's policymaker guide states that 40 states have passed 116 student-privacy laws since 2013 ([FPF, Policymakers' Guide to Student Privacy](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/FPF-Policymakers-Guide-to-Student-Privacy-Final.pdf)).
- The Public Interest Privacy Center states that state policymakers have passed "nearly 150 student privacy laws in 47 states and Washington, DC," and that over a thousand such bills have been introduced since 2014 ([Public Interest Privacy Center](https://publicinterestprivacy.org/resources/state-student-privacy/)) `[the two counts differ; they use different date ranges and inclusion criteria]`.
- The recurring categories these laws impose on vendors are: prohibitions on targeted advertising, sale, and profiling outside school purposes; mandatory written agreements with schools; security-programme duties; breach-notification deadlines; data-deletion obligations; and parent rights to inspect, copy, and correct ([FPF guide](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/FPF-Policymakers-Guide-to-Student-Privacy-Final.pdf); [Public Interest Privacy Center](https://publicinterestprivacy.org/resources/state-student-privacy/)).
- California's statute applies to an "operator" with actual knowledge that its site, service, or application "is used primarily for K–12 school purposes and was designed and marketed for K–12 school purposes" ([Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 22584(a)(7)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=22584)).
- A California legislative committee analysis states that enforcement of that statute is "primarily to public enforcement, with no express statutory private right of action authorizing pupils, students, or parents to bring civil actions for violations, and no provision for statutory damages or attorney's fees" ([AB 1159 committee analysis](https://billtexts.s3.amazonaws.com/ca/ca-analysishttps-leginfo-legislature-ca-gov-faces-billAnalysisClient-xhtml-bill-id-202520260AB1159-ca-analysis-393864.pdf)).
- A pending California bill, AB 1159, would replace "used primarily" and "designed and marketed" with "used for" and "designed or marketed," and would add a limited private right of action for those suffering actual harm, subject to a notice-and-cure framework ([AB 1159 bill text](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB1159)) `[pending legislation as of this compilation; not law]`.
- Illinois SOPPA requires an operator to notify the school of a breach of covered information no later than 30 calendar days after determining a breach occurred, and requires the school to notify affected parents no later than 30 calendar days after receiving that notice ([105 ILCS 85](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3806&ChapterID=17&Print=True)).
- Illinois SOPPA requires schools to post each written agreement with an operator within 10 business days of entering it, and provides that any agreement entered in violation of the Act "is void and unenforceable as against public policy" ([105 ILCS 85](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3806&ChapterID=17&Print=True)).
- Illinois SOPPA's enforcement section provides only that "[v]iolations of this Act shall constitute unlawful practices for which the Attorney General may take appropriate action under the Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act" ([105 ILCS 85/35](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3806&ChapterID=17&Print=True)).
- The Illinois Consumer Fraud Act separately provides that "[a]ny person who suffers actual damage as a result of a violation of this Act committed by any other person may bring an action against such person," subject to a three-year limitation ([815 ILCS 505/10a](https://law.onecle.com/illinois/815ilcs505/10a.html)) `[whether a SOPPA violation can be privately litigated through that route was not resolved by any authority located here; other Illinois statutes expressly negate a private right of action in their Consumer Fraud Act cross-reference, and SOPPA does not]`.
- New York requires each educational agency to publish a parents' bill of rights for data privacy and security and to include it in every contract with a third-party contractor that receives student data ([N.Y. Educ. Law § 2-d(3)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ny/education-law/edn-sect-2-d/)).
- Under New York's implementing regulation, a third-party contractor must notify the educational agency of a breach or unauthorised release "no more than seven calendar days after such discovery," and the agency must notify affected parents no more than 14 calendar days after discovery or receipt of the contractor's notice ([8 NYCRR Part 121, as published by NYSED Office of Counsel](https://www.counsel.nysed.gov/rules/indices-fulltext/2019/010)).
- Each breach or unauthorised release by a third-party contractor in New York is punishable by a civil penalty of the greater of $5,000 or up to $10 per student, teacher, and principal whose data was released, capped by the General Business Law § 899-aa(6)(a) maximum ([N.Y. Educ. Law § 2-d(6)(d)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ny/education-law/edn-sect-2-d/)).
- New York requires each *educational agency* — not the vendor — to designate a Data Protection Officer responsible for implementing the § 2-d policies and serving as the point of contact, and permits an existing employee to hold the role alongside other duties ([8 NYCRR § 121.8](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/8-NYCRR-121.8)).
- Colorado requires every school service contract provider to maintain a comprehensive information security program using appropriate administrative, technological, and physical safeguards ([C.R.S. § 22-16-110](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_22-16-110)).
- Colorado requires a provider to destroy a student's personally identifiable information as soon as practicable on the education entity's request, to destroy all such information after the contract ends within the contractual period or, absent one, when it is no longer needed, and to notify the education entity of the destruction date ([C.R.S. § 22-16-110](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_22-16-110)).
- Colorado gives parents the right to request a copy of their child's personally identifiable information "including student personally identifiable information maintained by a school service contract provider" ([C.R.S. § 22-16-112](https://www.cde.state.co.us/dataprivacyandsecurity/crs22-16-101)).
- New York State Technology Law § 106-b imposed a moratorium on biometric identifying technology in public and nonpublic elementary and secondary schools, including charter schools, pending a report and a Commissioner's determination ([NYS ITS report, August 2023](https://its.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2023/08/biometrics-report-final-2023.pdf)).
- On 27 September 2023 the Commissioner of Education ordered that "no school in the State of New York shall purchase or utilize facial recognition technology unless and until issuance of a superseding order," while authorising schools to decide locally on other biometric technology after considering privacy implications, civil-rights impact, effectiveness, and parental input ([NYSED determination, 27 September 2023](https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/programs/data-privacy-security/biometric-determination-9-27-23.pdf); [NYSED announcement](https://www.nysed.gov/news/2023/state-education-department-issues-determination-biometric-identifying-technology-schools)).
- The determination reached that conclusion after an Office of Information Technology Services report acknowledged that the risks of facial recognition in an educational setting may outweigh the benefits ([AP News, 27 September 2023](https://apnews.com/article/facial-recognition-banned-new-york-schools-ddd35e004254d316beabf70453b1a6a2)).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** The right way to read a hundred-plus statutes is by category rather than by name: they impose vendor-conduct prohibitions, contract-paper requirements, security-programme duties, breach deadlines, deletion duties, and parent inspect-and-correct rights, and the deadlines are the part that actually constrains operations — 7 days contractor-to-agency in New York, 30 days in Illinois, "as soon as practicable" on request in Colorado. Almost none of them create a private right of action; California's is public-enforcement only and Illinois routes enforcement through the Attorney General under the Consumer Fraud Act, though Illinois left the private-damages question unaddressed in a way that other Illinois statutes have been careful to foreclose. The scope trigger matters more than the substance: these statutes attach to services designed, marketed, or contracted for K–12 school purposes, so a direct-to-family assessment that never enters a district contract may fall outside them entirely and be governed instead by COPPA plus general consumer-privacy law — a reading of the statutory text, not a proposition anyone has litigated here. The sharpest state constraint located is not a data-privacy law at all: New York's Commissioner of Education has ordered that no school in the state, public or nonpublic, may purchase or use facial recognition technology, which forecloses the sale rather than regulating it.

## 4. State biometric statutes, and whether detecting a face is the same as recognising one

**Source:** Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act, 740 ILCS 14; *Rosenbach v. Six Flags*, 2019 IL 123186; *Rivera v. Google*, 238 F. Supp. 3d 1088 (N.D. Ill. 2017); *Zellmer v. Meta Platforms*, No. 22-16925 (9th Cir. 2024); *G.T. v. Samsung Electronics America* (N.D. Ill. 2024); *Patterson v. Respondus* (N.D. Ill. 2022); Texas Bus. & Com. Code § 503.001; RCW ch. 19.375.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- BIPA defines "biometric identifier" as "a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry," and expressly excludes writing samples, written signatures, photographs, demographic data, and physical descriptions such as height, weight, hair colour, or eye colour ([740 ILCS 14/10](https://codes.findlaw.com/il/chapter-740-civil-liabilities/il-st-sect-740-14-10/)).
- "Biometric information" is any information, however captured or stored, "based on an individual's biometric identifier used to identify an individual," and excludes information derived from the excluded items ([740 ILCS 14/10](https://codes.findlaw.com/il/chapter-740-civil-liabilities/il-st-sect-740-14-10/)).
- BIPA creates a private right of action for "any person aggrieved," with liquidated damages of $1,000 for a negligent violation or $5,000 for an intentional or reckless violation — or actual damages, whichever is greater — plus reasonable attorneys' fees, costs, expert fees, and other relief including an injunction ([740 ILCS 14/20](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/103/SB/10300SB2979.htm); [King & Spalding summary](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/illinois-bipa-reform-takes-effect)).
- The Illinois Supreme Court held that a plaintiff need not "plead and prove that they sustained some actual injury or damage beyond infringement of the rights afforded them" under BIPA in order to sue *(Rosenbach v. Six Flags Entertainment Corp., 2019 IL 123186.)* ([Illinois Supreme Court opinion](https://cases.justia.com/illinois/supreme-court/2019-123186.pdf?ts=1548434520))
- The plaintiff in *Rosenbach* was a mother suing over the collection of her 14-year-old son's fingerprint for a season pass, so the leading BIPA standing case is itself a minor's case ([Quinn Emanuel summary](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/publications/article-june-2019-the-rise-of-biometrics-laws-and-litigation/)).
- BIPA § 15(b) requires, before collection, written notice that a biometric identifier is being collected, written notice of the specific purpose and length of term, and "a written release executed by the subject … or the subject's legally authorized representative" ([740 ILCS 14/15(b)](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True)).
- The statute is silent on a minor's capacity to execute that release; it authorises either the subject or the legally authorised representative, and complaints involving children plead the parent or guardian as the representative *(e.g., Farwell v. Google LLC, class complaint, ¶ 66.)* ([complaint PDF](https://www.classaction.org/media/farwell-v-google-llc.pdf)) `[statutory text plus pleading practice; no located decision resolves whether a minor's own signature suffices]`
- BIPA § 15(a) requires a publicly available written policy establishing a retention schedule and destruction guidelines, with destruction when the initial purpose is satisfied or within three years of the individual's last interaction, whichever occurs first ([740 ILCS 14/15(a)](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True)).
- Senate Bill 2979, effective 2 August 2024, provides that repeated collection or disclosure of the same biometric identifier from the same person by the same method is a single violation for which the aggrieved person is entitled to at most one recovery, and confirms that a "written release" may be an electronic signature ([SB 2979 text](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/103/SB/10300SB2979.htm); [Greenberg Traurig analysis](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/8/bipa-update-illinois-limits-liability-and-clarifies-electronic-consent-for-biometric-data-collection)).
- In an April 2026 decision the Seventh Circuit held that the SB 2979 damages amendment applies retroactively to cases pending when it took effect, because it is a remedial change that does not alter BIPA's substantive standards of liability ([ABA Business Law Today, May 2026](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-may/7th-circuit-holds-bipa-damages-remedy-applies-retroactively/)).
- A federal court held that the photograph exclusion does not exempt a face-geometry scan derived from a photograph, reasoning that "nothing in the statute says … *how* the biometric measurements must be obtained" and that a biometric identifier is "a set of measurements of a specified physical component … used to identify a person" *(Rivera v. Google Inc., 238 F. Supp. 3d 1088 (N.D. Ill. 2017).)* ([opinion PDF](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.ilnd.323329/gov.uscourts.ilnd.323329.60.0.pdf))
- The same court noted that "if Google simply captured and stored the photographs and did not measure and generate scans of face geometry, then there would be no violation of the Act" ([Rivera opinion PDF](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.ilnd.323329/gov.uscourts.ilnd.323329.60.0.pdf)).
- The Ninth Circuit held that "face signatures" — abstract numerical representations that reveal nothing about a face's geometry, cannot be reverse-engineered, and cannot identify anyone — "are not biometric identifiers or information as defined by BIPA" *(Zellmer v. Meta Platforms, Inc., No. 22-16925 (9th Cir. 17 June 2024).)* ([Ninth Circuit opinion PDF](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2024/06/17/22-16925.pdf))
- In that case the panel held that evidence the signatures could be used to predict age and gender did not amount to identification for BIPA purposes ([Perkins Coie analysis](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/biometric-identifiers-must-identify-ninth-circuit-clarifies-scope-bipa)).
- The summary-judgment record in *Zellmer* described the first pipeline step, face detection, as one that "simply determines whether any face appears in a photo" and identifies no one ([defendant's summary-judgment brief, CourtListener](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.324427/gov.uscourts.cand.324427.67.0.pdf)) `[a party's characterisation accepted on that record, not a general technical finding]`.
- A Northern District of Illinois court concluded that "BIPA only covers those 'retina or iris scan[s], fingerprint[s], voiceprint[s], or scan[s] of hand or face geometry' that are capable of identifying an individual," so "the fact that the App performs face scans is not dispositive," and dismissed where the software merely grouped unidentified faces *(G.T. v. Samsung Electronics America, Inc. (N.D. Ill. 2024).)* ([FindLaw case text](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-n-d-ill-eas-div/116406656.html))
- That court expressly described the point as "another issue with law on both sides" before following the line of cases requiring capability of identification ([G.T. v. Samsung](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-n-d-ill-eas-div/116406656.html)).
- In three consolidated BIPA class actions over webcam-based exam monitoring, the court denied the vendor's motions to dismiss in all three cases, holding that the plaintiffs stated § 15(b) and § 15(d) claims, while remanding or dismissing the § 15(a) and § 15(c) claims for lack of Article III standing *(Patterson v. Respondus, Inc., No. 20 C 7692 and consolidated cases (N.D. Ill., 23 March 2022), Judge Rebecca R. Pallmeyer.)* ([opinion, govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ilnd-1_21-cv-02620/pdf/USCOURTS-ilnd-1_21-cv-02620-0.pdf))
- In the same opinion the court declined to enforce the vendor's Washington choice-of-law provision, concluding that BIPA "reflects a fundamental Illinois public policy of protecting individual privacy rights in biometric information" and that enforcing the clause would foreclose the plaintiffs' claims ([Patterson opinion](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ilnd-1_21-cv-02620/pdf/USCOURTS-ilnd-1_21-cv-02620-0.pdf)) `[a ruling on a motion to dismiss, where allegations are assumed true; not a merits finding about what the software collects]`.
- Texas defines "biometric identifier" as "a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or record of hand or face geometry," requires notice and consent before capture for a commercial purpose, requires destruction within a reasonable time and no later than the first anniversary of the date the collection purpose expires, and sets a civil penalty of up to $25,000 per violation enforceable only by the Attorney General ([Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 503.001](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_503.001); [Texas AG, Biometric Identifier Act](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection/file-consumer-complaint/consumer-privacy-rights/biometric-identifier-act)).
- Washington's statute expressly excludes from "biometric identifier" any "physical or digital photograph, video or audio recording or data generated therefrom," and may be enforced solely by the Attorney General under the Consumer Protection Act ([RCW 19.375.010 and 19.375.030](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.375&full=true)).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** The distinction between detection and recognition is doing real legal work, and the current weight of authority puts detection outside BIPA: the Ninth Circuit held that data incapable of identifying anyone is neither a biometric identifier nor biometric information, and a Northern District of Illinois court applied the same rule to dismiss where software merely grouped unidentified faces. But the line is narrower than it sounds. *Rivera* holds that deriving a face-geometry scan from a photograph is covered notwithstanding the photograph exclusion, so the safe harbour is not "we only used the webcam image" — it is "we never produced a representation capable of identifying anyone." Any identity step — matching the examinee's face to a photo ID, or confirming across sessions that this is the same enrolled child — moves the product from the outside of the definition to the inside, and one court has already refused to dismiss BIPA claims arising from exactly that kind of webcam exam monitoring, and refused to let a choice-of-law clause relocate the dispute. Jurisdiction is close to dispositive: Washington excludes video and anything derived from it, Texas gives the Attorney General exclusive enforcement at up to $25,000 per violation, and Illinois alone lets an individual sue for $1,000 or $5,000 in liquidated damages plus fees — now capped, after the August 2024 amendment and an April 2026 Seventh Circuit ruling applying it retroactively, at one recovery per person per collection method.

## 5. Recording a child at home: what the consent regimes actually reach, and what they do not

**Source:** Federal Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510–2523; state all-party-consent statutes; Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652B; *Robbins v. Lower Merion School District* (E.D. Pa. 2010) and contemporaneous reporting; 18 U.S.C. § 1801 and N.Y. Penal Law § 250.45; 18 U.S.C. § 2258A.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- The federal Wiretap Act covers wire, oral, and electronic communications, and every federal circuit to have considered the question has held that video surveillance without an audio component does not violate it, because acquiring an image does not acquire the contents of a communication *(citing United States v. Torres, 751 F.2d 875, 885–86 (7th Cir. 1984), and United States v. Falls, 34 F.3d 674, 679 (8th Cir. 1994).)* ([Villanova working paper survey](https://digitalcommons.law.villanova.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1010&context=wps)) `[a law-review survey's characterisation of circuit authority; the underlying opinions were not read directly]`
- The audio track of a video recording is an oral communication and is governed by the wiretap rules ([Villanova working paper survey](https://digitalcommons.law.villanova.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1010&context=wps)).
- Federal law sets a one-party-consent floor under 18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d), and states may impose stricter rules ([Recording Law, Federal Wiretap Act guide](https://www.recordinglaw.com/federal-wiretap-act-ecpa/)).
- Published guides disagree on how many states require all-party consent: one 2026 guide lists 12, another lists 13 while flagging Delaware, Michigan, and Nevada as arguable either way, and a third lists 10 ([Nimitai guide](https://nimitai.com/blog/one-party-vs-two-party-consent-states); [Copareo guide](https://copareo.com/en/call-recording-laws-by-state); [Recording Law video guide](https://www.recordinglaw.com/is-it-illegal-to-video-record-someone-without-their-consent/)) `[commercial practitioner guides, not primary law; the disagreement is about how to classify disputed states, not about the core group]`.
- California Penal Code § 632 makes it an offence to record a "confidential communication" without the consent of all parties, punishable by a fine not exceeding $2,500 per violation or up to a year's imprisonment, with a separate civil remedy under § 637.2 ([Cal. Penal Code § 632](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/penal-code/pen-sect-632/); [Justia 50-state survey](https://www.justia.com/50-state-surveys/recording-phone-calls-and-conversations/)).
- Section 632 defines a "confidential communication" as one carried on in circumstances reasonably indicating that a party desires it be confined to the parties, excluding circumstances where the parties may reasonably expect it may be overheard or recorded ([Cal. Penal Code § 632(c)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/penal-code/pen-sect-632/)).
- The common-law tort of intrusion upon seclusion, as formulated in Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652B, imposes liability on one who intentionally intrudes on another's solitude, seclusion, or private affairs where the intrusion would be highly offensive to a reasonable person, and its comments extend it to using a device "to oversee or overhear the plaintiff's private affairs" ([Fordham Law Review survey](https://ir.lawnet.fordham.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=5122&context=flr)).
- A Pennsylvania school district was sued in February 2010 after remotely activating webcams on school-issued laptops that students used at home; the case was brought by a 15-year-old who was disciplined at school on the basis of a photograph taken of him in his bedroom *(Robbins v. Lower Merion School District (E.D. Pa. 2010).)* ([Wikipedia case summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbins_v._Lower_Merion_School_District))
- The district's own review found that its technology staff captured at least 56,000 images through the remote-tracking program, which was in some cases left running for months after laptops were located ([Philadelphia Inquirer, 12 October 2010](https://www.inquirer.com/philly/news/year-in-review/20101012_Lower_Merion_district_s_laptop_saga_ends_with__610_000_settlement.html)).
- In October 2010 the district agreed to pay $610,000 to settle two suits — $175,000 in trust for Robbins, $10,000 for a second student, and $425,000 in plaintiffs' attorney fees — and the judge signed a permanent injunction barring the district from tracking laptops without consent from students and their parents ([Philadelphia Inquirer, 13 October 2010](https://www.inquirer.com/philly/news/local/20101013_Student_Blake_Robbins_in_Lower_Merion_webcam_case_signs_settlement.html)) `[settlement, not a merits judgment; the district denied intentional spying]`.
- In April 2010 the US Attorney's Office sought access to photographs of a number of children in that investigation, "some of which may include nude or partially clothed shots, inasmuch as taking nude images of children could be criminal conduct" ([Wikipedia case summary, citing contemporaneous reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbins_v._Lower_Merion_School_District)).
- On 17 August 2010 the US Attorney announced no charges would be filed against district officials, stating: "We have not found evidence that would establish beyond a reasonable doubt that anyone involved had criminal intent" ([Wikipedia case summary, citing contemporaneous reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbins_v._Lower_Merion_School_District)) `[criminal investigation closed without charges; the civil settlement was separate]`.
- The federal video-voyeurism offence at 18 U.S.C. § 1801 applies only within the special maritime and territorial jurisdiction of the United States, so most residential cases fall to state law ([18 U.S.C. § 1801](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=(title:18%20section:1801%20edition:prelim))).
- New York's unlawful-surveillance offence turns on *surreptitious* use of an imaging device without the subject's knowledge or consent, and one subdivision creates a rebuttable presumption of "no legitimate purpose" when a device is used in a bedroom; it is a class E felony ([N.Y. Penal Law § 250.45](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/250.45)).
- A "provider" that obtains actual knowledge of facts or circumstances from which there is an apparent violation of the enumerated federal child-exploitation offences must, "as soon as reasonably possible," report to the NCMEC CyberTipline, and a knowing and wilful failure to report carries statutory fines ([18 U.S.C. § 2258A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258A)).
- The 2023 edition of the US Code set those fines at not more than $150,000 for an initial failure and $300,000 for a subsequent one; the current preliminary text reflects 2024 amendments introducing higher tiered figures keyed to a provider's monthly active users ([2023 US Code text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title18/html/USCODE-2023-title18-partI-chap110-sec2258A.htm); [current preliminary text](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A18+section%3A2258a+edition%3Aprelim%29)).
- Whether an at-home proctoring service is a "provider" within the meaning of that section — which turns on the electronic-communication-service and remote-computing-service definitions — was not resolved by any authority located here *(negative finding from this search pass.)* `[open question; the duty is significant if the answer is yes]`
- Six US senators wrote to three proctoring vendors in December 2020 raising equity, accessibility, and privacy concerns, noting that students must "install intrusive software and provide extensive personal information – such as images of their home, photos of their identification, and personal information regarding their disabilities" ([Senator Blumenthal press release, 3 December 2020](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/newsroom/press/release/blumenthal-leads-call-for-virtual-exam-software-companies-to-improve-equity-accessibility-and-privacy-for-students-amid-troubling-reports); [letter text](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/2020.12.3%20Letter%20to%20Ed%20Testing%20Software%20Companies%20ExamSoft.pdf)) `[congressional correspondence, not law or enforcement]`.
- EPIC filed a complaint with the District of Columbia Attorney General on 9 December 2020 against five online-proctoring firms, alleging excessive collection of personal information and use of secret algorithms as unfair and deceptive trade practices ([EPIC complaint](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/privacy/dccppa/online-test-proctoring/EPIC-complaint-in-re-online-test-proctoring-companies-12-09-20.pdf)) `[advocacy complaint; no resulting enforcement action was located]`.

**DOK 2 — Summary:** The recording-consent regimes are narrower than the phrase "two-party consent state" suggests, and they attach to the wrong half of the problem: they govern audio, not silent video, and they turn on surreptitiousness, so a camera the family is told about and agrees to is unlikely to trip either the wiretap statutes or the video-voyeurism offences. The real exposure sits in three other places. The tort of intrusion upon seclusion has no consent-state map and no fixed elements beyond intentional intrusion on seclusion that a reasonable person would find highly offensive. The only well-documented American case of webcams capturing children in their bedrooms is *Robbins*, and it is worth reading precisely: the district settled for $610,000 and accepted a permanent injunction, a federal prosecutor investigated and closed the matter without charges for want of criminal intent, and the prosecutor's own filings show that the possibility of inadvertently capturing undressed children was treated as a serious criminal question rather than a hypothetical. And there is a federal reporting duty that attaches to "providers" who obtain actual knowledge of apparent child sexual abuse material — whether a proctoring operator is such a provider is unresolved, but if it is, storing video of children in their homes creates a review, escalation, and reporting obligation with a statutory deadline.

## 6. When the consenting adult is also the risk

**Source:** 16 C.F.R. §§ 312.5–312.6; 740 ILCS 14/15(b); *United States v. Edmodo, LLC* (N.D. Cal. 2023); legal scholarship on parental consent in children's privacy law.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- Every enumerated method of verifiable parental consent in the COPPA Rule is designed to answer one question — is this person the child's parent — and the Rule's standard is that the method be "reasonably calculated, in light of available technology, to ensure that the person providing consent is the child's parent" ([16 C.F.R. § 312.5(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.5)).
- Nothing in the Rule conditions the validity of parental consent on the parent acting in the child's interest, and no located provision creates any mechanism for the child's separate interest to be represented *(negative finding from a reading of 16 C.F.R. Part 312.)*
- The Rule gives the parent the right, at any time, to refuse further use or collection of the child's personal information and to direct the operator to delete it, subject only to the operator verifying that the requester is the parent ([16 C.F.R. § 312.6(a)(2)–(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.6)).
- The operator is shielded from liability under any federal or state law for a disclosure made in good faith following reasonable procedures in responding to such a parental request ([16 C.F.R. § 312.6(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.6)).
- BIPA likewise directs the written release to "the subject … or the subject's legally authorized representative," without qualification as to that representative's interests ([740 ILCS 14/15(b)](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True)).
- The FTC has held that an operator may not rely on a school as an intermediary to obtain verifiable parental consent, and may rely on school authorisation only under a written agreement containing five specified terms including a school representative's acknowledgement of authority *(United States v. Edmodo, LLC, No. 3:23-cv-02495 (N.D. Cal.), stipulated order entered 28 August 2023.)* ([FTC case page](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/202-3129-edmodo-llc-us-v); [Covington summary](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2023/05/ftc-announces-coppa-settlement-against-ed-tech-provider-including-strict-data-minimization-and-data-retention-requirements/))
- The FTC characterised that as "the first time the FTC has alleged an unfair trade practice in the context of an operator's interaction with schools" ([FTC press release, 22 May 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ftc-says-ed-tech-provider-edmodo-unlawfully-used-childrens-personal-information-advertising)).
- A *Texas Law Review* piece argues that reliance on parental consent "is ill-suited as a matter of doctrine and policy and is traditionally disfavored under common law," and that in the era of ed tech and AI-enabled tools parental consent "can no longer meaningfully serve its traditional purpose of protecting the best interests of the child" ([Texas Law Review](https://texaslawreview.org/childrens-digital-privacy-and-the-case-against-parental-consent/)) `[legal scholarship, not law]`.
- A *Virginia Law Review* article argues that the parental-control model "is erected in the name of children, but it mostly ignores their expressed privacy interests," risks harm to vulnerable children, and overburdens caregivers ([Virginia Law Review, "Rethinking Youth Privacy"](https://virginialawreview.org/articles/rethinking-youth-privacy/)) `[legal scholarship, not law]`.
- Scholarship on parental online disclosure notes that "[c]ourts have shown reluctance to grant children privacy rights in the context of family life," and that even where a child's reasonable expectation of privacy is recognised, courts often place higher value on the parent's interests ([University of Florida faculty scholarship](https://scholarship.law.ufl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1796&context=facultypub&httpsredir=1)) `[legal scholarship characterising case law, not a holding]`.
- The one US doctrinal device commentary points to for vindicating a child's interest against a parent's decision is the judicial-bypass mechanism recognised in *Bellotti v. Baird*, which is a reproductive-rights doctrine and is invoked here only as an analogy ([University of Florida faculty scholarship](https://scholarship.law.ufl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1796&context=facultypub&httpsredir=1)) `[analogy drawn by commentary; not authority applicable to assessment proctoring]`.
- No US statute, regulation, agency guidance, or decided case was located that addresses the specific conflict in which the parent who provides consent for a child's remotely proctored assessment is also the adult supervising the session and the most plausible source of improper assistance *(negative finding from this search pass, across children's-privacy, education-privacy, biometric, and test-security sources.)* `[absence of located authority, not evidence that no such authority exists]`

**DOK 2 — Summary:** The honest answer to whether the law has addressed this conflict is no. Children's privacy law is built entirely around verifying *identity* — that the consenting person is really the parent — and contains no test of whether the consent serves the child. The conflict is in fact sharper than it first appears, because the same parent who may have helped the child during the session holds a statutory right to demand deletion of the record of it, and the operator that complies in good faith is expressly immunised for doing so. The closest thing to a regulator engaging with a misaligned consent-giver is the Edmodo order, and it addresses a different misalignment — an operator leaning on schools to do its consent work — by requiring a written agreement and an explicit acknowledgement of authority, which is a paperwork answer to an authority problem rather than an interest-alignment problem. Substantial legal scholarship argues the parental-consent model is structurally unfit for exactly this reason, but that is argument, not law, and the only mechanism anyone points to for representing a child's interest against a parent's decision comes from an unrelated doctrine.

## 7. Retention and deletion of children's video and biometric data

**Source:** 16 C.F.R. §§ 312.8 and 312.10; 740 ILCS 14/15(a); Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 503.001(c)(3); C.R.S. § 22-16-110; the Edmodo stipulated order.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- An operator may retain a child's personal information "only as long as is reasonably necessary to fulfill the specific purpose(s) for which the information was collected," must delete it using reasonable measures when it is no longer necessary, and may not retain it indefinitely ([16 C.F.R. § 312.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.10)).
- The operator must establish, implement, and maintain a written data retention policy setting out the purposes of collection, the business need for retention, and a timeframe for deletion, and must publish that policy in its online notice ([16 C.F.R. § 312.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.10)).
- The FTC omitted from the final rule the proposed language barring retention "for any secondary purpose," explaining that it was unnecessary and had confused commenters ([Latham & Watkins summary](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)).
- Gibson Dunn notes there was disagreement within the Commission about the retention requirement, including as to what "indefinitely" means ([Gibson Dunn analysis](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-updates-to-coppa-rule-impose-new-compliance-obligations-for-online-services-that-collect-data-from-children/)) `[commentary on an internal Commission disagreement]`.
- The operator must maintain a written information security program with safeguards appropriate to the sensitivity of children's data and the operator's size and complexity, and must designate one or more employees to coordinate it ([16 C.F.R. § 312.8(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8)).
- That program must include at least annual risk assessments, safeguards designed against the identified risks, regular testing and monitoring, and at least annual evaluation and modification ([16 C.F.R. § 312.8(b)(2)–(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8)).
- Before allowing service providers or third parties to collect or maintain children's personal information, the operator must take reasonable steps to determine they are capable of maintaining confidentiality, security, and integrity, and must obtain written assurances to that effect ([16 C.F.R. § 312.8(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8)).
- BIPA sets an outer bound of destruction on purpose-satisfaction or three years from last interaction, whichever occurs first, and requires the entity to comply with its own published schedule absent a warrant or subpoena ([740 ILCS 14/15(a)](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True)).
- Texas sets a shorter outer bound: destruction within a reasonable time and no later than the first anniversary of the date the collection purpose expires ([Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 503.001(c)(3)](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_503.001)).
- Colorado requires the provider to notify the public education entity of the date on which all student personally identifiable information was destroyed ([C.R.S. § 22-16-110](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_22-16-110)).
- The Edmodo order requires the company to implement and adhere to a data retention schedule for children's personal information and to delete models and algorithms developed using that information ([Hunton summary of the proposed order](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-proposed-order-against-ed-tech-provider-edmodo)).
- The FTC's Edmodo complaint alleged, among other things, "retaining children's personal information for longer than reasonably necessary to fulfill the purpose for which the information was collected," and the $6 million penalty was suspended based on inability to pay ([Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-proposed-order-against-ed-tech-provider-edmodo)).
- The Ninth Circuit in *Zellmer* affirmed dismissal of the BIPA retention-policy claim for lack of Article III standing, holding the plaintiff never explained how he was harmed by that general duty in a concrete and particularised way ([Zellmer opinion](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2024/06/17/22-16925.pdf)).
- The Seventh Circuit has similarly treated the duty to publish a retention policy as owed to the public generally rather than to an individual plaintiff *(Bryant v. Compass Group USA, Inc., 958 F.3d 617 (7th Cir. 2020); Thornley v. Clearview AI, Inc., 984 F.3d 1241 (7th Cir. 2021).)* ([Quarles summary](https://www.quarles.com/newsroom/publications/biometric-information-privacy-update-new-illinois-appellate-ruling-expands-temporal-recovery-period-and-suggests-enhanced-damage-remedy-available)) `[a standing holding in federal court; it does not eliminate the duty, and state courts may hear the claim]`

**DOK 2 — Summary:** Collecting a child's video or biometric data is a commitment to a published, dated, enforceable deletion schedule and to actually meeting it — the retention policy must exist in writing, must appear in the public notice, and must name a deletion timeframe. The outer bounds differ by regime and the shortest one governs in practice: three years from last interaction under BIPA, one year after the purpose expires in Texas, "no longer reasonably necessary" with no indefinite retention under COPPA, and a notified destruction date to the contracting school in Colorado. The Edmodo order is the concrete precedent for how far deletion reaches, because it required deleting not only the data but the models and algorithms built from it — meaning derived artefacts, not just the original video, are within the commitment. One consolation is procedural: federal courts have repeatedly held that the retention-policy duty is owed to the public generally, so a plaintiff who alleges only a missing policy may lack Article III standing — but that is a forum limitation, not an exemption, and the duty remains fully enforceable by regulators and in state court.

## 8. What each obligation actually requires of a small administrative team

**Source:** The operative provisions cited in sections 1–7, read for the artefact, role, deadline, or recurring process each one demands.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- COPPA requires a *named person*: the operator must "[d]esignate one or more employees to coordinate the operator's information security program" ([16 C.F.R. § 312.8(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8)).
- COPPA requires a *recurring calendar commitment*: annual risk assessments, regular testing and monitoring of safeguards, and at least annual evaluation and modification of the programme ([16 C.F.R. § 312.8(b)(2)–(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8)).
- COPPA requires *two separate published documents*: the online notice of information practices and, within it, the written data retention policy naming purposes, business need, and deletion timeframe ([16 C.F.R. § 312.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.10)).
- COPPA requires a *live intake channel*: a means for a parent to review the information collected, refuse further use, and direct deletion, which must verify the requester is the parent and must not be unduly burdensome to them ([16 C.F.R. § 312.6(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.6)).
- COPPA requires a *vendor due-diligence step before onboarding any subprocessor*: reasonable steps to determine capability plus written assurances ([16 C.F.R. § 312.8(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8)).
- COPPA's consent methods each carry their own operational cost — a toll-free number or video-conference route requires "trained personnel," and the facial-recognition route requires trained personnel to confirm the match and prompt deletion of both the ID and the image ([16 C.F.R. § 312.5(b)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.5)).
- BIPA requires a *publicly available written policy* with a retention schedule and destruction guidelines, and compliance with that schedule absent a warrant or subpoena ([740 ILCS 14/15(a)](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True)).
- Breach deadlines are short and differ by state: 7 calendar days from discovery for a third-party contractor to notify a New York educational agency, 14 calendar days for that agency to notify parents, and 30 calendar days for an Illinois operator to notify the school and for the school to notify parents ([8 NYCRR Part 121](https://www.counsel.nysed.gov/rules/indices-fulltext/2019/010); [105 ILCS 85](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3806&ChapterID=17&Print=True)).
- Contract paper is per-jurisdiction and recurring: New York requires the parents' bill of rights in *every* contract with a third-party contractor receiving student data, Illinois requires schools to post each agreement within 10 business days, and Colorado requires the provider to notify the education entity of the destruction date ([N.Y. Educ. Law § 2-d(3)](https://codes.findlaw.com/ny/education-law/edn-sect-2-d/); [105 ILCS 85](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3806&ChapterID=17&Print=True); [C.R.S. § 22-16-110](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_22-16-110)).
- Parent access requests carry their own turnaround clocks: Illinois requires correction of a factual inaccuracy within 90 days where the school maintains the information ([Klein Thorpe & Jenkins summary of SOPPA](https://www.ktjlaw.com/blog/the-student-online-personal-protection-act-soppa/)).
- FERPA's school-official route requires the *school* to have named the criteria in its annual notification and to retain direct control, which means the vendor's compliance depends on a document the vendor does not write ([34 C.F.R. § 99.31(a)(1)(i)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/99.31); [ED guidance](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/FERPA%20Guidance%20for%20School%20Officials%20on%20Student%20Health%20Records.pdf)).
- New York's Data Protection Officer duty sits on the educational agency rather than the vendor, and the state expressly permits an existing employee to hold the role alongside other responsibilities ([8 NYCRR § 121.8](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/8-NYCRR-121.8)).
- The Edmodo order shows what a regulator will impose after the fact on an operator of any size: a written agreement with each school containing five specified terms, a prohibition on relying on schools for parental consent, an adhered-to retention schedule, and deletion of models and algorithms built from the data ([Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-proposed-order-against-ed-tech-provider-edmodo); [FTC stipulated order motion](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023129edmodojointmotionorder.pdf)).
- The Edmodo penalty of $6 million was suspended based on inability to pay, which indicates that the FTC will pursue an operator that cannot pay and will impose ongoing conduct obligations regardless ([Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-proposed-order-against-ed-tech-provider-edmodo)).
- No located obligation in any of these regimes scales down for a small team; the COPPA security-programme standard is the only one that expressly calibrates to "the operator's size, complexity, and nature and scope of activities," and it calibrates the *safeguards*, not the requirement to have a designated employee, annual assessments, testing, and annual review ([16 C.F.R. § 312.8(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8)).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** Read as an operations list rather than a legal one, the obligations resolve into a small number of recurring commitments: one named person who owns security, an annual assessment-and-testing cycle, two published documents that must stay accurate, a verified parent-request channel with response turnarounds, a per-subprocessor due-diligence file, a destruction schedule that is actually executed and in Colorado's case reported, and a breach clock that starts at 7 days in the strictest state. The multiplier is jurisdiction: a single assessment offered nationally can be subject to fifty breach regimes, a hundred-plus student-privacy statutes with differing scope triggers, one biometric statute with a private right of action, and a facial-recognition prohibition covering every school in New York. The only provision that acknowledges organisational size is COPPA's security standard, and it scales the safeguards rather than the structure — the designated employee, the annual cycle, and the written programme are required at any size. The Edmodo record is the clearest signal of how this lands on a small operator: the company had suspended US operations and could not pay, and the order still bound it to ongoing conduct obligations if it ever resumed.

## Candidate tensions

- **Being younger may buy the child fewer remedies, not more:** one federal district court held COPPA preempted the under-13 plaintiff's state biometric claim while her older co-plaintiffs' claims survived, and the Ninth Circuit — with the FTC as amicus — held that COPPA does not preempt parallel state law, so the same cohort's youngest and oldest members have different private remedies depending on the forum.
- **The strongest federal children's statute has no private enforcer and the strongest state biometric statute has nothing else:** COPPA carries per-violation penalties up to $53,088 but only regulators can bring it, while BIPA offers $1,000 or $5,000 in liquidated damages to any aggrieved person with no proof of injury — so the practical litigation risk is concentrated in one state and turns on a definitional question COPPA does not ask.
- **Face detection is probably outside biometric law and is exactly what an at-home child proctoring product needs:** *Zellmer* and *G.T. v. Samsung* put non-identifying face data outside BIPA, but *Rivera* keeps any identity-capable derived representation inside — so the same webcam feed is regulated or unregulated depending on a design choice that has no bearing on how intrusive the family experiences it as being.
- **The courts themselves say the detection-versus-recognition line is unsettled:** the *G.T.* court described it as "another issue with law on both sides" before choosing a side, which means a product built on the current weight of authority is built on a live question.
- **Two courts reached opposite results on the same defensive move in proctoring cases:** one enforced a choice-of-law clause and disposed of the BIPA claims, and another refused to enforce one because BIPA reflects a fundamental Illinois public policy — so whether a vendor can contract out of the only statute with a private right of action is genuinely open.
- **FERPA is either the governing framework or entirely inapplicable, and which one depends on the school's funding rather than on anything about the child:** the Department of Education says private and parochial K–12 schools generally fall outside it, so two children taking the same assessment can be covered by different regimes because of where they are enrolled.
- **The federal education-privacy statutes are the ones everyone names and the ones least likely to bite:** FERPA has no private right of action after *Gonzaga*, PPRA is keyed to eight content categories that may not include a cognitive-ability test, and the Department has signalled it will rewrite the third-party disclosure rules — while the state statutes nobody names carry the actual deadlines.
- **The consent-based defences to recording law protect the operator against precisely the wrong risk:** wiretap statutes govern audio, video-voyeurism statutes require surreptitiousness, and disclosed consented recording clears both — leaving the intrusion tort, which has no consent-state map, and the possibility of inadvertent capture, which consent does not cure.
- **The only documented American case of school webcams photographing children in bedrooms ended without criminal charges and with a $610,000 settlement:** the civil exposure was real and the criminal exposure was investigated and closed for want of intent, which cuts both ways depending on which half is quoted.
- **A federal duty may attach not to what the operator collects but to what it inadvertently sees:** if a proctoring service is a "provider" under 18 U.S.C. § 2258A, storing video of children at home creates a reporting obligation with a statutory deadline and fines — and whether it is a provider is unresolved, which is itself the exposure.
- **The parent is simultaneously the consent-giver, the supervisor, the device provider, the most plausible source of improper assistance, and the person entitled to demand deletion of the recording** — and the operator that deletes in good faith is expressly immunised, so the law's only response to the conflict is to protect the operator from the consequences of resolving it in the parent's favour.
- **Children's privacy law verifies who the parent is and never asks what the parent wants:** the substantial scholarship arguing this is the model's central defect is argument rather than law, and the only vindication mechanism commentators point to comes from an unrelated constitutional doctrine.
- **New York forecloses the sale rather than regulating it:** the Commissioner's order bars every school in the state, public and nonpublic, from purchasing or using facial recognition technology, which means the most protective jurisdiction on this question resolved it by prohibition rather than by consent, notice, or retention rules.
- **Retention duties are enforceable but the retention-policy claim is often unlitigable in federal court:** *Zellmer*, *Bryant*, and *Thornley* treat the publication duty as owed to the public generally, so the duty is real, the regulator can enforce it, and the private plaintiff may be sent back to state court rather than turned away.
- **Every obligation scales with jurisdictions served and almost none scales with team size:** COPPA's security standard is the one provision that calibrates to "the operator's size," and it calibrates the safeguards while still requiring a designated employee, annual risk assessment, ongoing testing, and annual review.

## Practical exposure summary

This section is written in plain English, and it is ordered by how much each item actually costs a small team to carry — not by how serious it sounds.

**The heaviest single burden is deletion you can prove.** Everything else is a document you write once and revise occasionally. Deletion is a promise you have to keep continuously, on a schedule, across every place the data ended up — the primary store, the backups, the logs, the analytics, the third-party services, and, per the Edmodo order, any model or algorithm trained on it. You will be committing to an outer bound as short as one year in Texas and three years in Illinois, to a written schedule published where anyone can read it and compare it against your behaviour, and in Colorado to telling the school the date destruction actually happened. Video is the worst category for this because it is bulky, it gets copied, and it is hard to prove you got all of it. If there is one thing to think hard about before collecting, it is this one, because it is the only obligation that never stops.

**Second is running a live parent-facing operation.** A parent can, at any time, ask what you hold on their child, refuse further collection, and demand deletion — and you have to verify that the requester really is the parent while not making it burdensome for them. Separately, breach clocks start at seven calendar days in New York. Parent correction requests run on ninety days in Illinois. None of these are hard individually; all of them require a person who is reachable, who recognises a request when it arrives, and who can act inside a deadline. A small team either designates that person or discovers on day one of an incident that nobody owns it.

**Third is the consent gate at the front door.** Because a K–8 cohort straddles thirteen, you need a neutral age question before you collect anything at all, and for anyone under thirteen you need verifiable parental consent using a method from a closed list — several of which require trained staff on a phone or a video call, or trained staff confirming an ID-to-face match and then deleting both immediately. This is a real product build and a real staffing question, not a checkbox, and it must run before the first byte is collected.

**Fourth is the standing security programme.** One named employee coordinating it, a written programme, an annual risk assessment, regular testing, an annual review, and written assurances from every subprocessor before you let them touch children's data. The safeguards can be proportionate to your size; the structure cannot.

**Fifth is contract and notice paperwork, which is high-volume but low-difficulty.** A published privacy notice with the retention policy inside it. A public biometric retention policy if you touch anything identity-capable. New York's parents' bill of rights inside every school contract. Whatever the school's own FERPA annual notification says about who counts as a school official — a document you depend on and do not control. This is tedious and it multiplies with every jurisdiction, but it is bounded work.

**Two things you should size before you commit rather than after.** One: if the product ever converts a face into something capable of identifying a person, you have opted into the one US statute where an individual can sue you for liquidated damages plus attorneys' fees without showing any injury, and a court has already refused to let a proctoring vendor use a choice-of-law clause to escape it. Detection that never identifies is, on current authority, outside that statute — but the courts deciding it say the question has law on both sides. Two: if you store video of children in their homes and a court later concludes you are a "provider" under the federal child-exploitation reporting statute, you have acquired a review-and-report duty with a statutory deadline. Nobody has answered that question for proctoring, and an unanswered question about a criminal-adjacent reporting duty is a worse position to be in than a settled bad answer.

**And one thing the law will not help with at all.** The parent who consents is the parent who supervises, provides the device, and is the likeliest source of improper help — and then holds the right to demand you delete the recording of the session. There is no statute, no regulation, no agency guidance, and no decided case addressing that conflict. Scholars have written at length about why the parental-consent model cannot handle it. Whatever you do about it will be a design decision and a contract term, not a legal requirement, and you will not be able to point to anyone who told you to do it.

**Finally, jurisdiction decides more than anything else here.** Where the operator sits, where the child sits, where the school sits, and where the data sits can all be different places, and the answer changes with each: Washington excludes video from its biometric definition entirely, Illinois lets an individual sue, Texas gives only the Attorney General a $25,000-per-violation hammer, New York bars every school in the state from using facial recognition at all, and the federal education statutes may not reach a private school in any of them. A small team cannot realistically carry fifty variants. The practical question is not "how do we comply everywhere" but "how few jurisdictions and how little data can this run on" — because every obligation above is priced per jurisdiction and per data category, and the two levers a small team actually controls are which states it serves and what it decides not to collect.
