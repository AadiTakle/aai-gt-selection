## Sources

1. **Beck, J. E. & Chang, K.-m. (2007). "Identifiability: A Fundamental Problem of Student Modeling."** *User Modeling 2007 (UM 2007)*, LNCS 4511, pp. 137–146. Springer. <https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~listen/pdfs/UM2007_dirichlet.pdf> — **[FULL]** Establishes that a latent-knowledge model's observable performance curve is a many-to-one image of its parameter space: an infinite family of parameter sets produce numerically identical performance predictions while making wildly different claims about knowledge and learning. Concludes that more performance data cannot fix this and that prior beliefs must be injected.

2. **Doroudi, S. & Brunskill, E. (2017). "The Misidentified Identifiability Problem of Bayesian Knowledge Tracing."** *Proc. 10th Int. Conf. on Educational Data Mining (EDM 2017)*, pp. 143–149. <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED596611.pdf> — **[FULL]** Shows Beck & Chang's phenomenon is *not* unidentifiability; BKT is identifiable from the joint distribution of three sequential observations under mild non-degeneracy conditions. Renames the real problem *semantic model degeneracy*, and demonstrates that fitting a misspecified model produces degenerate parameters whose *form depends on sequence length*.

3. **van de Sande, B. (2013). "Properties of the Bayesian Knowledge Tracing Model."** *Journal of Educational Data Mining* 5(2), 1–10. <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1115329.pdf> — **[FULL]** Solves the BKT HMM analytically: `P(C_j) = 1 − P(S) − A·e^(−βj)` with `A = (1 − P(S) − P(G))·(1 − P(L0))` and `β = −log(1 − P(T))`. Derives the admissible-parameter constraints, including that the maximum sensible learning rate shrinks as the guess rate rises.

## Findings

### A. What the identifiability literature actually says

**F1 — The original claim: performance data underdetermine the latent parameters, and more data does not help.** [S1] Beck & Chang exhibit three BKT parameter sets — `(L0=0.56, T=0.1, G=0.00, S=0.05)`, `(0.36, 0.1, 0.30, 0.05)`, `(0.01, 0.1, 0.53, 0.05)` — that produce performance curves "equivalent under finite precision arithmetic" while implying completely different knowledge trajectories. Their closing claim is the strong one: *"acquiring additional training data is not a solution to this problem. Even with an order of magnitude more data, there are still many sets of parameters that will fit the student data equally well ... performance data are not enough."* They also note they "suspect identifiability affects not just knowledge tracing but any modeling approach that acknowledges student performance is a noisy reflection of his knowledge."

**F2 — The algebra of the confound: a chance floor and a low starting level are the same parameter.** [S3] van de Sande solved the BKT HMM in closed form:

> `P(C_j) = 1 − P(S) − A·e^(−βj)`, where `A = (1 − P(S) − P(G))·(1 − P(L0))` and `β = −log(1 − P(T))`.

The guess rate and the initial knowledge level enter the observable curve **only through their product `A`**. Every `(G, L0)` pair on a hyperbola is observationally identical. Beck & Chang's three models are three points on one such curve. Crucially for us, **the growth-rate factor `β` is algebraically separate from `A`** — the rate is *not* confounded with the guess/level pair in the marginal curve, *provided the exponential functional form is correct*. van de Sande also states plainly that "it is reasonable for the HMM form of the model to find negative learning when fitting to student data": a negative rate is a legitimate output of an honest estimator, not a bug.

**F3 — The corrective: BKT is identifiable; the diagnosis was wrong.** [S2] Doroudi & Brunskill show Beck & Chang conflated "same *marginal* distributions P(C_1), P(C_2), … P(C_n)" with "same *joint* distribution." Conditioning on even one prior observation splits the three models apart immediately: "except for P(C_1), the models never agree on the probability that a student would answer the step correctly." Invoking Anandkumar et al.'s HMM identifiability result, they establish that BKT is identifiable from `P(C_1)`, `P(C_1,C_2)`, `P(C_1,C_2,C_3)` — i.e. **more than two observations per student suffices for a unique global likelihood maximum** — under non-degeneracy conditions `P(L0) ∉ {0,1}`, `P(T) ≠ 1`, `P(G) ≠ 1 − P(S)`. Empirical wide spreads of fitted parameters therefore "cannot be due to the presence of multiple global maxima ... and hence must be due to multiple local optima."

**F4 — The finding that most directly predicts a contamination floor: misspecification bias whose *form depends on sequence length*.** [S2, §4.2] Doroudi & Brunskill generated data from an **AFM — our functional family** — specifically `P(C_i) = 1/(1 + exp(−θ + 2 − 0.1i))` with `θ ~ N(0,1)`, then fit BKT to it:

| fitted BKT parameter | 20 opportunities/student | 200 opportunities/student |
|---|---|---|
| P(L0) | 0.09 | 0.001 |
| P(T) | 0.05 | 0.05 |
| P(G) | 0.14 | 0.28 |
| P(S) | 0.46 | 0.03 |

Same true generating model, same true rate, only the sequence length changed — and the misfit migrated from the slip parameter to the guess parameter. Their diagnosis is explicit and is the sentence to carry forward: *"not only can alternative models of student learning lead to fitting (near) degenerate parameters, but varying the number of observations can lead to different forms of degeneracy! This is a counterintuitive phenomenon that we believe is not the result of not having enough data (students) to fit the models well, but rather the result of the mismatch between the true form of student learning and the model we are using [to] fit student learning."* They add: fitting a 2-state BKT to a 10-state graded-mastery HMM yields a `P(T)` **smaller than every true transition probability in the generating model** — "while the learned BKT model makes it appear as though learning happens very slowly, according to the true student model, learning actually occurs much more often but in more progressive increments."

**F5 — A guess floor mechanically narrows the admissible rate band.** [S3] van de Sande's constraints for the knowledge-tracing *algorithm* to behave sensibly are `P(G) + P(S) < 1` and `0 < P(T) < 1 − P(S)/(1 − P(G))`. The second **supersedes** the first and makes the ceiling on the admissible learning rate a decreasing function of the guess rate. Baker et al. [S17] impose the stronger `P(G) < 0.5`, `P(S) < 0.5`. So in the BKT family, a high chance floor is not merely noise — it shrinks the space in which a learning rate can be estimated at all.

**F6 — Empirically, near-identical models fit very different rates with indistinguishable predictive accuracy.** [S5] Käser, Koedinger & Gross fit AFM and three close cousins (LG, MA, DIS/DISMA) to mastery-learning data from 134 children with confirmed external post-test gains. "Different parameter fits lead to the same prediction accuracy on unseen data." The AFM "fits learning rates γ_k in a very small range around 0 and 45% of the learning rates are not significantly different from zero"; in their Version 2 data, **AFM fit positive learning rates for 54% of skills while LG and MA fit positive rates for 100% of skills** — despite modelling the same data with the same predictive accuracy. They also attribute *directional* biases: AFM biased to **underestimate** rate (mastery attrition leaves weak students at high opportunity counts), LG biased to **overestimate** rate (its mastery-aligned end point is always a success).

### B. The clamping convention — the field's own documented source of one-sided positive bias

**F7 — The canonical logistic learning-rate estimators are deliberately non-negativity-constrained.** [S4] PFA's own introduction states: "PFA was bounded to a minimum of 0 γ to **prevent over fitting from resulting in negative learning rates**." Secondary descriptions of the Cen/DataShop AFM specification report the same restriction: learning rates are constrained positive "under the assumption that practice can only improve the likelihood of a correct step" [S16, SECONDARY]. Käser's contrast [S5] is the empirical footprint of this choice: unconstrained → 54% positive; constrained → 100% positive. Any estimator that clamps λ at 0, uses a softplus/exp link on λ, or places a prior with mass concentrated on λ > 0 will produce `E[λ̂ | true λ = 0] > 0` **exactly**, with a Monte-Carlo standard error that shrinks as 1/√N — arbitrarily many SEs from zero, from no learning at all.

**F8 — On the guess parameter and a 0.2 chance floor.** The field treats guess as a **bounded nuisance parameter absorbing everything that decouples response from knowledge**, not as a chance rate. Beck & Chang list what it absorbs: "lucky guesses, baseline performance for some testing formats (e.g. multiple choice tests), using partial knowledge to answer a question, using a weaker version of the correct rule ... or even the inaccuracy from using a speech recognizer" [S1]. Their Reading Tutor model used `G = 0.53` deliberately because of ASR noise. Corbett's Cognitive Tutor code caps guess at 0.30 [S1]; standard BKT toolkits default to caps of 0.3 for both slip and guess [S23-adjacent, SECONDARY]; Baker et al. use 0.5 [S17]. A 5-option chance floor of 0.20 sits just under the 0.30 convention. **I found no source that derives a rule for fixing P(G) = 1/k for k-option multiple choice inside BKT.** Where guess is left free, van de Sande's identity `A = (1−S−G)(1−L0)` [F2] says it will trade off one-for-one against the estimated starting level.

### C. Per-student learning rate: how the field estimates it and how reliable it is

**F9 — AFM and PFA, the two workhorse logistic models, deliberately do not estimate a per-student rate.** AFM's stated assumptions include that "students learn at the same rate"; β_k (difficulty) and γ_k (rate) are per-skill and the student gets an *intercept only* [S16, SECONDARY; corroborated by the AFM equation in S7 §3.2.1]. PFA goes further and removes the student parameter altogether: "α has been removed from the model since it is not usually [able to] be estimated ahead of time in adaptive situations" [S4]. PFA's γ and ρ (gains from prior successes and failures) are per-KC. Pavlik et al. concede the cost: "The fact that the subject parameter in LFA captures slightly more student variability than the performance accounting in PFA implies that future work might further improve the adaptive PFA model by adaptively estimating a subject parameter" [S4].

**F10 — The per-student-rate model is iAFM:** `logit(p_ij) = (θ + θ_i + β_k(j)) + (δ + δ_i + γ_k(j))·T_ik(j)`, with θ_i, δ_i, β_k, γ_k as zero-mean random effects and `T` the prior-opportunity count on that KC [S9; S7 eq. 2]. The per-student rate δ_i is a random-effect slope, estimated by penalized likelihood (lme4 / mixed-effects logistic regression) and reported as a shrunken BLUP. The analytic unit is the **(student, KC) pair**, and its length `L` is what governs how much information the slope has.

**F11 — Reliable per-student rate estimation has been demonstrated exactly once, and only half-succeeded.** [S7] Liu & Koedinger designed the near-ideal study: 196 high-school geometry students spanning honors to inclusion classrooms, 5–50 observations per skill per student ("excellent depth"), two architecturally unrelated models cross-checked (iAFM and iBKT), and closely-mapped external pretests and posttests. Results:
- **Ability**: reliable (strong cross-model Spearman agreement in both datasets) and valid (iAFM intercept vs pretest: r = 0.74 Ch.3, r = 0.66 Ch.4; adding the individualized slope *improved* the intercept's validity from r = 0.62/0.58).
- **Learning rate**: reliable and valid in **Chapter 4 only**. In Chapter 3, cross-model slope agreement was "much milder," and neither model's rate estimate predicted pre-post gains (iAFM: β = 0.396, p = 0.144; iBKT: β = 0.062, p = 0.218). In Chapter 4 both did (iAFM β = 0.576, p = 0.007; iBKT β = 0.133, p = 0.004).
- Cross-unit consistency: "Estimates of student ability are fairly consistent ... Estimates of student learning rate are less consistent," and the authors cannot distinguish unreliability from genuine content-specific rates.
- Their own framing of the risk: "More parameters create greater degrees of freedom and increase the likelihood that the model may be underdetermined by the data."
- Their diagnosis of the Chapter 3 failure is a **range-restriction argument**: "When students are practicing many opportunities at ceiling ... the individualized models will often assign them a lower 'learning rate' due to an essentially flat learning trajectory." Chapter 4 worked because its KCs were harder (mean β = 0.799 ≈ p 0.69, vs 1.253 ≈ p 0.78) — "A dataset with more difficult items should provide more sensitive measures of individual differences in improvement, since it avoids ceiling effects."

**F12 — The prior negative result.** [S7, describing its own reference 9] "We did not find evidence that individualizing student rate parameters consistently improved predictive accuracy ... nor could we validate the parameter estimates on out-of-tutor assessment data," on datasets that "either contained a small number of students or were largely sparse in observations for student-skill pairs, with the exception of two datasets. These two datasets happened to be the ones on which the Individualized-slope Additive Factors Model did achieve higher predictive accuracy." Also: "pooling students into 'groups' rather than generating individualized estimates worked well on those datasets."

**F13 — Severe intercept–slope collinearity, even with deep data.** [S7] iAFM student intercept vs iAFM student slope: **r = 0.78, p < 0.0001**. Liu & Koedinger offer three candidate readings (rich-get-richer, fast-learners-know-more, a shared trait) and do not choose. Whatever the substantive story, the estimation consequence is that the two parameters are far from orthogonal: variance can move between them.

**F14 — At scale, and with feedback, the between-student rate variance is tiny.** [S8] From 27 datasets, ~1.3M observations, ~7,000 students, ~200 observations per student: median student learning rate ≈ **0.1 log-odds (~2.5% accuracy) per opportunity**; median IQR of *student* rate = **0.018 log-odds**; median IQR of *student initial knowledge* = **0.830 log-odds**. The ratio is ~46:1. Translated into practice: the slower half of learners needs ~7.89 opportunities to mastery, the faster half ~6.94 — "a typical slower learner needs only one extra opportunity to keep pace with a typical faster learner," against a **ten-opportunity** gap for initial knowledge. Their own framing: "While hoping to create a method to identify high ability learners and understand their characteristics, we instead found strikingly similar rates of learning across students." Replicated at scale on a different platform [S13]: rate IQR = [7.01, 8.25] opportunities vs initial-knowledge IQR = [2.78, 12.18].

**F15 — And that tiny variance estimate is itself specification-dependent.** [S8, Supplementary Information] The same iAFM on the same data gives a median student-rate IQR of **0.018** under the best KC model but **0.002** under a single-KC model (means 0.028 vs 0.005). Their recovery simulation validated the model only after **multiplying the individual slope hyperparameters by 10** (and/or dividing the intercept hyperparameters by 10) — so what is demonstrated is that iAFM can recover a *ten-times-inflated* between-student rate variance, not that it can recover the small one.

**F16 — And it is sharply sensitive to which observations enter the fit.** [S9] Refitting iAFM under truncation at L = 10 inflates σ̂δ by a median **+118%** (IQR +44% to +297%; up in 18/20 datasets); a short-vs-long-stratum comparison gives median **+233%** (up in 9/9). σ̂θ is essentially unchanged (median −1.5%). The *mean* slope also inflates: median +18%, from 0.088 to 0.126 log-odds. Structural explanation: "at `T_ik(j) = 0` the model's predicted log-odds reduces to the intercept terms alone," so intercepts are pinned by the first opportunity while "each slope is a line fit through whatever range of opportunities a student happened to contribute." And the mechanism that matters most for a 30-trial block: **"a linear approximation of a concave curve produces a steeper slope when fit through early opportunities."** Four of 26 datasets produced singular fits, "meaning the data are too sparse for one of the random-effects parameters to be reliably distinguished." The authors explicitly decline to give a minimum-opportunity threshold and explicitly decline to claim the regularity is false: "These results do not establish that students learn at substantially different rates."

### D. Spurious rate from the administration loop, not the estimator

**F17 — Item ordering bias produces an increasing learning curve under the true model.** [S6] Effenberger, Pelánek & Čechák simulate an AFM (300 students, 15 items, two KCs), then predict with *the same true model*. Under random item order the marginal learning curve behaves; under fixed item order with a 10% skip probability, "the item ordering bias can lead to an increasing marginal learning curve" — a fitted improvement with no change in the generating parameters. Their listed AFM assumptions include: "The model ignores the difficulty of items, i.e., two [items in the same KC are treated identically]" and "The model does not take into account any biases in data, e.g., a mastery attrition bias." Their diagnostic recommendation is the *completed* learning curve (a partial-dependence plot), which "uses the same population of items for each practice opportunity count, diminishing the effect of item ordering and attrition biases" — but "only helps if the model itself is not biased." They also found randomized k-fold and time-aware CV "overly optimistic compared to student-level cross validation," and that adding student priors did not reduce RMSE on their data and even worsened it.

**F18 — Population-composition drift across the opportunity axis distorts rate.** [S5, S22] In mastery-learning data, faster students exit early, so high opportunity counts are dominated by weaker students — biasing AFM rates *downward*; mastery-aligning the curves biases them *upward* because the aligned endpoint is a guaranteed success [S5]. The general lesson transfers: **any systematic difference between the population/conditions contributing early trials and those contributing late trials lands on λ.**

### E. Deep knowledge tracing

**F19 — DKT buys prediction, not a rate.** [S10] Khajah, Lindsey & Mozer show DKT's advantage over BKT largely dissolves once BKT is given forgetting, skill discovery, and latent student ability; they attribute DKT's success "to its flexibility and generality in capturing statistical regularities directly present in the inputs and outputs," not to representation discovery. The cost is stated bluntly: "DKT's advantages come at a price: interpretability. DKT is [a] massive neural network model with tens of thousands of parameters which are near-impossible to interpret." Their contrast: "the model parameters (forgetting rate, student ability, etc.) are psychologically meaningful" in the extended BKT. Precondition on DKT's power: "As long as there are sufficient data to constrain the model." Independently, Xiong et al. attribute part of DKT's reported ASSISTments advantage to duplicated data rows and inconsistent skill tagging [S19, SECONDARY], and Gervet et al. find logistic regression competitive or better except on very large datasets [S20, SECONDARY]. **DKT and its successors contain no per-student rate parameter to read off.**

### F. Elo / Glicko-style online ability tracking

**F20 — Elo's rate of change is a hyperparameter you set, not a quantity you estimate.** [S11] Pelánek states the equivalence directly: "The parameter K corresponds to the learning rate used in SGD." Elo "can model changing skill, but does not make fixed assumptions about the nature of learning. Since the Elo rating system does not make any specific assumptions, it should not be expected to bring an optimal performance for a particular situation." The design lever is the *uncertainty function*: for a genuinely changing skill "K should stay large"; for a nearly constant skill "we can use an uncertainty function which approaches zero with an increasing number of attempts." The field's recommended default, `U(n) = a/(1 + bn)` with a = 1, b = 0.05, **decays step size with experience** — deliberately tuned for convergence to a static estimate, i.e. it *suppresses* apparent within-session movement rather than measuring it. Where learning is modelled, it enters as an *asymmetric update for correct vs incorrect answers* — "Performance Factor Analysis Extended / Elo" (PFAE) — not as an estimated per-student slope. Pelánek also reports that global skill estimated on disjoint item subsets correlates only **~0.6**, which he calls "quite high" given intrinsic noise; that is a ceiling on the reliability of the *level*, before anyone tries to differentiate it.

**F21 — Math Garden's child-scale evidence is about months, and it uses response time.** [S21, SECONDARY] 3,648 children, >3.5M arithmetic problems **over ten months**, items targeted at mean success probability .75, with on-the-fly item calibration and a scoring rule that uses **both accuracy and response time**. The reported strengths are measurement precision, validity, reliability, and "many interesting options for monitoring progress." **I found no Math Garden result claiming measurement of within-session growth.**

### G. How many opportunities does the field think it needs?

**F22 — Explicit numbers I found, with what each one actually means:**

| Number | What it is | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **>2 observations per student** | sufficient for *structural identifiability* of BKT (unique global likelihood max) — nothing about precision | [S2] |
| **≥3 repetitions per item** | inclusion threshold for a per-item forgetting-rate estimate in a 20-min session | [S12] |
| **~7 opportunities per KC** | median opportunities to 80% mastery, *with feedback* | [S8], [S13] (7.22) |
| **~7 opportunities per KC (minimum)** | stated minimum for mastery estimation in OLI courseware | [S14] SECONDARY, unverified |
| **≥10 students per KC, ≥2 opportunities** | data-filtering thresholds used in the 27-dataset collection | [S8] |
| **5–50 observations per skill per student** | what its authors call "excellent depth," purpose-collected — and even then per-student *rate* validated in only 1 of 2 datasets | [S7] |
| **~200 observations per student** | the depth of the 27-dataset collection behind the rate-regularity result | [S8] |
| **median (student, KC) pair length 6.5–7.24** | actual depth in that same collection; median pair lengths range 2 to 21 across datasets | [S8], [S9] |
| **no threshold stated** | [S9] explicitly declines to give one, recommending instead that papers report "the median pair length, the spread of pair lengths, and the share of observations contributed by long pairs" | [S9] |

**F23 — Nobody in this literature states a minimum number of opportunities for estimating an *individual student's* learning rate.** The closest thing to a standard is a *procedure*, not a number: cross-model agreement plus external validation [S7], and sequence-length sensitivity reporting [S9].

### H. Spacing / memory models, insofar as they bear on rate from few trials

**F24 — A per-person rate *is* recoverable in ~20 minutes, but with four design features our screener lacks.** [S12, S25, S26] The Groningen adaptive-fact-learning line estimates a per-learner rate of forgetting from a 20-minute session (~31.7 items, ~7 repetitions per item), and the estimate explains **62.4% of variance in 3-day delayed recall** — far more than working memory (1.2%) or general cognitive ability (1.7%). The estimate is stable over time but does not transfer across materials [S26]. The design features that make it work:
1. **The same items are repeated** (~7 times each), so the growth signal is within-item, not between-item.
2. **Feedback is present** during learning — first presentations are study trials showing cue *and* answer.
3. **Response latency is used as a continuous signal** alongside binary accuracy at every update.
4. **A strong parametric memory model** [S25] reduces the estimand to a single scalar per item–learner pair, with the decay rate of each presentation's contribution tied to activation at presentation time.

## What this means for a 30-trial, no-feedback, single-session block

1. **The literature does not support calling this an identifiability failure.** [F3] BKT — a strictly harder inference problem than a two-parameter logistic growth curve — is structurally identifiable from three sequential observations. A MAP 1PL with `θ(t) = θ0 + λt` over 30 trials on a pre-calibrated bank is, to a first approximation, an identified model. The problem is almost certainly not that infinitely many `(θ0, λ)` pairs fit the data equally; it is that they fit *nearly* equally, in a direction the priors and constraints break asymmetrically.

2. **But the literature names three mechanisms that each produce a positive λ from a non-learning cohort, and all three are live in this design.**
   - **One-sided constraint.** [F7] If λ is bounded below at 0, softplus-linked, or given a prior concentrated on positives — the documented field convention for both PFA and DataShop AFM — then a null cohort yields `E[λ̂] > 0` exactly, and the Monte-Carlo SE shrinks with cohort size. 8.6 MC SEs from zero across 3,200 children is what this mechanism *looks like*: a fixed positive bias divided by a shrinking SE. Käser's 54%-positive (unconstrained) versus 100%-positive (constrained) contrast [F6] is the empirical signature.
   - **Curvature / functional-form misfit.** [F16] "A linear approximation of a concave curve produces a steeper slope when fit through early opportunities." With items above level and a 0.2 chance floor, the *observable* curve is compressed against the floor early and expands later; a straight line in log-odds fitted to that shape has a positive slope even with no change in latent ability. [F4] is the general statement: fit the wrong shape and one parameter absorbs the misfit, and *which* parameter absorbs it changes with sequence length.
   - **Position–difficulty coupling.** [F17] Item ordering bias produces an increasing marginal learning curve under the *true* generating model. In `θ(t) = θ0 + λt`, λ is the **only** term that can absorb monotone drift across trial position. Every non-learning source of monotone within-session drift — calibration error correlated with presentation order, item-exposure or adaptive-selection effects, format familiarization, warm-up, strategy stabilization, test-wiseness — is therefore routed into λ by construction.

3. **The confound most likely to be limiting recovery is θ0–λ collinearity, not λ's identifiability.** [F13] iAFM student intercept and slope correlate r = 0.78 in purpose-collected data with 5–50 observations per (student, skill). [F16] The structural asymmetry is that the intercept is pinned at `T = 0` while the slope is a line through whatever range of trials the child contributed. Any asymmetry in how the two are regularized — e.g. a tight informative prior on θ0 from the child's measured level, with a diffuse or one-sided prior on λ — will transfer variance from θ0 into λ. [F2] is the same trade-off written out algebraically in the BKT family: `A = (1 − S − G)(1 − L0)` fuses the chance floor with the starting level.

4. **The field's own best estimate of the true signal is very small — and it was measured under conditions strictly more favourable than ours.** [F14] Median between-student rate IQR = 0.018 log-odds/opportunity (SD ≈ 0.013 under normality) versus 0.830 for initial knowledge, on data with **instructional feedback on errors** and ~200 observations per student. A 30-trial no-feedback block removes the feedback that produced most of that learning, and cuts depth by ~7×.

   *My own arithmetic on those published quantities, flagged as mine and not a source claim:* for a logistic slope fitted over `t = 1..30`, the Fisher information in λ is approximately `p(1−p) · Σ(t − t̄)² = p(1−p) · n(n²−1)/12 ≈ p(1−p) · 2247`. At `p ≈ 0.3` (above-level, 5-option), `p(1−p) ≈ 0.21`, giving `SE(λ̂) ≈ 0.046` log-odds/trial. Against a true between-child SD of ≈ 0.013, reliability ≈ `0.013²/(0.013² + 0.046²) ≈ 0.08`, implying a correlation with truth of ≈ **0.28**. The reported r = 0.33 sits essentially on top of that. If that coincidence is not accidental, the 30-trial recovery ceiling is an *information* limit, not an estimator defect — and no reweighting, reparameterization, or prior choice moves it much. Under the same arithmetic, reliability 0.5 needs ≈ 68 trials and reliability 0.8 needs ≈ 109 trials in a single reasoning area. **This whole calculation is hostage to the true between-child rate SD**: if the real figure for gifted screening on above-level novel items is 0.05 rather than 0.013, then 30 trials gives reliability ≈ 0.54 and the design is salvageable. Establishing which regime we are in is a prerequisite question, not a detail.

5. **The one design in the literature that recovers a per-person rate from a single ~20-minute session differs from ours on four axes** [F24]: repeated items rather than all-novel items, feedback rather than none, response latency as a second continuous channel, and a strong parametric memory model rather than a free linear slope. Our design currently differs on all four.

6. **Elo/Glicko offers no route to a per-child rate.** [F20] The rate of change in an Elo system is `K`, a designer hyperparameter; the recommended default uncertainty function *decays* the step size with experience. Reading a growth rate off an Elo trajectory means reading off a quantity you chose. [F21] Math Garden's child-scale growth evidence is at the scale of months and leans on response time.

7. **DKT is not a candidate.** [F19] It has no rate parameter, its reported advantages over well-extended BKT are contested, and its precondition — "sufficient data to constrain the model" — is exactly what 30 trials per child does not supply.

## Productive tension

**Tension 1 — Is the fix a prior, or a different model?**
- *Beck & Chang pole* [S1]: performance data are structurally insufficient. "Prior beliefs are necessary to bias the student modeling search, and even large quantities of performance data alone are insufficient to properly estimate the model." Injecting a prior (Dirichlet, or a shrinkage prior on λ) is the principled remedy, and it improved held-out AUC (0.620 ± 0.002 vs 0.614 ± 0.002) *and* parameter plausibility. Fairly stated, this pole is not anti-empirical; it says the model class is wider than the data can arbitrate and the analyst must supply the missing constraint.
- *Doroudi & Brunskill pole* [S2]: the model is identifiable, so a prior is not needed for identification. What a prior does is *paper over a functional-form error*. "Perhaps, obtaining semantically degenerate parameters from a fit should signal that our students may be learning in more complicated ways than the simple BKT model can predict, and so we should try to find alternative models that fit our data better." Fairly stated, this pole says that when your parameter comes out implausible, the honest response is to change the model or the design, not to constrain the parameter into plausibility.
- **The stakes for us are direct and opposite:** the first pole says put a prior on λ; the second says a prior on λ is precisely how you manufacture a contamination floor.

**Tension 2 — Does an individual learning rate exist as a measurable trait?**
- *Regularity pole* [S8, S13]: on 1.3M+ observations across 27 datasets, learning rate is "astonishingly similar" across students; the interesting variance is all in the intercept. The authors pre-empt the obvious objection with three arguments: they *do* see rate variation across KCs; they *do* see large intercept variation; and their simulations recover high rate variation when injected. Their conclusion, stated against their own hopes, is that they set out "to identify high ability learners" and found near-constant rates instead.
- *Individual-differences pole* [S7, S12, S26]: rate *is* measurable and predictive when you build for it. Liu & Koedinger's Chapter 4 rate estimates were reliable across two unrelated architectures and predicted pre-post gains controlling for pretest. The forgetting-rate literature gets 62.4% of delayed-recall variance from a 20-minute per-person rate estimate, stable over time.
- *Artifact pole* [S9]: the regularity number itself moves by +118% to +233% depending on which observations enter the fit, and the reported variation "is not a privileged estimate of any underlying 'true' student-level variation." This pole is scrupulous about not overclaiming the converse: "These results do not establish that students learn at substantially different rates."
- **Note the honest asymmetry:** the individual-differences successes all involve either repeated exposures to the *same* material plus latency (forgetting rate) or a specific harder-KC dataset (Liu & Koedinger Ch. 4). The regularity pole is measuring something broader. It is not obvious these camps are even estimating the same construct.

**Tension 3 — Interpretable parameters versus predictive accuracy.**
- *Interpretability pole* [S10, S11]: Khajah/Mozer argue that in education, unlike vision or speech, "the input and output elements of models are psychologically meaningful," so structured interpretable models are appropriate and match DKT's accuracy once properly extended. Pelánek's case for Elo is the same instinct in a simpler form: one parameter per student, one per item, no fitting machinery.
- *Predictive pole* [S18, S20]: with enough data, flexible models win and interpretability is a preference, not a requirement.
- **Our problem is entirely a parameter-interpretation problem, which puts us squarely in the interpretability camp — but note the uncomfortable detail:** the interpretability camp's own flagship estimators (PFA, DataShop AFM) clamp the learning rate at ≥ 0 and cap the guess parameter, which is a tacit admission that the interpretable parameter is not trustworthy unaided.

**Tension 4 — Direction of the rate bias in short sequences.**
- *Underestimation pole* [S5, S22]: mastery attrition means high opportunity counts are dominated by weak students, biasing rate downward; AFM fit positive rates for only 54% of skills despite confirmed learning.
- *Overestimation pole* [S9, S6]: early-opportunity truncation inflates both the mean slope (+18%) and its variance (+118%) via curvature; item ordering bias inflates the marginal curve.
- These are not contradictory — they are different data-generating regimes. **Which one applies depends on the administration loop, which is why this is an administration-design question as much as an estimator question.** A single fixed-length block with no mastery-based exit is much closer to the overestimation regime.

## Starter hypotheses (NOT conclusions — candidates for the human owner)

These are framed as *falsifiable candidates* with the check that would discriminate them. None is an insight; the human owner writes those.

**H1 (constraint artifact).** The contamination floor is the arithmetic consequence of a one-sided constraint or asymmetric prior on λ. *Check:* refit the null cohort with λ unconstrained and a symmetric prior; the sign of λ̂ should be ~50/50. If it is, the floor is bookkeeping [F7, F6]. If λ̂ stays positive in ~all children, H1 is not the (only) story.

**H2 (curvature artifact).** The floor is what a straight line in log-odds does to a concave or floor-compressed true curve over early trials [F16, F4]. *Check:* fit a saturating/exponential form and a free-per-position form to the null cohort; the linear-λ estimate should be positive while the free-position fit shows no monotone trend.

**H3 (position–difficulty artifact).** Any correlation between trial position and effective item difficulty maps one-to-one onto λ, because λ is the only term that can absorb monotone drift [F17]. *Check:* within-child randomization of item order with a between-order comparison of λ̂; and fit λ alongside a free trial-position covariate to see how much of λ survives.

**H4 (variance transfer).** θ0 and λ are near-collinear at 30 trials, and any asymmetry in how they are regularized moves variance from θ0 into λ [F13, F2, F16]. *Check:* look at the posterior correlation of (θ0, λ) per child; compare λ̂ under (a) an informative θ0 prior from the child's measured level and (b) a diffuse θ0 prior.

**H5 (chance-floor squeeze).** A 0.2 guess floor on above-level items narrows the admissible rate band and pushes the estimator toward the boundary [F5, F8]. *Check:* re-simulate with a 3AFC or constructed-response format (floor 0.33 / ~0) and see whether the null-cohort λ̂ bias and the recovery r move as predicted.

**H6 (information ceiling).** r = 0.33 is close to the maximum achievable at 30 trials given the field's own estimate of between-child rate variance, so no estimator change fixes it [see §4 of the previous section]. *Check:* run the recovery simulation across a grid of injected true-rate SDs and read off the SD at which 30 trials reaches usable reliability. Then argue separately about whether that SD is plausible for above-level novel items. This is the single highest-value calculation on the list, because it decides whether the problem is *estimator* or *design*.

**H7 (report as an interval, or not at all).** The field's practice is shrunken BLUPs, variance components rather than BLUP-IQRs (because BLUP shrinkage depends on per-child data density [S9]), and refusal to report parameters that fail cross-model agreement [S7]. A screener that reports λ as a point score is doing something no paper in this literature does. *Candidate:* report λ only with a posterior interval, and suppress the report when the interval covers zero.

**H8 (add response latency).** The only literature that recovers a per-person rate in ~20 minutes uses latency as a second continuous channel [F24, F21]. 30 binary responses carry ~30 bits; 30 responses plus latencies carry substantially more. *Candidate:* an accuracy-plus-latency scoring rule of the Math Garden / Groningen type.

**H9 (repeat the knowledge component).** Both the BKT/AFM opportunity axis and the Groningen design measure improvement on *repeated exposure to the same KC*. 30 all-novel items means every item is seen once, so the entire growth signal rides on the difficulty calibration rather than on within-item improvement [F10, F24]. *Candidate:* isomorph families with 3–5 repetitions each, at the cost of bank novelty.

**H10 (cross-architecture agreement as the release gate).** Adopt Liu & Koedinger's reliability standard directly: fit two structurally unrelated models (e.g. a logistic growth model and a latent-state model) and require rank agreement on λ before any λ is reported [F11].

**H11 (report the intercept, not the slope).** Every large-scale result in this lane finds the *intercept* reliable and valid and the *slope* fragile [F11, F14, F16]. If above-level initial performance is the reliable signal, the honest product may be an above-level level estimate rather than a rate — with the rate demoted to a research quantity. This is a product hypothesis, not a measurement one, and belongs to the owner.

**H12 (specify the null explicitly and test against it).** [F4] Doroudi & Brunskill's method is the template: generate data from a model where the parameter of interest is *known to be zero*, fit the production estimator, and read off what it reports. The existing 3,200-child null cohort already does this; the missing step is doing it at several block lengths (10 / 20 / 30 / 60) to see whether the bias moves with length, which would confirm misspecification rather than constraint as the mechanism.

## What this does NOT support

1. **It does not support "BKT is unidentifiable, therefore our λ is unidentifiable."** [F3] The current state of the literature is the opposite: BKT is identifiable from three sequential observations under mild conditions, and the widely-cited 2007 claim was a conflation of marginal with joint distributions. Citing Beck & Chang (2007) as authority for "our model is unidentifiable" would be citing a paper the field has corrected.

2. **It does not support identifiability failure as the *cause* of a contamination floor.** Identifiability is a structural, asymptotic property. A fixed positive bias whose Monte-Carlo SE shrinks with cohort size is a *bias*, and this literature attributes such biases to one-sided constraints [F7], functional-form misspecification [F4, F16], and data-collection bias [F17, F18] — not to unidentifiability. Conversely, nothing here rules out a *local-optima* contribution [F3, S23].

3. **It does not license transferring the 0.018 log-odds between-student rate IQR to our setting.** [F14] That figure comes from course-embedded practice **with instructional feedback on errors**, ~200 observations per student, and knowledge components rather than novel above-level reasoning items. It is also specification-dependent by an order of magnitude (0.018 vs 0.002 under a single-KC model [F15]) and window-dependent by 118–233% [F16]. Using it as "the true rate variance" in a power calculation is a modelling choice that must be defended, not a fact.

4. **It does not establish that 30 trials is insufficient in principle.** No source in this lane states a minimum number of opportunities for per-student rate estimation; [S9] explicitly declines to. The ~7-opportunity figures [F22] are *opportunities to mastery with feedback*, not opportunities to estimate a rate — conflating them would be a serious error. The ~68 / ~109 trial figures in the previous section are **my arithmetic under stated assumptions**, not a published result, and they collapse if the true between-child rate SD is larger than the PNAS figure.

5. **It does not support DKT or its successors as a remedy.** [F19] They contain no per-student rate parameter, their advantage over well-extended BKT is contested [S19, S20], and they require far more data per learner than 30 trials.

6. **It does not support Elo/Glicko as a rate estimator.** [F20] `K` is chosen, not estimated; the field's recommended uncertainty function deliberately decays step size with experience. A "rate of rating change" read off an Elo trajectory is not an estimate of a learning rate.

7. **It does not support Math Garden as evidence that growth is measurable within a single session.** [F21] Its reported growth monitoring is over ten months. Its relevance to us is the *scoring rule* (accuracy plus response time) and the target success probability (.75), not a within-session growth claim.

8. **It does not support the claim that a 0.2 chance floor is handled by any principled rule in BKT.** [F8] I found no paper deriving "fix P(G) = 1/k for k-option multiple choice." The field's practice is a hard cap at 0.3 or 0.5 by convention, and where guess is free it trades off algebraically against the initial-knowledge estimate [F2].

9. **Sources I could not verify from primary text, whose claims must be re-checked before use:** [S14] Bälter et al.'s "minimum of seven opportunities" (Springer auth wall); [S16] Cen et al. 2006's exact assumption wording and the DataShop positivity restriction; [S17] Baker et al. 2008's exact constraints; [S19] Xiong et al.'s duplication finding; [S20] Gervet et al.'s conditions; [S21] Klinkenberg et al.'s specifics; [S22] Nixon et al. 2013; [S23] Shchepakin et al.'s derived inequalities; [S26] Sense et al. 2016's stability coefficients; [S15] Corbett & Anderson 1995.

10. **Two bibliographic cautions.** (a) I could **not verify the author list of arXiv:2605.01690** [S9], despite reading its full text — the listing page did not render a reliable author block for me. (b) I could **not confirm the existence** of a paper titled "Standard Error Considerations on AFM Parameters" (Durand, Goutte & Léger, EDM 2018); only the related 2017 NRC technical report on AFM surfaced. Do not cite either without independent confirmation.
