### Category 1: How ability tests are constructed, scaled, and reported

*General idea: A reported ability score such as "IQ 132" is not a measurement in the way a length is a measurement — it is the end product of a long chain of construction decisions (who was sampled for the norms, when they were sampled, how raw counts were transformed onto a scale, and how the scale was stretched across ages), each of which introduces error that the single reported number conceals. Every one of those decisions degrades fastest in the upper tail, which is precisely the region where gifted identification operates, so the psychometric properties that are strongest in a test's marketing (headline composite reliability near .96) are weakest exactly where the decision is being made.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: What professional standards actually require of a norm sample, and what published norm samples actually contain**
  - **Source:** [American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, & National Council on Measurement in Education (2014), *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Standard 5.8 states that "Norms, if used, should refer to clearly described populations. These populations should include individuals or groups with whom test users will ordinarily wish to compare their own examinees." *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Standard 5.8, p. 104. Washington, DC: AERA. ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - Standard 5.9 states that "Reports of norming studies should include precise specification of the population that was sampled, sampling procedures and participation rates, any weighting of the sample, the dates of testing, and descriptive statistics. Technical documentation should indicate the precision of the norms themselves." *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, Standard 5.9, p. 104.)* `T1`
      - Standard 5.11 assigns the publisher a continuing obligation: "If a test publisher provides norms for use in test score interpretation, then as long as the test remains in print, it is the test publisher's responsibility to renorm the test with sufficient frequency to permit continued accurate and appropriate score interpretations." *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, Standard 5.11, p. 105.)* `T1`
      - Standard 5.6 requires that "Testing programs that attempt to maintain a common scale over time should conduct periodic checks of the stability of the scale on which scores are reported," and specifies that where scales rest on precalibrated item response theory parameter estimates, "periodic analyses of item parameter stability should be routinely undertaken." *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, Standard 5.6, p. 103.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* are jointly sponsored and jointly copyrighted by AERA, APA, and NCME; the 2014 edition is the sixth in a line beginning with the 1954 APA *Technical Recommendations for Psychological Tests and Diagnostic Techniques*, and each association's governing body has formally approved it as representing best practice for its members. *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, Preface; NCME, "Testing Standards," ncme.org.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The governing professional standard in United States testing does not merely ask that a test have norms; it asks that the norm population be named precisely enough that a user can tell whether their own examinees belong to it, that the sampling procedure and participation rate be disclosed, that the *precision of the norms themselves* be documented as a separate quantity from the reliability of the test, and that the publisher keep renorming for as long as the test is sold. The standard also treats scale stability over time as an ongoing empirical obligation rather than a one-time construction step. This matters because it establishes that "the norms are old" and "the norm sample was unrepresentative" are not pedantic objections invented by critics — they are failures against a standard the field itself wrote.

- **Subcategory 1.2: The reported metrics — standard scores, percentile ranks, stanines, NCEs — and the information they destroy**
  - **Source:** [Riverside Insights / Lohman, CogAT Forms 7 and 8 score documentation (2011–2020)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The CogAT Standard Age Score (SAS) is a normalized standard score with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of **16** (not 15), reported for each battery and for composites, and ranging from 50 to 160 on Forms 7 and 8. *(Riverside Insights, "Types of Scores," CogAT Elevate online help; Riverside Insights, CogAT Report Guide: List of Student Scores.)* `T3` `[COI: Riverside Insights publishes CogAT]`
      - CogAT scoring proceeds raw score → Universal Scale Score (USS) → normative scores; the USS is described as "a developmental scale score that is used as the entry for all of the age and grade norms tables" providing "a continuous growth scale of cognitive development from kindergarten through grade 12." *(Riverside Insights, "Types of Scores," CogAT Elevate online help.)* `T3` `[COI: publisher describing its own scaling method]`
      - In the published CogAT Forms 7/8 conversion table, every Standard Age Score from **135 through the scale maximum of 160 maps to a single age percentile rank of 99 and stanine of 9**; SAS 130 maps to percentile rank 97. At the other end, every SAS from 50 through 63 maps to percentile rank 1 and stanine 1. *(Riverside Insights, 2020, *CogAT Forms 7/8 Complete Norms and Score Conversions Guide*, Table 37, p. 126.)* `T3` `[COI: publisher's own norm table]`
      - CogAT reports age-normed scores (standard age scores, age percentile ranks, age stanines), grade-normed scores (grade percentile ranks, grade stanines), and Normal Curve Equivalents, and the publisher's own conversion guidance treats percentile-rank-to-stanine mapping as fixed bands (percentile ranks 96–99 → stanine 9; 89–95 → 8; 77–88 → 7; 60–76 → 6; 41–59 → 5; 24–40 → 4; 12–23 → 3; 5–11 → 2; 1–4 → 1). *(Riverside Insights, 2020, *CogAT Forms 7/8 Complete Norms and Score Conversions Guide*, pp. 10–11.)* `T3` `[COI: publisher documentation]`
      - The Otis–Lennon School Ability Test reports a School Ability Index together with percentile rank, stanine, and NCE by age, and percentile rank, stanine, and NCE by grade; the publisher also offers scaled scores "to represent performance on a continuous growth scale" and to support out-of-level testing. *(Pearson Assessments, OLSAT 8 product documentation.)* `T3` `[COI: Pearson publishes the OLSAT]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two things are visible in the publisher's own tables that are rarely said out loud. First, the standard-score metric is not standardized across instruments: a CogAT SAS uses SD 16 while a Wechsler or Stanford–Binet composite uses SD 15, so a nominal cutoff of "130" is a different point in the distribution depending on which test produced it — and the publisher's own table confirms it, placing SAS 130 at the 97th percentile rather than the 98th. Second, percentile ranks and stanines are lossy compressions that fail catastrophically in the tail: a 26-point band of CogAT standard scores from 135 to 160 collapses into the single value "99th percentile," and stanine 9 absorbs everything from the 96th percentile upward. Any selection system whose input is a percentile rank or a stanine has therefore already discarded most of the discriminating information in the range where gifted identification happens, before any human ever looks at the number.
    - **Link to source:** [CogAT Forms 7/8 Norms and Score Conversions Guide](https://onlinehelp.riversideinsights.com/Help/Elevate/assets/docs/CogAT_78_COMP_NandSC_Guide_091820.pdf) · [Riverside "Types of Scores"](https://onlinehelp.riversideinsights.com/Help/Elevate/Topics/015_Interpreting_Scores/Types_of_Scores.htm)

- **Subcategory 1.3: Scaling — raw to scale score, IRT, and what vertical scaling assumes**
  - **Source:** [Kolen & Brennan (2014); Li & Lissitz (2012)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The standard reference treatment of the field distinguishes equating (alternate forms of the same test), scaling to achieve comparability, and linking as separate operations with different assumptions and different defensible interpretations. *(Kolen & Brennan, 2014, *Test Equating, Scaling, and Linking: Methods and Practices*, 3rd ed., New York: Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7.)* `T2`
      - Kolen and Brennan distinguish two definitions of growth used in vertical scaling: a *domain* definition covering content across all grade levels, and a *grade-to-grade* definition assessed using a test level appropriate to each grade; the domain definition is difficult to operationalize because a test spanning all grades would be very long and would contain many items far too easy or too difficult for any given examinee. *(Li & Lissitz, 2012, *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 36(1):3–20, §1.3, summarizing Kolen & Brennan, DOI 10.1177/0146621611432864.)* `T2`
      - Two assumptions must be satisfied for IRT vertical scaling: (1) unidimensionality of the test at each grade level, meaning items measure a single latent trait at their targeted grade, and (2) test construct invariance across grades, meaning tests at different difficulty levels maintain the same construct. *(Li & Lissitz, 2012, *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 36(1):3–20, §1.4, DOI 10.1177/0146621611432864.)* `T2`
      - Kolen and Brennan noted that separate calibration of test levels may be preferable to concurrent calibration "because it mitigates the effects of violations of the unidimensionality assumption in IRT," and concluded that vertical scaling is a very complex process in which many design decisions materially affect the resulting scale. *(Tong, NCME ITEMS Module 31, "Scaling I: Overview of Procedures," summarizing Kolen & Brennan, 2004, p. 391.)* `T3`
      - Scale shrinkage — decreasing within-grade score variability as grade level increases — has been documented in IRT vertical scales and has led to sustained criticism of them; the causes are not fully understood, and assumption violations (local item dependence, multidimensionality, and dissimilar reliability across grade-level tests) are commonly cited candidate explanations. *(Topczewski, 2013, "Effect of Violating Unidimensional Item Response Theory Vertical Scaling Assumptions on Developmental Score Scales," doctoral dissertation, The University of Iowa, DOI 10.17077/etd.nthg3aa0.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Putting a raw count of correct answers onto an interpretable scale is a modeling act, not a conversion. When the scale is stretched *vertically* — across ages or grades, as every developmental scale score and every "continuous growth scale" claim requires — it rests on two assumptions that are known to be routinely false in practice: that the test measures one thing at each level, and that it measures the *same* thing at every level. The empirical signature of these violations, scale shrinkage, has been visible in vertical scales for decades and remains unexplained. This is the most under-examined layer in the whole chain, because vertical scales are usually presented to users as a self-evident growth ruler rather than as a model output with falsifiable premises.
    - **Link to source:** [Kolen & Brennan (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7) · [Li & Lissitz (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621611432864)

- **Subcategory 1.4: Precision — why one number is not a measurement, and why the SEM you are shown is the wrong SEM**
  - **Source:** [AERA, APA, & NCME (2014), Standards 2.13–2.15]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Standard 2.13 requires that "The standard error of measurement, both overall and conditional (if reported), should be provided in units of each reported score." *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, Standard 2.13, p. 45.)* `T1`
      - Standard 2.14 requires that "When possible and appropriate, conditional standard errors of measurement should be reported at several score levels unless there is evidence that the standard error is constant across score levels. Where cut scores are specified for selection or classification, the standard errors of measurement should be reported in the vicinity of each cut score." Its comment adds that "Estimation of conditional standard errors is usually feasible with the sample sizes that are used for analyses of reliability/precision" and that any assumption of constancy must be justified. *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, Standard 2.14, p. 46.)* `T1`
      - Standard 2.15 requires investigation and reporting when "there is credible evidence for expecting that conditional standard errors of measurement or test information functions will differ substantially for various subgroups." *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, Standard 2.15, p. 46.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* separately warn, in the credentialing chapter, that "Subscores are often based on relatively small numbers of items and can be much less reliable than the total score. Moreover, differences in subscores may simply reflect measurement error." *(AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014, ch. 11, p. 175.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The professional standard already requires conditional standard errors at multiple score levels and specifically near any cut score, and explicitly rejects the default assumption that error is constant across the scale. It also rules that this is *feasible* with ordinary reliability sample sizes, removing the usual excuse. In other words, the practice of reporting one global SEM and one global reliability coefficient is not merely a simplification; where a cut score exists, it is a departure from the standard.
    - **Link to source:** [Testing Standards, chapter 2](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)

  - **Source:** [Kolen, Hanson, & Brennan (1992); Kolen, Zeng, & Hanson (1996); Harvill (1991) reporting Feldt, Steffen, & Gupta (1985); Nicewander (2018)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kolen, Hanson, and Brennan set out a procedure for estimating reliability and conditional standard errors of measurement of *scale* scores from a single administration, and showed that the choice of raw-to-scale transformation (including score normalization, the number of scale points used, and the equating transformation) itself changes scale-score reliability and conditional SEM. *(Kolen, Hanson, & Brennan, 1992, *Journal of Educational Measurement*, 29(4):285–307, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1992.tb00378.x.)* `T2`
      - The same paper notes that reporting conditional standard errors by score level was already recommended by the 1985 edition of the *Standards*, and describes a procedure for constructing score scales that deliberately equalize standard errors of measurement along the scale. *(Kolen, Hanson, & Brennan, 1992, *Journal of Educational Measurement*, 29(4):285–307.)* `T2`
      - An IRT method for conditional SEM of scale scores accounts for the additional measurement error introduced simply by *rounding scale scores to integers*. *(Kolen, Zeng, & Hanson, 1996, *Journal of Educational Measurement*, 33(2):129–140, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1996.tb00485.x.)* `T2`
      - Feldt, Steffen, and Gupta found that SEM *in raw-score units* peaked in the middle of the score range and varied across score levels, with the maximum "often more than twice the minimum," a result consistent across five estimation methods, three tests, and two grade levels; they concluded that "the standard error of measurement computed by the traditional formula for the test as a whole does not adequately summarize the error propensity of many — perhaps most — examinees." *(Harvill, 1991, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*, 10(2):33–41, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1991.tb00195.x, quoting Feldt, Steffen, & Gupta, 1985, p. 358.)* `T3`
      - Nicewander demonstrated that conventional conditional SEMs can positively mislead about where a test is precise: on one test, conditional *reliabilities* ranged from .17 at a number-correct score of 6 to .91 at a score of 19, dropping rapidly below 10 and above 23, while the conventional CSEM implied the test was *most* precise for scale scores between 23 and 25 — a region containing only about 7% of the population. *(Nicewander, 2018, *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, 79(1):5–18, DOI 10.1177/0013164418758373.)* `T2`
      - Empirical CSEM curves for a large operational admissions test hovered near 1.5 scale points through the middle of the score scale and decreased to 1 or below at the very bottom and very top of the scale. *(ACT, 2013, Research Report 2013-7, "A Comparison of Three Methods for Computing Scale Score Conditional Standard Errors of Measurement.")* `T3` `[COI: ACT is the publisher of the test analyzed]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Precision is not a scalar property of a test. It varies by score level, and — this is the part usually missed — *which* score levels look precise depends entirely on which metric the error is expressed in. In raw-score units, error is largest in the middle and smallest at the extremes, because a near-perfect or near-zero raw score has little room to vary. On a bounded scale score, error again shrinks at the extremes for the same mechanical reason. But conditional *reliability* — error relative to the true-score variance actually present at that level — collapses in both tails. Nicewander's result is the sharpest form of the problem: a test can appear at its most precise, by conventional CSEM, in exactly the region where it is least able to distinguish one person from another. Rounding to integer scale scores adds error on top of all of this. A single reported number with no interval is therefore not just incomplete; it is systematically misleading in a direction that flatters the test at the extremes.
    - **Link to source:** [Kolen, Hanson & Brennan (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1992.tb00378.x) · [Nicewander (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164418758373) · [Harvill (1991)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1991.tb00195.x)

  - **Source:** [Riverside Insights, CogAT Form 7 Score Interpretation Guide (2020)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - CogAT score reports display a confidence band around each battery score, described as "the range in which the score is likely to fall if the student were tested again," and the publisher's own ability-profile classification is defined by whether those confidence bands overlap. *(Riverside Insights, 2020, *CogAT 7 Score Interpretation Guide*, v2.1, pp. 12, 105–106.)* `T3` `[COI: publisher documentation about its own reporting]`
      - The publisher instructs users that an unusually wide confidence band is itself a diagnostic signal — of "confusion on the student's part in following directions or recording answers" — and flags scores obtainable by random responding as "targeted (chance) scores." *(Riverside Insights, 2020, *CogAT 7 Score Interpretation Guide*, v2.1.)* `T3` `[COI: publisher documentation]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** At least one widely used group screener does report interval information at the individual level and builds its profile interpretation on interval overlap rather than point comparison. This is worth recording as a positive existence proof: the objection to point scores is not that intervals are impractical to produce or communicate to teachers, because a commercial publisher already does both.
    - **Link to source:** [CogAT 7 Score Interpretation Guide](https://www.riversidedatamanager.com/BalancedManagement/DigitalResources/Baggage_Files/CogAT/CogAT_7_SIG_v.2-1_092220.pdf)

- **Subcategory 1.5: Ceilings, floors, and the thin-tail problem — norms are least trustworthy exactly where identification happens**
  - **Source:** [Pearson (2014/2016) WISC-V Technical Report 6 and WISC-IV Technical Report 7, Extended Norms]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Among the 2,200 cases in the WISC-V normative sample, "only 16 children obtained any composite score of 150 or higher." *(Pearson, WISC-V Technical Report 6: Extended Norms, p. 1.)* `T3` `[COI: Pearson publishes the WISC-V]`
      - Among the 2,200 cases in the WISC-IV standardization sample, "only one child obtained a GAI score of 151 and none obtained an FSIQ score of 150 or higher." *(Pearson, WISC-IV Technical Report #7: Extended Norms, p. 1.)* `T3` `[COI: Pearson publishes the WISC-IV]`
      - Pearson developed WISC-V extended norms "in response to requests from the National Association for Gifted Children," raising the subtest scaled-score maximum from 19 to 28 and the composite maximum to 210, and states they are "useful when a child's scaled score on one or more subtests is at the maximum value (i.e., 19 points)." *(Pearson, WISC-V Technical Report 6: Extended Norms, p. 1.)* `T3` `[COI: publisher describing a remedy for its own instrument's ceiling]`
      - Pearson notes that because "the natural ceilings of the WISC-IV subtests are not equally high," maximum extended scaled scores differ across subtests and across ages, so that "the possible extended scaled, IQ, and index scores are higher for a younger child, so his or her scores could be lower when assessed a few years later." *(Pearson, WISC-IV Technical Report #7: Extended Norms.)* `T3` `[COI: publisher documentation]`
      - The SB5 Interpretive Manual introduces an Extended IQ (EXIQ) scale supporting Full Scale IQ scores below 40 (down to about 10) and above 160 (up to 225). *(SB5 Interpretive Manual, as described in distributor documentation, mindresources.com; Roid, 2003.)* `T3` `[COI: test author and distributor documentation]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The publishers' own technical reports are the most damning available evidence about the upper tail. In a nationally representative sample of 2,200 children, the number of observations available to anchor the region above a composite of 150 was *one* for the WISC-IV and *sixteen* for the WISC-V. Extended norms above those points are therefore statistical extrapolations supplemented by targeted high-ability oversampling, not norms in the ordinary sense of empirical frequency. Pearson's own caveat that extended scores are higher for younger children — so a child can score lower on retest years later purely because of ceiling geometry — is an admission that the extended scale is not invariant across age. Anyone reasoning about a "160" or a "180" is reasoning about a model extrapolation, and the further out the score, the more it is model and the less it is data.
    - **Link to source:** [WISC-V Technical Report 6](https://www.pearsonassessments.com/content/dam/school/global/clinical/us/assets/wisc-v/wisc-v-technical-report-6-extended-norms.pdf) · [WISC-IV Technical Report 7](https://www.pearsonassessments.com/content/dam/school/global/clinical/us/assets/wisc-iv/wisc-iv-technical-report-7.pdf)

  - **Source:** [Irby & Floyd (2017); Hagmann-von Arx, Lemola, & Grob (2016/2018)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Three brief intelligence tests were administered to 36 children with intellectual giftedness, scored live by primary examiners and rescored by a secondary examiner, yielding six IQs per student (216 values) submitted to a generalizability-theory analysis; the resulting dependability coefficient was only **.80**, which the authors characterize as "relatively low exchangeability across tests and examiners." *(Irby & Floyd, 2017, *Psychology in the Schools*, 54(9):1064–1078, DOI 10.1002/pits.22068.)* `T2`
      - In that analysis, error components for examiner, examiner-by-examinee interaction, examiner-by-test interaction, and test contributed little to IQ variability, but the **test-by-examinee interaction contributed about one-third of the variance in IQs**. *(Irby & Floyd, 2017, *Psychology in the Schools*, 54(9):1064–1078.)* `T2`
      - Across 206 typically developing children aged 6 to 11 administered five individually administered intelligence tests, scores correlated strongly with little or no mean difference at the sample level, but at the individual level "test scores significantly differed across tests for 12% to 38% of the children," and the differences depended not on which test was used but on unexplained error. *(Hagmann-von Arx, Lemola, & Grob, 2018, *Assessment*, 25(6):691–701, DOI 10.1177/1073191116662911.)* `T2`
      - In a small exploratory study of mathematically highly gifted children and adolescents, the authors reported a notable ceiling effect (mean scaled score 16.6 against a maximum of 19 on one subtest), restricted score variance, and consequently downward-biased correlation estimates, and stated that a formal range-restriction correction was available but not applied. *(Schneider & Krüger, 2026, *Journal of Intelligence*, 14(4):52, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence14040052.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** When you actually test the same gifted child on more than one instrument, the instruments disagree far more than their individual reliability coefficients would lead you to expect — dependability of .80 across a set of brief tests, with a third of the variance attributable to the specific pairing of *this child* with *this test*. That interaction term is the crux: it is not examiner noise and not a constant test-level offset that could be corrected with a conversion table, but an irreducible person-by-instrument effect. The parallel finding in a typically developing sample, where up to 38% of children got significantly different scores depending on which test they happened to take, shows this is not exclusively a tail phenomenon — but ceiling-induced range restriction makes it worse in the tail and simultaneously biases the correlations one would use to detect it downward.
    - **Link to source:** [Irby & Floyd (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.22068) · [Hagmann-von Arx et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1073191116662911)

    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**

- **Subcategory 1.6: Norm obsolescence — the Flynn effect, its reversal, and the documented cost of renorming**
  - **Source:** [Trahan, Stuebing, Fletcher, & Hiscock (2014)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 285 studies (N = 14,031) since 1951 in which two intelligence tests with different normative bases were administered, the random-effects meta-analytic mean was **2.31 standard score points per decade, 95% CI [1.99, 2.64]**. *(Trahan, Stuebing, Fletcher, & Hiscock, 2014, *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(5):1332–1360, DOI 10.1037/a0037173.)* `T1`
      - For the 53 comparisons (N = 3,951, excluding three atypical studies that inflated estimates) involving modern (post-1972) Stanford–Binet and Wechsler tests, the mean was **2.93 IQ points per decade, 95% CI [2.3, 3.5]** — comparable to the conventional "about 3 points per decade" estimate and *not* consistent with the hypothesis that the Flynn effect was diminishing. *(Trahan et al., 2014, *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(5):1332–1360.)* `T1`
      - For modern tests, study sample type (larger increases in validation research samples than in test standardization samples) and order of administration explained unique variance, but **age and ability level were not significant moderators**. *(Trahan et al., 2014, *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(5):1332–1360.)* `T1`
      - The authors frame the Flynn effect explicitly as a norms-obsolescence phenomenon: "The Flynn effect refers to the observed rise in IQ scores over time, which results in norms obsolescence." *(Trahan et al., 2014, *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(5):1332–1360.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The best available synthesis puts drift at roughly 2.3 points per decade across all instruments and roughly 2.9 points per decade for the modern individually administered batteries, with tight confidence intervals from a meta-analytic rather than scorecard method. Critically for gifted work, ability level was not a significant moderator, meaning this meta-analysis provides no support for assuming the drift is smaller (or larger) in the upper tail — though "not a significant moderator" in a set of 53 comparisons is weak evidence of equality rather than evidence of it. A norm set that is fifteen years old is, on these figures, mis-centred by roughly four points, which is more than the distance between many adjacent identification thresholds.
    - **Link to source:** [Trahan et al. (2014), Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037173)

  - **Source:** [Bratsberg & Rogeberg (2018); Dutton, van der Linden, & Lynn (2016)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using Norwegian military conscription records for birth cohorts 1962–1991 (817,611 individuals, of whom 736,808 had a valid ability score), mean IQ rose from 99.5 for the 1962 cohort to 102.3 for the 1975 cohort, then declined to 99.4 for the 1989 cohort before rising slightly to 99.7 for 1991. *(Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018, *PNAS*, 115(26):6674–6678, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1718793115.)* `T2`
      - For the rising period 1962–1975 the average **within-family** Flynn effect was 0.26 IQ point per year, closely matching the 0.28 annual gain estimated by a meta-analysis of 271 independent samples from 31 countries. *(Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018, *PNAS*, 115(26):6674–6678.)* `T2`
      - For 1975–1991, the observed within-family annual decline was −0.08 IQ point per year against an across-family trend of −0.23 point per year; after correcting for selective non-scoring (conscription test coverage fell from 93% in 1980 to 83% in 1991, with lower-scoring firstborns more likely to have unscored younger brothers), a Bayesian selection model recovered the full increase, turning point, and decline from within-family variation alone. *(Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018, *PNAS*, 115(26):6674–6678.)* `T2`
      - Because within-family variation controls for everything siblings share, the recovery of the reversal within families rules out changing family composition — including dysgenic fertility and immigration — as the primary driver. *(Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018, *PNAS*, 115(26):6674–6678.)* `T2`
      - A systematic literature review of the negative Flynn effect had earlier concluded that dysgenic trends were "the simplest explanation for the negative Flynn effect" — a conclusion the Norwegian within-family design directly contradicts. *(Dutton, van der Linden, & Lynn, 2016, *Intelligence*, 59:163–169, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.10.002; contradiction noted in Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Score drift is not a fixed upward constant that can be handled with a standing correction factor. In the best-instrumented national dataset available, the trend rose at about 0.26 points a year, turned over at the 1975 birth cohort, and then fell — and the fall shows up between brothers in the same household, which forecloses the composition-change explanations that a widely cited review had favoured. The practical implication for norms is uncomfortable: the sign of the correction is not knowable a priori for a given place and period, so "adjust downward for norm age" is as likely to be wrong as right for cohorts after the turning point. It also demonstrates how a systematic review of a literature can be overturned by a single study with a better identification design.
    - **Link to source:** [Bratsberg & Rogeberg (2018), PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1718793115) · [Dutton et al. (2016), Intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2016.10.002)

  - **Source:** [Kanaya, Scullin, & Ceci (2003)]
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Analysing 8,944 special-education assessments from nine school districts across the United States (testing from 1989 through 1995), students in the borderline and mild intellectual-disability range **lost an average of 5.6 points when retested on a renormed test** and were more likely to be classified as intellectually disabled than peers retested on the same test. *(Kanaya, Scullin, & Ceci, 2003, *American Psychologist*, 58(10):778–790, DOI 10.1037/0003-066x.58.10.778.)* `T2`

      - The authors emphasise that because the standard deviation of scores is smaller within the tails of the distribution, a 5.6-point shift there represents **about 72% of the standard deviation of scores in that range** — "a far larger magnitude effect than it might if it were to occur near the middle of the IQ distribution." *(Kanaya et al., 2003, *American Psychologist*, 58(10):778–790.)* `T2`

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the cleanest natural experiment on what norm replacement actually does to real classification decisions. The authors' point about tail standard deviations is the one to carry forward: the same absolute point shift is a much larger standardized shift in the tails, so norm-vintage effects on threshold decisions are amplified precisely where selection systems set their cuts. The study is on the lower tail, and I have found no equally clean study of the same mechanism at the upper tail — but the arithmetic of compressed tail variance is symmetric.

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **"Precision is worst in the tails" is true only on some metrics, and false on others.** Feldt, Steffen, and Gupta found raw-score SEM *peaks in the middle* of the range and is smallest at the extremes, with the maximum often more than twice the minimum. ACT's operational data show scale-score CSEM *decreasing* at both ends. Yet Nicewander showed conditional *reliability* collapsing in both tails while conventional CSEM implied the test was most precise at the top. All three are correct simultaneously, because they measure different things: absolute error shrinks near a bounded scale's edges, while error *relative to the true variance present there* explodes. Any argument that begins "the SEM is larger in the tails" is imprecise and can be refuted on its own terms; the defensible version is about conditional reliability and information, not about SEM in raw or bounded-scale units.

2. **Publisher-authored evidence is systematically more favourable, and this is now quantified twice.** Two independent meta-analytic teams found that the test author's participation in a study was a significant moderator of effect size — Lohman for CogAT, Naglieri for NNAT. This is not an allegation about individuals; it is a measured property of these literatures. But it cuts in an awkward direction, because the most detailed critiques of the Raven's and NNAT norms also come from Lohman, who authors a competing instrument. There is no source in this literature that is simultaneously well-informed and disinterested.

3. **The equity case for nonverbal tests and the psychometric case against them do not resolve.** Lee et al. found the NNAT does identify more students of colour than other nonverbal tests, while also finding it correlates only .31 with comparable ability measures and .20 with teacher ratings. Lohman argues nonverbal-only selection "would exclude the majority of the most academically accomplished students in all ethnic groups." A test can be simultaneously more equitable in outcome and weaker as a measure, and the literature has not produced a framework for trading those off.

4. **The Flynn effect's most cited corrective may be pointed the wrong way.** Trahan et al. found no diminishment through 2010 and no ability-level moderation; Bratsberg and Rogeberg found a clear turning point at the 1975 birth cohort in a near-population dataset, with decline recoverable within families. A practitioner correcting old norms downward by ~3 points per decade may be right for pre-1975 cohorts and wrong for later ones. Dutton et al.'s systematic review favoured a dysgenic explanation that the within-family design directly contradicts — a case where the higher-tier source is the one that was overturned.

5. **A publisher tells users to prefer the least reliable score it produces.** The CogAT interpretation guide states the ability profile is "far more informative and useful" than the composite. Watkins et al. found no WISC-V difference score reached .80 long-term stability and some had near-chance replication. These are different instruments, so this is not a direct contradiction — but the general psychometric result about difference scores is not instrument-specific, and the profile claim has not been independently validated for CogAT with retest data that I could locate.

6. **The professional standard requires things almost nobody does.** Standard 2.14 requires conditional SEMs near cut scores and rejects the constancy assumption absent justification; Standard 5.9 requires documenting "the precision of the norms themselves." I found no instrument in this review whose public documentation clearly satisfies Standard 5.9 for the upper tail — which is unsurprising given that WISC-IV had one observation above a composite of 150.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of these is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. They are cross-source connections I noticed while assembling the facts above; none has been tested, and each may dissolve under scrutiny.*

1. **Starter hypothesis: the reported quantity and the decision quantity are different quantities, and the field has never separated them.** Every fact in subcategories 1.2, 1.4, and 1.5 points the same way: percentile-rank compression destroys tail information, conditional reliability collapses in the tail, and norm anchoring in the tail rests on single-digit observation counts. Yet identification systems consume a point score or a percentile rank. The hypothesis is that a demonstration of screener effectiveness should be evaluated on *decision agreement under uncertainty* — how often two instruments, or two administrations, place the same child on the same side of a threshold — rather than on a correlation coefficient, because correlation is computed on the metric that has already thrown the tail information away. What would falsify this: if threshold-agreement rates turn out to be predictable from the correlation plus a normality assumption, the distinction would be bookkeeping rather than substance. The author should check whether McBee, Peters, and Waterman (2014, *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 58(1):69–89, DOI 10.1177/0016986213513794) on combination rules already formalises part of this.

2. **Starter hypothesis: the person-by-instrument interaction is the effect that matters, and it is invisible to every standard validation design.** Irby and Floyd found the test-by-examinee interaction contributed about a third of IQ variance among gifted children, with examiner effects negligible. Hagmann-von Arx et al. found 12–38% of typically developing children scoring significantly differently depending on which test they took, attributable to "unexplained error." Both findings require a *crossed* design — the same children on multiple instruments — which is exactly what conventional concurrent-validity studies (one sample, two tests, one correlation) collapse into a single number. The hypothesis is that generalizability theory with a crossed person-by-instrument design is the right tool for comparing a new screener against an established one, and that it will routinely reveal disagreement that a validity coefficient conceals. What would falsify this: if the interaction variance turns out to be small once ceiling-induced range restriction is properly corrected, then correlation was adequate all along.

3. **Starter hypothesis: publisher-favourable bias in this literature is measurable and should be treated as a formal moderator rather than a caveat.** Two separate meta-analytic teams independently found the same structural effect for two different instruments. The hypothesis is that any credible comparative evaluation of a new screener should pre-register author-affiliation as a coded moderator and should treat its own results as inside that same bias structure, not outside it. What would make this seed collapse: if the moderator effect in Ozen et al. and Lee et al. turns out to be confounded with study design or publication venue rather than reflecting author interest — and note that Ozen et al. found *publication type* was also a significant moderator, so the confound is live.

4. **Starter hypothesis: norm-vintage effects and tail-compression effects multiply rather than add.** Kanaya et al.'s key observation was that a 5.6-point shift equals about 72% of a standard deviation in the tail because tail variance is compressed. Separately, CogAT's own table shows a 26-point standard-score band collapsing to a single percentile rank at the top. If both are true, then a modest norm-vintage error and a modest measurement error compound into a large *rank* displacement in the tail while looking small in standard-score units — which is precisely the unit publishers report in. The hypothesis is that the practically relevant sensitivity analysis for any threshold system is expressed in rank or selection-set terms, not in score-point terms. What would falsify this: if empirical tail distributions are less compressed than the normal model implies (heavy tails), the multiplication would be weaker than the arithmetic suggests.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **CogAT norm sample size is unverified.** Publisher and secondary sources describe "a very large, stratified national norm group" but I could not obtain the CogAT Form 7 or Form 8 *Research and Development Guide* to verify an actual N, the stratification variables, the participation rate, or the dates of testing. This is exactly the information Standard 5.9 requires be public.
- **OLSAT 8 reliability is unverified.** I found secondary claims of internal-consistency coefficients around .72 to .96 but could not trace them to the technical manual or a peer-reviewed review, and did not assert them. The School Ability Index metric (M = 100, SD = 16) is also secondary-sourced.
- **Warne's (2015) CogAT7 test review is paywalled.** Only its existence and bibliographic details are verified. No substantive evaluative claim from it appears above, which leaves a genuine gap: this is the most obvious independent review of the most widely used screener, and I could not read it.
- **I could not locate a study of norm-vintage effects at the *upper* tail comparable to Kanaya et al. at the lower tail.** The arithmetic about compressed tail variance is symmetric, but symmetry of arithmetic is not evidence of symmetry of effect. Practice effects, ceiling geometry, and selection into retesting all plausibly differ between the tails.
- **Trahan et al.'s finding that ability level was not a significant moderator is weak evidence, not evidence of no effect.** With 53 modern-test comparisons, a null moderator result has limited power. Treating "the Flynn effect is uniform across ability levels" as established would overread the source.
- **No direct retest-stability evidence for CogAT ability profiles.** The general difference-score result is robust and instrument-independent in its mechanism, but I found no CogAT-specific test-retest study of profile classification stability. The publisher's base rates (A ≈ 50%/40%, B ≈ 26%/36%, E ≈ 9%) describe prevalence, not stability.
- **Vertical scaling evidence for ability screeners specifically is thin.** The scale-shrinkage and construct-shift literature I located comes from achievement testing and simulation studies. Whether the CogAT Universal Scale Score or comparable developmental scales on ability tests exhibit the same pathologies is, as far as I could verify, not publicly documented.
- **Assumption carried throughout:** I have treated Crossref metadata as authoritative for author, year, journal, volume, issue, and page. Where a source was accessible only through an abstract or a publisher landing page, the numeric findings quoted are those stated in the abstract, and I have not independently verified figures reported only in the body of paywalled articles.
