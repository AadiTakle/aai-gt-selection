# 02 — Dynamic Testing and Rate Stability

*General idea.* Scoring a learning rate presumes that rate is a real, stable, individually varying property that a test can capture. Three bodies of evidence bear on that presumption and they do not agree. The dynamic-testing tradition — test, teach, retest, or graduated prompting — has spent five decades trying to measure learning potential and does reliably add small-to-moderate unique variance over static ability, mostly for young children on skills close to the taught content, and mostly with the strong caveat that its central index, the pretest-to-posttest change score, is the field's oldest unsolved psychometric problem. Against it stands a large observational literature, now replicated, reporting that once initial knowledge is modeled, students' rates of improvement per practice opportunity are nearly identical: the interquartile spread in rate is an order of magnitude smaller than the spread in starting point, and a "slow" learner needs roughly one extra practice opportunity, not ten. Cutting across both is the retest literature, which shows that a substantial share of any observed climb is warm-up, familiarity, and test-specific strategy rather than the target construct — gains large enough to have destroyed a test's criterion validity in at least one high-stakes sample, and demonstrably unequal across demographic groups. All sources below are published studies of real learners; each DOI was checked against Crossref or DataCite, and none is retracted.

## 1. What the dynamic-testing tradition actually delivers over static ability

**Source:** Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs (2008), *The Journal of Special Education* `T2`; Dixon, Oxley, Gellert & Nash (2023), *Reading and Writing* `T2`; Dixon, Oxley, Nash & Gellert (2023), *Journal of Learning Disabilities* `T2`; Wood, Biggs & Molnar (2024), *Perspectives of the ASHA Special Interest Groups* `T2`; Grigorenko & Sternberg (1998), *Psychological Bulletin* `T2`; Fuchs, Fuchs, Compton, Hollenbeck, Craddock & Hamlett (2008), *Journal of Educational Psychology* `T2`.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- A mixed-methods review of 24 dynamic-assessment studies, with correlational analysis on 15 of them, found dynamic assessment showed superior predictive validity to traditional testing when feedback was not contingent on student response, when applied to students with disabilities rather than at-risk or typically achieving students, and when the criterion was an independent dynamic or criterion-referenced test rather than a norm-referenced test or teacher judgment (Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs, 2008, doi:10.1177/0022466907310366).
- A systematic review of 17 peer-reviewed articles reporting 18 studies published 1992–2020 found that, after static predictors were accounted for, dynamic measures of phonological awareness and decoding explained 1–21% of variance in the growth of word-reading accuracy and typically 1–9% in word-reading fluency (Dixon, Oxley, Gellert & Nash, 2023, doi:10.1007/s11145-022-10312-3).
- In that same review, dynamic measures of morphological awareness accounted for 4–33.4% of variance in reading-comprehension outcomes, while one dynamic decoding assessment accounted for 1% (Dixon, Oxley, Gellert & Nash, 2023, doi:10.1007/s11145-022-10312-3).
- A random-effects meta-analysis of 35 studies yielding 192 effect sizes found an overall pooled effect of g = 0.49 between dynamic assessments of word reading and word-reading outcome measures in children aged 4–10 (Wood, Biggs & Molnar, 2024, doi:10.1044/2024_persp-23-00262).
- That meta-analysis found dynamic assessment significantly better suited to children aged 4–5 and 6–7 than to children aged 8–9 (p = .0001), with validity otherwise consistent across typically developing versus at-risk and bilingual versus monolingual participants (Wood, Biggs & Molnar, 2024, doi:10.1044/2024_persp-23-00262).
- A companion systematic review of 14 articles found the predictive power of dynamic assessment of decoding and phonological awareness appeared to wane with increasing age as static measures explained more variance in outcomes (Dixon et al., 2023, doi:10.1177/00222194221117510).
- The canonical *Psychological Bulletin* review of dynamic testing concluded that the field had no consensus on the psychometrics of measuring change and treated the pretest-to-posttest difference score as its central unresolved measurement problem (Grigorenko & Sternberg, 1998, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.124.1.75; a publisher correction is on record at doi:10.1037/0033-2909.124.3.443, not a retraction).
- In a study of 122 third graders assessed in the fall and randomly assigned to 16 weeks of validated versus conventional word-problem instruction, structural equation models showed the dynamic assessment measured a dimension of pretreatment ability distinct from language, nonverbal reasoning, attentive behavior, calculation skill and word-problem skill (Fuchs et al., 2008, doi:10.1037/a0012657).
- In that study, instruction condition alone accounted for the year-end word-problem outcome proximal to instruction, whereas language, pretreatment math skill and the dynamic assessment were all needed to forecast the outcome more distal to instruction (Fuchs et al., 2008, doi:10.1037/a0012657).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** Dynamic testing does add something over static ability, but the increment is small-to-moderate, narrow, and conditional. It is largest for young children, for skills close to what was taught, and for far-transfer criteria that static pretests underdetermine; it shrinks as children get older and static measures catch up. The tradition's own reviewers have flagged that its headline index is a change score, whose psychometrics were never settled, and that its measured advantage varies with how feedback is delivered and which criterion is chosen — both degrees of freedom that inflate apparent validity.

## 2. The case that learning rates barely differ

**Source:** Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin (2023), *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* `T2`; Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali (2024), *Proceedings of the 17th International Conference on Educational Data Mining* `T2`; Lee, Lichand, Barnard, Klotz, Thille, Kim & Domingue (2026), *Proceedings of the Thirteenth ACM Conference on Learning @ Scale* `T2`.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- A mixed-effects growth model was fit to 1.3 million observations across 27 datasets of real students in elementary, secondary and college math, science and language courses (Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin, 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- Despite prior lectures and readings, the median initial performance across datasets was 0.638 log odds, or 65.42% correct, at the start of practice (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- The median overall learning rate was 0.09 log odds per practice opportunity, converting to a 2.5 percentage-point gain per opportunity from a 65% starting point (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- The median number of practice opportunities a typical student needed to reach 80% mastery of a typical knowledge component was 7.24 (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- The median standard deviation of student initial-knowledge estimates was 0.651 log odds with a median interquartile range of 0.830, versus a median standard deviation of 0.015 and interquartile range of 0.018 log odds for student learning rate (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- Expressed in percentage terms, the interquartile range of student learning rate spanned only 1.70% to 2.56% gain per opportunity, while the interquartile range of initial knowledge spanned 55.21% to 75.17% correct (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- A typical student in the bottom half of learning rate needed a median 7.89 opportunities to reach mastery versus 6.94 for the top half — a one-opportunity gap — whereas the bottom half of initial knowledge needed 13.13 opportunities versus 3.66 for the top half, a roughly ten-opportunity gap (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- Learning-rate variation across knowledge components was orders of magnitude larger than across students, with a median standard deviation of 0.102 and an interquartile range of 0.132 log odds, seven times the student figure (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- Disaggregated by domain, the median interquartile range of student learning rate was 0.045 log odds in the six language datasets versus 0.017 in the 16 math and 0.008 in the five science datasets; by grade level it was 0.015 for upper elementary, 0.018 for middle/high, and 0.018 for college (Koedinger et al., 2023, doi:10.1073/pnas.2221311120).
- The finding was independently replicated on 821,890 observations from more than 15,000 students across six math topics in an intelligent tutoring system during the 2022–23 school year, in a live "business as usual" context with a student population broadly representative of US K-12 (Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali, 2024, doi:10.5281/zenodo.12729850).
- Refitting the same model on 26 of the original 27 datasets while truncating practice sequences showed that capping at the first ten practice opportunities per student–skill pair inflated the median estimated interquartile range of student learning rates by 75%, and capping at five inflated it by 205%, with individual datasets ranging from negligible to seventeen-fold (Lee et al., 2026, doi:10.1145/3774398.3811619).
- The authors of that sensitivity analysis state explicitly that their results do not establish that students learn at substantially different rates, only that the published estimate of small slope variation warrants checking against which observations enter the fit (Lee et al., 2026, doi:10.1145/3774398.3811619).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** On the largest observational evidence base available, differences in learning *rate* are roughly an order of magnitude smaller than differences in *starting point*, and the gap they produce — one extra practice opportunity — is trivial next to the ten-opportunity gap produced by prior knowledge. The result is not a single-lab artifact: it replicates on a much larger, more representative, live tutoring corpus. The strongest published challenge is methodological rather than substantive — the rate-variance estimate turns out to be sharply sensitive to how many practice opportunities per student enter the model, which matters directly for any assessment that observes only a handful of opportunities — but that challenge explicitly declines to claim rates do differ.

## 3. What an observed climb is made of, and where real rate differences survive

**Source:** Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018), *Intelligence* `T2`; Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007), *Journal of Applied Psychology* `T2`; Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad (2007), *Journal of Applied Psychology* `T2`; Schleicher, Van Iddekinge, Morgeson & Campion (2010), *Journal of Applied Psychology* `T2`; Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn (2019), *Cognition* `T2`; Soderstrom & Bjork (2015), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* `T2`.

**DOK 1 — Facts:**

- A longitudinal multilevel meta-analysis of 174 samples from 122 studies, yielding 786 test outcomes and a total sample of 153,185, found a standardized mean change of 0.33 from the first to the second administration of a cognitive ability test with no intervention in between (Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, doi:10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003).
- That meta-analysis found gains of 0.50 from first to third administration and 0.17 from second to third, with no further score gains after the third administration (Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, doi:10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003).
- Retest gains from first to second administration differed significantly by test content, at 0.18 for figural, 0.33 for verbal and 0.41 for tests mixing several contents, and equivalence of test forms, retest interval and participant age were also significant moderators (Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, doi:10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003).
- A meta-analysis of 50 studies covering 107 samples and 134,436 participants found an adjusted overall retest effect of d = .26 for cognitive ability tests in selection settings, larger when practice was accompanied by coaching and when identical rather than alternate forms were used (Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard, 2007, doi:10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373).
- In a latent-variable analysis of 941 candidates taking a cognitive ability test in a high-stakes context, no measurement bias was present at initial testing, but retesting introduced both measurement and predictive bias, with the retest factor less saturated with g and more associated with memory — changes that eliminated the test's criterion-related validity (Lievens, Reeve & Heggestad, 2007, doi:10.1037/0021-9010.92.6.1672).
- Across large applicant samples taking three written tests (N = 7,031) and five performance tests (N = 2,060), White applicants showed larger retest score improvements than Black or Hispanic applicants on several of the assessments, with the differential larger on written than on performance tests (Schleicher, Van Iddekinge, Morgeson & Campion, 2010, doi:10.1037/a0018920).
- In a test–retest study in which healthy young adults performed four implicit learning tasks twice about a week apart, three of the four — probabilistic classification, serial response, and implicit category learning — showed medium test–retest reliability by Cohen's guidelines, while artificial grammar learning did not (Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn, 2019, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2019.05.007).
- In that same study, implicit learning on all four tasks was uncorrelated with independent measures of IQ, working memory, and explicit learning ability (Kalra, Gabrieli & Finn, 2019, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2019.05.007).
- An integrative review of the motor- and verbal-learning literatures concluded that performance observed during acquisition is often an unreliable index of long-term learning, that improvements in performance can fail to yield durable learning, and that certain instructional manipulations have opposite effects on performance and on learning (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, doi:10.1177/1745691615569000).
- That review also found that learners themselves routinely misinterpret their performance during acquisition as a reliable guide to what they have durably learned (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015, doi:10.1177/1745691615569000).

**DOK 2 — Summary:** Simply taking a cognitive test a second time buys about a third of a standard deviation with no learning intervention at all, rising to half a standard deviation by the third sitting before plateauing, and the size of that free gain depends on item format, form equivalence and interval — exactly the design parameters a rate-scoring instrument controls. Those gains are not construct-neutral: in one high-stakes sample the retest score was less g-loaded, more memory-loaded, and no longer predicted the criterion at all, and in large applicant samples the gains accrued unequally by race. The counter-case for genuine rate differences is real but comes from a different place — implicit learning tasks show medium rank-order stability across a week and are uncorrelated with IQ or working memory — and a separate literature warns that within-session performance change is a poor proxy for durable learning in the first place.

## Candidate tensions

- Dynamic assessment adds unique variance over static ability, yet the largest observational study of practice data reports almost no between-student variance in rate for it to be adding.
- The regularity finding replicates on 15,000+ students, while a refit of the same 26 datasets shows the rate-variance estimate inflates 75–205% when practice sequences are truncated to the five-to-ten observations a testing session could realistically collect.
- Dynamic assessment's incremental validity is largest for the youngest children and for skills closest to what was taught, but a selection instrument is most needed for older applicants and untaught content.
- The tradition's central index is a pretest-to-posttest change score whose psychometrics its own foundational review called unsettled, and which no subsequent meta-analysis has resolved.
- Mere repetition yields d ≈ .26 to .33 with no instruction, so a "learning rate" measured across two occasions is confounded with a gain the retest literature can produce for free.
- Retest gains were shown in one high-stakes sample to be less g-loaded, more memory-loaded, and to eliminate criterion validity — meaning larger observed gains can mean less valid measurement, not more learning.
- Retest score improvements were larger for White than for Black or Hispanic applicants, and larger on written than on performance formats, so a rate score can encode differential test-wiseness alongside any learning signal.
- Stable individual differences in learning appear on implicit tasks over a one-week retest interval but are uncorrelated with IQ and working memory, while the academic-practice literature finds near-constant rates — the disagreement may be about task type rather than about the phenomenon.
- Where dynamic assessment has been tested against a full school year, it forecast the outcome distal to instruction but was unnecessary for the proximal one, so its value may lie specifically in far transfer rather than in rate as such.
- Performance change observed during acquisition is an established poor proxy for durable learning, so a single-session climb and a semester of learning may not be the same construct measured at different lengths.
