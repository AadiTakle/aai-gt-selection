### Category 1: Response time as measurement information — joint RT–accuracy models

*General idea: Response time is the cheapest process signal to collect during any digital assessment, and a mature psychometric literature exists for modelling it jointly with correctness. The question here is not whether response time correlates with performance — it plainly does — but how much additional measurement information about a person's ability it supplies once correctness has already been used, under what modelling assumptions that gain survives, and what it costs in bias, validity, and estimation burden.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: The joint (hierarchical) framework — what it actually models, and what it assumes**
  - **Source:** van der Linden (2006); van der Linden (2007); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A lognormal model for item response times was specified with a parameter structure analogous to two-parameter logistic item response models: one *speed* parameter per person, plus *time intensity* and *time discrimination* parameters per item. Fitted by Gibbs sampling to response times from an adaptive form of a vocational aptitude battery, the lognormal specification fit well while a normal model could not accommodate the characteristic right skew of the response-time distributions. *(van der Linden, 2006, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 31(2):181–204, DOI 10.3102/10769986031002181.)* `T2`
      - A two-level "plug-and-play" hierarchical framework was proposed in which a measurement model for accuracy (e.g., normal ogive) and a measurement model for response time (e.g., lognormal) are specified separately at the first level and joined at the second level only through correlations among their person parameters and among their item parameters. The paper explicitly criticises earlier response-time models for building a speed–accuracy tradeoff into the parameter structure and for being unclear about which level of parameterisation they represent. *(van der Linden, 2007, *Psychometrika* 72(3):287–308, DOI 10.1007/s11336-006-1478-z.)* `T2`
      - The standard hierarchical model makes five distinct conditional-independence assumptions: local independence of the accuracies; conditional independence of the response times given latent speed; conditional independence of response time and accuracy given both latent variables; conditional independence of response time and ability given speed; and conditional independence of accuracy and speed given ability. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, in *Essays on Contemporary Psychometrics*, Springer, pp. 307–325, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - The same review states that conditional independence of response time and accuracy "often appears to be violated in practice," listing five published lines of evidence, and that both positive and negative conditional dependence can occur on different items *within the same test*, so dependence must be examined item by item. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - The same review notes that no formal empirical study of one of the five assumptions — residual dependence between accuracy and latent speed — had been conducted, and that no test had been developed to detect it. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The dominant approach does not treat response time as a covariate bolted onto a scoring rule. It treats speed as a *separate latent trait* with its own item parameters, and links it to ability only through a population-level correlation. That architecture is what makes the model interpretable, but it also loads the entire claim of added information onto a set of independence assumptions, at least one of which is known to fail routinely and another of which has apparently never been empirically tested at all.
    - **Link to source:** [Hierarchical framework](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-006-1478-z), [Lognormal RT model](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986031002181), [Limitations and risks review](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16)

- **Subcategory 1.2: How much precision does response time actually add? (the incremental-validity core)**
  - **Source:** van der Linden, Klein Entink & Fox (2010); Ranger (2013); Bolsinova & Tijmstra (2018); Reis Costa, Bolsinova, Tijmstra & Andersson (2021); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Within the hierarchical framework, response times were shown to act as *collateral information* for estimating response-model parameters through two channels: the second-level parameter estimates, and the posterior distribution of response parameters given the response times, which functions as an empirical prior. In an empirical study, both the accuracy *and* the bias of item and person parameter estimates improved — unlike conventional hierarchical item response modelling, where accuracy gains are usually purchased with increased bias. *(van der Linden, Klein Entink & Fox, 2010, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 34(5):327–347, DOI 10.1177/0146621609349800.)* `T2` `[COI: the lead author's listed affiliation on this article is CTB/McGraw-Hill, a commercial test publisher]`
      - Two later academic sources independently attribute to Ranger (2013) the result that the size of the precision gain for ability is bounded by the magnitude of the latent speed–ability correlation, and that the marginal gain from additional items decreases rapidly once speed itself is estimated with reasonable precision — so the largest relative gains occur on *short* tests. *(Ranger, 2013, *Psychometrika* 78(3):538–544, DOI 10.1007/s11336-013-9324-6 — bibliographic record verified; substantive content read here only through the two citing sources named next: Reis Costa et al., 2021, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128, and Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - An extension adding cross-loadings between ability and individual response times was proposed, on the argument that the standard model's assumption that *only* overall speed carries ability information ignores collateral information in the residual response times. Gains over both an accuracy-only model and the standard hierarchical model were evaluated by simulation and then applied to a high-stakes educational test. *(Bolsinova & Tijmstra, 2018, *British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology* 71(1):13–38, DOI 10.1111/bmsp.12104; Crossref records online-first 2017-06-21.)* `T2`
      - In the largest reported application — 10 released mathematics items from an international computer-based assessment — expected-a-posteriori reliability was 0.73 for an accuracy-only model, 0.77 for the simple-structure hierarchical model, and 0.80 for the cross-loading model; corresponding average standard errors were 0.51, 0.49, and 0.45. The authors' own characterisation of the difference between models was that "there was no substantial difference," while still describing the precision increase as notable. *(Reis Costa, Bolsinova, Tijmstra & Andersson, 2021, *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:579128, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128.)* `T2`
      - In the same study's country-level analyses, the *additional* reliability increase from adding cross-loadings on top of the simple-structure hierarchical model was "generally between 0.02 and 0.03," and was generally *less* sizable than the increase obtained from moving from the accuracy-only model to the simple hierarchical model; the authors conclude "the biggest gain in precision was already obtained by using a simple-structure hierarchical model." They also warn that the extra information captured "may not reflect the testing objective." *(Reis Costa et al., 2021, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128.)* `T2`
      - A review by two of that study's co-authors states the opposite theoretical expectation for the cross-loading extension: because its collateral information "increases linearly with every additional item," whereas the standard hierarchical model "can never do better than the inclusion of a single perfectly estimated covariate," the cross-loading gain "may exceed the original gain" and this is "especially likely for larger tests." *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the only subcategory that speaks directly to incremental validity, and the honest reading is that the effect is real, replicated, and modest. On a short mathematics test, using response times moved a reliability coefficient from 0.73 to 0.80 and shrank the average standard error from 0.51 to 0.45 — and the authors who produced those numbers described the between-model difference as not substantial. The gain has a theoretical ceiling set by how strongly speed and ability correlate in the population, and the marginal contribution shrinks as more items are administered, which means the signal is most useful precisely where there is least accuracy data. The elaborated cross-loading model is theoretically expected to scale better with test length, but in the one large empirical application reported here it added only 0.02–0.03 on top of the simple model.
    - **Link to source:** [Collateral information](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621609349800), [Bound on the gain](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-013-9324-6), [Cross-loading extension](https://doi.org/10.1111/bmsp.12104), [Large-scale application](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128)

- **Subcategory 1.3: The speed–accuracy tradeoff — why "faster = smarter" is a category error**
  - **Source:** Heitz (2014); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023); Goldhammer (2015); Goldhammer & Kroehne (2014); Goldhammer, Naumann, Stelter, Tóth, Rölke & Klieme (2014); Naumann & Goldhammer (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The speed–accuracy tradeoff — that increasing the speed at which a cognitive task is executed generally decreases accuracy — is documented as a *within-person* phenomenon with its own history, physiology, and methodology. *(Heitz, 2014, *Frontiers in Neuroscience* 8:150, DOI 10.3389/fnins.2014.00150.)* `T2`
      - A methodological review states explicitly that the between-person speed–ability correlation estimated in joint models "should not be confused with" the within-person speed–accuracy tradeoff, that the tradeoff is only *one* of the factors contributing to the between-person association, and that a more competent person can be both faster and more accurate — which is why the between-person correlation can be positive even though the within-person tradeoff pushes it negative. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - The same review reports that published estimates of the between-person speed–ability correlation include positive values, negative values, and values close to zero, citing separate empirical studies for each case. It further states that no inference about what would happen to a person's performance if they responded faster or slower can be drawn from this correlation, because that is a counterfactual within-person question the model cannot address. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - Separating ability from speed was argued to require *experimental control* of response speed rather than statistical modelling alone, in a target article devoted to the problem of whether a test measures ability, speed, or both. *(Goldhammer, 2015, *Measurement: Interdisciplinary Research and Perspectives* 13(3–4):133–164, DOI 10.1080/15366367.2015.1100020.)* `T2`
      - A method for experimentally controlling the time individuals spend on task in speeded performance measures was developed and reported. *(Goldhammer & Kroehne, 2014, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 38(4):255–267, DOI 10.1177/0146621613517164.)* `T2`
      - In 1,020 adults aged 16–65 completing computer-based reading and problem-solving tasks, the time-on-task effect *reversed sign by task type*: positive in problem solving (fixed effect β₁ = 0.56, z = 2.30, p = .02, at average task difficulty) and negative in reading. In problem solving the positive effect increased with task difficulty and decreased with skill; in reading the negative effect was stronger for easier tasks and increased with skill. The authors conclude time on task "has no uniform interpretation." *(Goldhammer, Naumann, Stelter, Tóth, Rölke & Klieme, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(3):608–626, DOI 10.1037/a0034716.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up study reported that time-on-task effects in digital reading are *non-linear* and moderated by both person skills and task demands. *(Naumann & Goldhammer, 2017, *Learning and Individual Differences* 53:1–16, DOI 10.1016/j.lindif.2016.10.002.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Speed and ability are separate latent quantities, and the sign of their relationship is not a fact of nature — it is an empirical property of a particular test, task type, difficulty level, and examinee skill level. The same behaviour (spending longer) predicts better outcomes on tasks requiring controlled processing and worse outcomes on tasks that reward fluent routine processing, and the effect is non-linear on top of that. Crucially, even a well-estimated between-person speed–ability correlation licenses no statement at all about what would happen if a given individual sped up or slowed down; that within-person question requires an experimental manipulation of speed, not a richer model.
    - **Link to source:** [Speed–accuracy tradeoff review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00150), [Time-on-task sign reversal](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034716), [Non-linearity](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2016.10.002), [Ability, speed, or both](https://doi.org/10.1080/15366367.2015.1100020)

- **Subcategory 1.4: Evidence-accumulation decomposition — drift rate as an ability-like parameter**
  - **Source:** Ratcliff, Schmiedek & McKoon (2008); Schmiedek, Oberauer, Wilhelm, Süß & Wittmann (2007); Lerche, von Krause, Voss, Frischkorn, Schubert & Hagemann (2020); Dutilh, Vandekerckhove, Ly, Matzke, Pedroni, Frey, Rieskamp & Wagenmakers (2017); Schubert (2019); van der Maas, Molenaar, Maris, Kievit & Borsboom (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Simulations showed that the diffusion model reproduces the "worst performance rule" — stronger correlations between intelligence and slow response-time quantiles than fast ones — under *either* of two different assumptions: that higher-ability people have higher drift rates, *or* that they have lower boundary separation. The two accounts are distinguishable only because the model also predicts accuracy. The pattern emerged only when other components of processing also varied across subjects. *(Ratcliff, Schmiedek & McKoon, 2008, *Intelligence* 36(1):10–17, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2006.12.002.)* `T2`
      - Individual differences in components of response-time distributions were related to working memory and intelligence; this study is repeatedly cited in later peer-reviewed work as having shown that working-memory capacity can be predicted from drift rate across a range of response-time tasks. *(Schmiedek, Oberauer, Wilhelm, Süß & Wittmann, 2007, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 136(3):414–429, DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.136.3.414 — bibliographic record verified; the specific characterisation above is taken from citing sources, not from the article text, and no effect size is asserted here.)* `T2`
      - In structural equation models, a domain-general drift-rate factor showed a strong relationship with general intelligence, and domain-specific drift factors related to matching domain scores: figural intelligence with figural drift r = .90 — which the authors themselves caution against overinterpreting because the residual variance of figural drift did not differ significantly from zero — numeric r = .74, and verbal r = .50. A latent psychometric-speed factor correlated most highly with latent drift rate (r = .59), less with threshold separation (r = .42), and essentially not at all with nondecision time (r = .04). The authors also report that models of *mean response time* did not reproduce the domain-general/domain-specific pattern that drift rates did. *(Lerche, von Krause, Voss, Frischkorn, Schubert & Hagemann, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(12):2207–2249, DOI 10.1037/xge0000774.)* `T2`
      - In a preregistered, analyst-blinded study of 916 participants, the classical worst performance rule was *rejected* for both easy and difficult perceptual stimuli; after exploratory release of the sign constraints on the correlations, the evidence still favoured absence of the effect (Bayes factors in favour of the null of 6.29 for easy and 19.9 for hard stimuli), and controlling for response caution by splitting into ten homogeneous-caution subgroups did not change the conclusion. In the *same* data, the predicted positive correlation between working-memory capacity and diffusion drift rate was "strongly confirmed" for both difficulty levels, although the authors note the drift–capacity correlations were moderate relative to comparable published studies and raise selective reporting and publication bias as a possible explanation for the apparent robustness of the worst performance rule in the wider literature. *(Dutilh, Vandekerckhove, Ly, Matzke, Pedroni, Frey, Rieskamp & Wagenmakers, 2017, *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics* 79(3):713–725, DOI 10.3758/s13414-017-1304-y.)* `T2`
      - A meta-analysis across 19 studies containing 23 datasets and 3,767 participants found robust evidence for the worst performance rule, but with a *logarithmic* rather than linear increase across response-time quantiles: r = −0.18 for fast responses, −0.28 for average responses, and −0.33 for slow responses. The largest change was between the fastest and average responses, not between average and slowest — leading the author to propose renaming it the "not-best performance rule." The effect was *attenuated* in less intelligent samples and was *larger* when response times were correlated with speed tests than with intelligence or memory tests. *(Schubert, 2019, *Intelligence* 73:88–100, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2019.02.003.)* `T1`
      - An explicit theoretical link was argued between process models for decision making and latent variable models for individual differences. *(van der Maas, Molenaar, Maris, Kievit & Borsboom, 2011, *Psychological Review* 118(2):339–356, DOI 10.1037/a0022749 — title- and bibliographic-level verification only in this pass; no specific finding is claimed here.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The evidence-accumulation decomposition is the strongest theoretical case for response time carrying ability information, because it explains *why* time and correctness are two views of one process, and because drift rate — not raw or mean response time — is the parameter that behaves like an ability. Latent drift-rate factors relate substantially to intelligence factors and outperform mean response time in reproducing the structure of intelligence. But the headline empirical regularity built on raw quantiles is shakier than its reputation: a large preregistered blinded test failed to find the worst performance rule at all while simultaneously confirming the drift-rate mechanism said to cause it, and the meta-analytic pattern is compressed into the fast-versus-average contrast rather than the average-versus-slow contrast the rule emphasises. Two of the meta-analytic moderator results also cut against a strong ability interpretation: the effect is weaker in lower-ability samples and stronger against speed tests than against intelligence tests.
    - **Link to source:** [Diffusion account of the worst performance rule](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2006.12.002), [Drift rates and intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000774), [Preregistered blinded test](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-017-1304-y), [Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2019.02.003)

- **Subcategory 1.5: Estimation requirements — what it costs to get a usable response-time-derived parameter**
  - **Source:** Lerche, Voss & Nagler (2017); Lerche & Voss (2017); Enkavi, Eisenberg, Bissett, Mazza, MacKinnon, Marsch & Poldrack (2019); Kang, De Boeck & Ratcliff (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In simulation studies spanning trial numbers from 24 to 5,000 and three optimisation criteria, maximum likelihood was superior for uncontaminated data while Kolmogorov–Smirnov outperformed the alternatives in the presence of fast contaminants, and chi-square was generally least precise. The authors report that "under certain conditions even small numbers of trials (N < 100) are sufficient for robust parameter estimation," with parsimonious three- and four-parameter models achievable at fewer than 100 trials and six- and seven-parameter models requiring more. The associated doctoral work states that increasing trials beyond 500 improves precision only marginally, and that fixing the intertrial variabilities of drift rate and starting point yields more reliable estimates of the four main parameters. *(Lerche, Voss & Nagler, 2017, *Behavior Research Methods* 49(2):513–537, DOI 10.3758/s13428-016-0740-2; Crossref records online-first 2016-06-10. Supporting statements: Lerche, 2016 dissertation, DOI 10.11588/heidok.00022279.)* `T2` and `T3` respectively
      - In two studies with sessions one week apart — 105 participants on a lexical decision and a recognition memory task, and 128 participants on an associative priming task — retest reliability of the main diffusion parameters was reported as satisfying, with r > 0.70 for drift rate and threshold separation in all three tasks. *(Lerche & Voss, 2017, *Psychological Research* 81(3):629–652, DOI 10.1007/s00426-016-0770-5; Crossref records online-first 2016-04-23.)* `T2`
      - A large retest study reported that self-report survey measures showed high test–retest reliability while measures derived from behavioural tasks did not, both in a systematic literature review and in a new sample, and attributed the difference to between-subject variability rather than to measurement noise alone. The authors also compared model-derived parameters against raw response times. *(Enkavi, Eisenberg, Bissett, Mazza, MacKinnon, Marsch & Poldrack, 2019, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 116(12):5472–5477, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1818430116. This study concerns self-regulation task batteries, not ability tests; specific reliability coefficients are not asserted here.)* `T2`
      - A diffusion-based item response model was published specifically to represent conditional dependence between response accuracy and response time. *(Kang, De Boeck & Ratcliff, 2022, *Psychometrika* 87(2):725–748, DOI 10.1007/s11336-021-09819-5 — title- and bibliographic-level verification only; no finding claimed.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The trial-count objection to evidence-accumulation modelling is weaker than commonly assumed, but only for deliberately simplified models: parsimonious specifications can recover parameters from under a hundred trials of a *binary* task, and the drift-rate estimates so obtained show week-to-week retest correlations above 0.70. Two constraints matter more than trial count. First, these results come from repeated homogeneous two-choice trials, not from heterogeneous curriculum items. Second, the broader literature on individual differences from behavioural tasks reports systematically weak retest reliability relative to self-report, which is a warning about the general enterprise of deriving stable person parameters from task behaviour rather than a specific indictment of diffusion modelling.
    - **Link to source:** [Trial-count requirements](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-016-0740-2), [Retest reliability of diffusion parameters](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-016-0770-5), [Task-measure reliability](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818430116)

- **Subcategory 1.6: Response time in adaptive testing, item selection, and time-limit management**
  - **Source:** van der Linden, Scrams & Schnipke (1999); van der Linden (2009); Fan, Wang, Chang & Douglas (2012); Choe, Kern & Chang (2018); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023); Wise & Kong (2005); Wise (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Response-time constraints were proposed as a mechanism for controlling *differential speededness* in computerised adaptive testing — the problem that an adaptive test can function as a speed test for some examinees and a power test for others. *(van der Linden, Scrams & Schnipke, 1999, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 23(3):195–210, DOI 10.1177/01466219922031329.)* `T2`
      - A predictive-control method for managing speededness during adaptive testing was subsequently published. *(van der Linden, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 33(1):25–41, DOI 10.1177/0146621607314042; Crossref records online-first 2008-03-19 with issue volume 33(1), commonly cited as 2009.)* `T2` `[COI: at around this period the author's listed affiliation on related work was a commercial test publisher]`
      - Two item-selection criteria using a lognormal response-time model were proposed: maximising information per unit of time, and an inverse-time-weighted stratification variant intended to give more balanced item exposure than the purely information-based approach. The stated motivation is that the most informative item at the current ability estimate may be very time-consuming and therefore not the most efficient item to administer. *(Fan, Wang, Chang & Douglas, 2012, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 37(5):655–670, DOI 10.3102/1076998611422912.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up study characterises the information-per-unit-time criterion as effectively reducing average completion time for a fixed-length test "with minimal decrease in the accuracy of ability estimation," but also as producing "extremely unbalanced exposure of items"; and it reports that the recommended exposure-control remedy comes "at substantial costs of attenuating the reduction in average testing time, increasing the variance of testing times, and further decreasing estimation accuracy." *(Choe, Kern & Chang, 2018, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 43(2):135–158, DOI 10.3102/1076998617723642; Crossref records online-first 2017-08-18.)* `T2`
      - A methodological review warns that once speed influences the ability estimate, a well-informed examinee can optimise speed to maximise the estimate — responding as slowly as the time limit permits when the speed–ability association is negative — and concludes that using the hierarchical model to improve precision of ability estimates in high-stakes settings "may be ill-advised," while being more defensible in low-stakes settings. The same review notes that in low-stakes settings the risk of heterogeneous and disengaged responding is *higher*, that disengaged responses will bias the estimated speed–ability correlation, and that "it is unlikely that any method will succeed in detecting disengaged responses with such a degree of accuracy that their presence no longer biases" that correlation. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - A response-time-based index of examinee effort ("response time effort") was introduced as a measure of motivation in computer-based tests, and rapid-guessing behaviour was later reviewed as an identifiable, interpretable phenomenon with measurement consequences. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2; Wise, 2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 36(4):52–61, DOI 10.1111/emip.12165.)* `T2` `[COI: the 2017 article lists the author's affiliation as NWEA, a commercial assessment provider; the 2005 article's affiliation was not verified in this pass]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** In adaptive delivery, the best-evidenced uses of response time are *operational* rather than psychometric: equalising how speeded a test feels across examinees, and buying back testing time by preferring items that yield information per second rather than information per item. Even that trade is not free — the time savings are purchased with unbalanced item exposure, and the standard fix for exposure gives back much of the time saving while further degrading estimation accuracy. There is also a direct incentive argument against letting response time enter a consequential score: if speed shifts the estimate, speed becomes gameable, and the review literature says so explicitly. Separately, response time has a well-established use in flagging non-effortful responding, which is information about *engagement*, not about ability.
    - **Link to source:** [Differential speededness](https://doi.org/10.1177/01466219922031329), [Information per unit time](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998611422912), [Costs of exposure control](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998617723642), [Rapid guessing](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12165)

- **Subcategory 1.7: Where response-time information is confounded — position, fatigue, reading load, motor speed, and age**
  - **Source:** Kail (1991); Ratcliff, Love, Thompson & Opfer (2012); Thompson, Ratcliff & McKoon (2016); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023); Debeer & Janssen (2013); Fox & Marianti (2016); Naumann & Goldhammer (2017); AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A review of 72 published studies yielding 1,826 paired response times (an adult condition mean paired with the corresponding younger-group mean) found that children's and adolescents' response times increase linearly as a function of adult response times in corresponding conditions, and that the amount of increase declines with age in a manner well described by an exponential function — consistent with a general, non-task-specific component of processing speed that changes rapidly in childhood and more slowly in adolescence. *(Kail, 1991, *Psychological Bulletin* 109(3):490–501, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.109.3.490; indexed as a review.)* `T1`
      - With 130 children (mean ages spanning 8.51–15.68 years) and 72 college-aged adults on numerosity discrimination and lexical decision, diffusion fits showed that *all* model components contributed to children's slower responses: children extracted lower-quality evidence (lower drift rate), boundary separation decreased significantly with age, and the nondecision component — which includes encoding, memory access, and response execution — also decreased significantly with age. Older adults, by contrast, extract evidence of similar quality to younger adults. *(Ratcliff, Love, Thompson & Opfer, 2012, *Child Development* 83(1):367–381, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8624.2011.01683.x.)* `T2`
      - In a developmental sample spanning first through eighth grade on symbolic and non-symbolic numeric comparison, individual participants' accuracy and response times were *uncorrelated*; accuracy was mainly determined by drift rate while response time was mainly determined by boundary separation, leading the authors to conclude that "RT and accuracy operate largely independently." Older participants were additionally faster at encoding, transforming the stimulus representation, and executing responses. *(Thompson, Ratcliff & McKoon, 2016, *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* 150:48–71, DOI 10.1016/j.jecp.2016.04.005.)* `T2`
      - A methodological review identifies non-stationarity of speed as a routine violation: examinees are known to spend relatively long on the first few items, speeding occurs near the end of tests with effective time limits, and when items are presented in booklets so that item position differs across examinees, positive residual dependencies between adjacent items remain even if every examinee shows an identical slow-start/fast-finish pattern. It also warns that construct-irrelevant influences on test performance — the review names test-taking experience and general reading skill — can affect expected response times, so that adding response time may *increase* the discrepancy between the estimated latent variable and the construct the test was intended to measure, even while increasing its apparent precision. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - Item-position effects on accuracy have been modelled explicitly within an item response framework. *(Debeer & Janssen, 2013, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 50(2):164–185, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12009 — title- and bibliographic-level verification only.)* `T2`
      - Joint modelling of ability together with *differential speed* — that is, allowing the latent speed of an examinee to change over the course of a test rather than assuming it is stationary — has been developed. *(Fox & Marianti, 2016, *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 51(4):540–553, DOI 10.1080/00273171.2016.1171128 — title- and bibliographic-level verification only.)* `T2`
      - Time-on-task effects in digital reading were reported to be moderated by task demands as well as person skills, in a study whose framing distinguishes routine from controlled processing. *(Naumann & Goldhammer, 2017, *Learning and Individual Differences* 53:1–16, DOI 10.1016/j.lindif.2016.10.002.)* `T2`
      - The joint professional testing standards require that score interpretations be validated for the specific intended use, and treat construct-irrelevant variance as a threat to validity. *(American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association & National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Washington, DC: AERA. No DOI; no ISBN asserted here because it was not verified in this pass, and the specific standard numbers were not confirmed against the text.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Any measured latency is a sum of a decision component, an encoding component, and a motor-execution component, and for children the age-related change in the non-decision components and in decision caution is as large and as systematic as the change in evidence quality. That is the sharpest form of the confounding problem: two children of identical ability can differ in measured time because one is more cautious or slower to read and press, and in at least one developmental sample individual accuracy and response time were statistically unrelated because they are governed by different parameters. Layered on top of that are position and fatigue effects that break the stationarity assumption the standard joint model relies on, and reading demand that loads on time independently of the target construct. The professional standards framing matters here because the failure mode is not noise — a confounded time signal can raise apparent precision while moving the estimate *away* from the construct.

#### Tensions and contradictions

- **Does the cross-loading extension add a lot or a little?** The same two authors state in a review that the cross-loading gain "may exceed the original gain" from adding speed, "especially for larger tests" (Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023), while the large empirical application they co-authored found the additional gain was "generally between 0.02 and 0.03" and "generally less sizable" than the accuracy-only-to-hierarchical step, concluding that "the biggest gain in precision was already obtained by using a simple-structure hierarchical model" (Reis Costa et al., 2021). The theoretical argument and the one large empirical test point in opposite directions. Note that the empirical test used only 10 items, which is exactly the short-test regime where the simple model is expected to do best — so the tension may be a test-length artefact rather than a genuine disagreement, but no one in these sources resolves it.
- **Is the worst performance rule real?** A meta-analysis of 19 studies and 3,767 participants reports "robust evidence" for it (Schubert, 2019), while a preregistered, analyst-blinded study of 916 participants found evidence *for the null* (Bayes factors 6.29 and 19.9 favouring absence) and explicitly raised publication bias as an explanation for the meta-analytic picture (Dutilh et al., 2017). A meta-analysis inherits the selection biases of its input literature; a single preregistered study inherits the idiosyncrasies of one task and sample. Both authors independently converge on the same *mechanism* (drift rate), which makes this a disagreement about the raw-quantile phenomenon, not about evidence accumulation.
- **Does response time make the estimate better or just tighter?** van der Linden, Klein Entink & Fox (2010) report that response times improved *both* accuracy and bias — unusual for a hierarchical borrowing-strength method. Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023) argue the opposite risk: because ability now depends on speed, a misspecified speed–ability relationship introduces bias that an accuracy-only model would not have had, and the resulting increase in precision may come "at the cost of increasing the discrepancy" between the latent variable and the intended construct. Precision and validity are pulling apart, and the sources disagree about which direction dominates.
- **Do accuracy and time carry the same information or different information?** The diffusion framing says they are two projections of one process (Ratcliff, Schmiedek & McKoon, 2008), which is why drift rate can be recovered from their joint distribution. But in a developmental sample, individual accuracy and response time were *uncorrelated*, because accuracy tracked drift rate while time tracked boundary separation (Thompson, Ratcliff & McKoon, 2016). Both can be true simultaneously, but they imply very different expectations about how much a time measurement adds once accuracy is known.
- **Low stakes as the safe harbour, or the dangerous one?** Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023) argue that using speed to sharpen ability estimates is more defensible in low-stakes settings because a little bias is tolerable — and then, in the same section, that low-stakes settings carry the greatest risk of disengaged and heterogeneous responding, which is precisely what biases the speed–ability correlation, and that no detection method is likely to be accurate enough to eliminate the problem. The recommended safe harbour contains the recommended hazard.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each item below is a starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate, test, and either own or discard. None of these is a finding, and none of them is yet the author's position.*

1. **Starter hypothesis: the response-time precision gain may be worth roughly a modest increase in test length, and that is the honest unit to argue in.** The published figures are reliability 0.73 → 0.80 and average standard error 0.51 → 0.45 on a 10-item test (Reis Costa et al., 2021). *Author's own arithmetic, to be checked:* applying the Spearman–Brown relation, moving a reliability of 0.73 to 0.80 corresponds to roughly 1.5 times the test length — about five more items on a ten-item test. That framing makes the trade concrete and falsifiable. It also has two caveats the author must resolve before using it: expected-a-posteriori reliability is not classical reliability, and Spearman–Brown assumes parallel added items. If the arithmetic survives scrutiny, the interesting question is whether the modelling complexity, the bias risk, and the confound exposure documented in subcategories 1.6 and 1.7 are a better deal than simply asking a handful of additional questions.
2. **Starter hypothesis: the strongest evidence-based case for response time may be about *engagement and process*, not about *ability*.** The best-replicated operational uses in these sources are detecting non-effortful responding (Wise & Kong, 2005; Wise, 2017), equalising speededness across examinees (van der Linden, Scrams & Schnipke, 1999), and buying testing time (Fan et al., 2012), while the ability-precision gain is modest and bounded (Ranger, 2013, as reported by two citing sources; Reis Costa et al., 2021). If the author pursues this, the claim to test is that response time's incremental value is highest for *validity screening* — deciding whether a response should count at all — and lowest for *score sharpening*. The counter-evidence to hunt for is any study showing a large ability-precision gain that is not attributable to filtering out disengaged responses.
3. **Starter hypothesis: for children, the non-decision and caution components may dominate the ability component enough to invert the usual reasoning.** Children differ from adults on *every* diffusion component including boundary separation and nondecision time (Ratcliff, Love, Thompson & Opfer, 2012), overall developmental speed change is well described by a single general age function across many tasks (Kail, 1991), and in one developmental sample accuracy and time were uncorrelated because they were driven by different parameters (Thompson, Ratcliff & McKoon, 2016). The hypothesis to interrogate is that in a developing population a raw time signal is closer to a measure of caution, reading fluency, and motor execution than of ability — and therefore that any use of it must either model those components explicitly or be restricted to within-person comparisons where they are held roughly constant. The author should look specifically for studies that estimate how much of the between-child variance in latency is non-decision variance, since none of the sources here quantifies that share directly.
4. **Starter hypothesis: the "collateral information" argument and the "construct-irrelevant variance" argument may be the same mechanism viewed from two ends.** Response time helps sharpen the estimate exactly to the extent that speed correlates with whatever the score is tracking (Ranger, 2013, as reported by citing sources), but if part of what the score tracks is construct-irrelevant — test-taking experience, reading skill — then borrowing strength from speed imports more of that irrelevant part (Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023). The testable version: the size of the precision gain and the size of the construct-contamination risk should be *positively* related, not independent. If that holds, then a large observed precision gain is a warning sign rather than a success metric, which is close to the opposite of how such gains are usually reported.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **Nothing in this category addresses learning rate.** Every source located here targets a static ability or trait estimate. No evidence was found in this pass that response time adds incremental information about a *rate of change* or growth parameter. Searches for peer-reviewed evidence that response-time features improve student-model or knowledge-tracing predictions beyond correctness surfaced only low-quality or preprint material that did not meet the citation bar, so no claim — positive or negative — is recorded. This is the single largest gap relative to the organising question and should be treated as unresolved rather than as a null result.
- **The quantitative incremental-validity evidence rests on very few applications.** The specific numbers reported here (reliability 0.73/0.77/0.80; standard errors 0.51/0.49/0.45; cross-loading gains of 0.02–0.03) come from a *single* study on 10 items of one mathematics assessment (Reis Costa et al., 2021). No meta-analysis of incremental precision gains from joint response-time modelling was located. Any general claim about "how much response time adds" is currently a one-study claim.
- **Ranger (2013) was not read directly.** Its bibliographic record was verified against Crossref, but the substantive results attributed to it — that the gain is bounded by the speed–ability correlation and that marginal gains decay with test length — were read through two academic citing sources (one journal article and one edited-volume chapter). Both cite it consistently, but the original derivation and any conditions attached to it were not inspected.
- **Effect sizes for the drift-rate/intelligence link vary widely and one is unstable.** The reported figural drift–figural intelligence correlation of r = .90 is disclaimed by its own authors because the residual variance was not significantly different from zero (Lerche et al., 2020). The domain-specific values (.74 numeric, .50 verbal) and the psychometric-speed-to-drift value (.59) are the ones to rely on. Independent replication of the domain-general/domain-specific pattern was not located in this pass.
- **The most relevant paradigm mismatch is unmeasured.** All diffusion estimation guidance located here (Lerche, Voss & Nagler, 2017; Lerche & Voss, 2017) comes from many repeated trials of homogeneous *binary* tasks. No source was found establishing whether drift rate can be recovered with comparable reliability from a short sequence of heterogeneous curriculum items with more than two response options. Trial-count feasibility claims should not be transferred across that boundary without evidence.
- **One of the five conditional-independence assumptions has apparently never been tested.** Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023) state that residual dependence between accuracy and latent speed has not been formally studied in real data and that no detection test exists. Model fit therefore cannot currently be claimed as complete for the standard joint framework.
- **Device, input modality, and mode confounds are asserted here only indirectly.** Targeted searches did not surface a peer-reviewed study isolating the effect of input device (touch versus mouse versus keyboard) on measured item response times in child assessment. The motor-and-encoding confound is supported here only through the diffusion nondecision-time findings (Ratcliff et al., 2012; Thompson et al., 2016), which show that the non-decision component varies systematically with age but do not decompose it by device. This should be flagged as a reasoned inference from adjacent evidence, not a documented finding.
- **The professional-standards citation is at the level of general principle.** The joint testing standards were cited for the requirement to validate score interpretations for their intended use and for the treatment of construct-irrelevant variance as a validity threat, but specific standard numbers, edition pagination, and ISBN were not verified against the source text in this pass.
- **Publication bias is an open question, raised by the sources themselves.** Dutilh et al. (2017) explicitly suggest that the apparent ubiquity of the worst performance rule may partly reflect selective reporting, and state that this can only be resolved by additional large-scale preregistered studies. No such follow-up replication was located here.
- **COI coverage is incomplete.** Commercial affiliations were confirmed via indexed metadata for two of the sources cited (a commercial test publisher for one modelling paper; a commercial assessment provider for the rapid-guessing work). Affiliations for several other cited studies were not checked, so absence of a COI tag in this document should not be read as confirmation of independence.
