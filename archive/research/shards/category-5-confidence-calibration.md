### Category 5: Confidence, metacognition and calibration — what the extra rating actually buys

*General idea: Asking a test-taker "how sure are you?" is the cheapest supplementary signal available, and it is the one with the deepest measurement literature behind it. That literature is also the one most contaminated by its own arithmetic. Almost every popular calibration index is a function of accuracy, so an apparent "added" signal can be a rearrangement of the score you already had; the field's own methodologists spent two decades building bias-free alternatives precisely because of this. Where incremental validity over accuracy has been measured properly, it is real but small and inconsistent across criteria — positive on some outcomes, exactly zero on others in the same study. The trait interpretation is weaker still: within-person accuracy of monitoring has poor test–retest and cross-task stability, in adults and in children. Young children can produce confidence judgments that discriminate correct from incorrect answers from about age three to five, so feasibility is not the binding constraint; validity and stability are. And the act of asking is not free: eliciting confidence measurably changes the primary task, in at least one large pre-registered study by suppressing rule-based learning.*

- **Subcategory 5.1: What is actually being measured — the judgment types are not interchangeable**
  - **Source:** Nelson & Narens (1990), as quoted in Mitchum, Kelley & Fox (2016); Rhodes & Tauber (2011); Fleming & Lau (2014); Destan, Hembacher, Ghetti & Roebers (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The standard vocabulary of this literature — monitoring versus control, and the specific judgment types — traces to a 1990 chapter titled "Metamemory: A Theoretical Framework and New Findings"; a later peer-reviewed paper attributes to it the characterisation of judgments of learning as "conscious reflection on one's own memory performance or encoding activities for the purpose of exerting strategic control over one's study and retrieval activities (T. O. Nelson & Narens, 1990)." *(Mitchum, Kelley & Fox, 2016, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 145(2):200–219, DOI 10.1037/a0039923, quoting Nelson & Narens, 1990, *Psychology of Learning and Motivation* 26:125–173, DOI 10.1016/S0079-7421(08)60053-5.)* `T2` — *note: the Crossref record for the 1990 chapter lists only Nelson as author; the conventional citation is Nelson & Narens.*
      - **Timing changes the measure, not just its precision.** A meta-analysis of the delayed judgment-of-learning effect covering 4,554 participants and 112 effect sizes, using gamma correlations between judgments and memory accuracy, found that delaying the judgment rather than making it immediately after study "leads to robust benefits to relative accuracy (g = 0.93)." *(Rhodes & Tauber, 2011, *Psychological Bulletin* 137(1):131–148, DOI 10.1037/a0021705.)* `T2`
      - A second meta-analysis in the same paper, over 3,807 participants and 98 effect sizes, found that delayed judgments also produce "a modest but reliable benefit for memory performance relative to immediate JOLs (g = 0.08)" — i.e. the judgment procedure itself has a small effect on the thing being judged. *(Rhodes & Tauber, 2011, *Psychological Bulletin* 137(1):131–148, DOI 10.1037/a0021705.)* `T2`
      - A methodological review distinguishes three things that are routinely conflated under the word "metacognition": **metacognitive bias** ("a difference in subjective confidence despite basic task performance remaining constant"), **metacognitive sensitivity** ("how good one is at distinguishing between one's own correct and incorrect judgments") and **metacognitive efficiency** ("a subject's level of metacognitive sensitivity given a certain level of task performance"). *(Fleming & Lau, 2014, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 8:443, DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2014.00443.)* `T2`
      - Prospective and retrospective judgments drive different downstream behaviour and mature on different schedules. In 5-, 6- and 7-year-olds (N = 101), judgments of learning given after study and confidence judgments given after the memory test were compared alongside their corresponding control actions (study-time allocation and answer selection); the paper concludes that "some metacognitive judgments may be acted on with greater ease than others among young children." *(Destan, Hembacher, Ghetti & Roebers, 2014, *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* 126:213–228, DOI 10.1016/j.jecp.2014.04.001.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** "Confidence" is not one measurement. A prospective judgment made before or during study, a prospective judgment made after a delay, a feeling-of-knowing about material you cannot currently retrieve, and a retrospective confidence rating attached to an answer you have just given are four different quantities with four different accuracy profiles. The single largest known moderator in the whole area is not the person but the *schedule*: moving a judgment from immediately-after-study to delayed changes relative accuracy by nearly a full standard deviation. That is larger than most of the person-level effects reported anywhere in this category, which means any design that fixes a judgment timing has already made the most consequential decision, usually without knowing it. The review literature also insists on separating bias (how confident, on average), sensitivity (how well confidence tracks correctness) and efficiency (sensitivity adjusted for how good you were at the task) — and these three come apart empirically, so a single "calibration score" is under-specified until you say which of the three it is.
    - **Link to source:** [Rhodes & Tauber 2011 delayed-JOL meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021705) · [Fleming & Lau 2014, "How to measure metacognition"](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00443)

- **Subcategory 5.2: Calibration metrics and their arithmetic — the confound with accuracy is the central technical fact**
  - **Source:** Murphy (1973); Masson & Rotello (2009); Maniscalco & Lau (2012); Barrett, Dienes & Seth (2013); Fleming & Lau (2014); Juslin, Winman & Olsson (2000); Schraw, Kuch & Gutierrez (2013); Schraw, Kuch, Gutierrez & Richmond (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The decomposition underlying every "calibration versus resolution" discussion originates in a 1973 meteorology paper, "A New Vector Partition of the Probability Score," which partitions the Brier probability score into components. *(Murphy, 1973, *Journal of Applied Meteorology* 12(4):595–600, DOI 10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012<0595:ANVPOT>2.0.CO;2.)* `T2` — *only the bibliographic record was verified; the paper's text was not obtained, so the specific algebraic form of the partition is deliberately not stated here.*
      - The default relative-accuracy statistic in this field is the Goodman–Kruskal gamma correlation between judgment and outcome, used as the effect metric in the largest meta-analysis of judgment accuracy. *(Rhodes & Tauber, 2011, DOI 10.1037/a0021705; the canonical methodological comparison is Nelson, 1984, "A comparison of current measures of the accuracy of feeling-of-knowing predictions," *Psychological Bulletin* 95(1):109–133, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.95.1.109, whose bibliographic record is verified but whose text was not obtained.)* `T2`
      - **Gamma is biased.** "The authors demonstrate that the empirically determined value of G systematically deviates from its actual value under realistic conditions"; a distribution-specific variant is introduced to explain why the bias arises, and the paper concludes that "caution is needed when using G as a measure of accuracy, and alternative measures are recommended." *(Masson & Rotello, 2009, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 35(2):509–527, DOI 10.1037/a0014876.)* `T2`
      - **The explicit statement of the accuracy confound.** "We argue that currently available methods are inadequate because they are influenced by factors such as response bias and type 1 sensitivity (i.e. ability to distinguish stimuli)" — type 1 sensitivity being, in a knowledge test, simply how good the person is at the task. The proposed fix, meta-d′, is "a method of measuring type 2 sensitivity that is free from these confounds." *(Maniscalco & Lau, 2012, *Consciousness and Cognition* 21(1):422–430, DOI 10.1016/j.concog.2011.09.021.)* `T2` — *interest note: the authors also distribute the Matlab implementation of the measure they propose.*
      - A formal analysis of the same problem states that "approaches based on Type II receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves or Type II d-prime risk confounding metacognition with response biases in either the Type I (classification) or Type II (metacognitive) tasks," and reports that while meta-d′ values "typically reflect the underlying model well and are stable under changes in decision criteria," "in relatively extreme cases, meta-d' can become unstable." *(Barrett, Dienes & Seth, 2013, *Psychological Methods* 18(4):535–552, DOI 10.1037/a0033268.)* `T2`
      - The corollary definition: metacognitive **efficiency** exists as a separate construct precisely because sensitivity is not interpretable without conditioning on task performance — it is defined as sensitivity "given a certain level of task performance." The same review warns that "many studies use a statistical correlation coefficient (e.g., Pearson's r) or its variant to assess this degree of association, and such measures are susceptible to undesirable influences from factors such as response biases." *(Fleming & Lau, 2014, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 8:443, DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2014.00443.)* `T2`
      - **The flagship "bias" finding may itself be an artifact of accuracy.** A quantitative review of two-alternative general-knowledge studies argued that the hard–easy effect "has been interpreted with insufficient attention to the scale-end effects, the linear dependency, and the regression effects in data," and reported "(a) very little support for a cognitive-processing bias in these data" and "(c) near elimination of the hard-easy effect when there is control for scale-end effects and linear dependency." *(Juslin, Winman & Olsson, 2000, *Psychological Review* 107(2):384–396, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.107.2.384.)* `T2`
      - **No single calibration index is sufficient.** A confirmatory factor analysis of ten calibration measures computed on one common data set, spanning "five interpretative families of measures used to assess monitoring accuracy based on a 2 (performance) × 2 (monitoring judgment) contingency table," found a two-factor solution corresponding to sensitivity and specificity, "yielding two orthogonal factors that explained close to 100% of sample variance," with the eight remaining measures explained to between 91 and 99 percent by those two factors. The authors conclude: "no single measure explains all the variance in monitoring judgments." *(Schraw, Kuch & Gutierrez, 2013, *Learning and Instruction* 24:48–57, DOI 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2012.08.007.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up comparing five statistics (G index, gamma, d′, sensitivity, specificity) computed from the same raw scores across vocabulary, probability and paper-folding tests found a three-factor model best fitting, with sensitivity and specificity "subsumed under a general 3rd-order discrimination process as measured by d′." *(Schraw, Kuch, Gutierrez & Richmond, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(4):1192–1202, DOI 10.1037/a0036653.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the subcategory that decides the whole question, and its answer is uncomfortable. The most widely used calibration indices are arithmetically entangled with the accuracy they are supposed to supplement. Signed bias — mean confidence minus proportion correct — is a linear function of the score by construction. Gamma is demonstrably biased under realistic conditions. Type-II d′ and type-II ROC measures confound metacognition with both first-order ability and response bias, which is the stated reason meta-d′ exists at all, and the entire concept of "metacognitive efficiency" is an admission that sensitivity is uninterpretable unless you condition on performance. The single most-cited "metacognitive bias" phenomenon in the literature, the hard–easy effect, largely disappears once scale-end effects and linear dependency are controlled. Two independent factor analyses of the measurement space agree that no one index captures monitoring, and that at minimum you need separate accounts of judgments about correct answers and judgments about incorrect answers. The practical implication is blunt: any claim that a calibration score "adds information beyond accuracy" is not evaluable unless the index used is one that has been explicitly de-confounded from accuracy, and most reported indices are not.
    - **Link to source:** [Maniscalco & Lau 2012 on meta-d′ and the type-1 sensitivity confound](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2011.09.021) · [Juslin et al. 2000 on the hard–easy effect as artifact](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.107.2.384) · [Schraw et al. 2013 factor analysis of ten calibration scores](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2012.08.007)

- **Subcategory 5.3: Confidence-weighted and certainty-based scoring — fifty years of trying, with null results and a fairness problem**
  - **Source:** Echternacht (1972); Ben-Simon, Budescu & Nevo (1997); Hassmén & Hunt (1994); Smrkolj, Bančov & Smrkolj (2022); Wu, Vanerum, Agten, Christiansen, Vandenabeele, Rigo & Janssen (2021); Baldiga (2014); Gardner-Medwin (1995/2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The formal definition, from the field's founding review: "Confidence testing is a method of testing where weights are assigned directly or indirectly to item responses in such a way as to reflect the examinee's belief in the correctness of the alternative or alternatives so marked." The same review notes the countervailing property of plain scoring: "More items can be administered in a given period of time using this method than by any other method requiring a more complicated response, and the cost for scoring the test is also less." *(Echternacht, 1972, *Review of Educational Research* 42(2):217–236, DOI 10.3102/00346543042002217.)* `T2` `[COI: the author was affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation, which published a technical-report version of the same work, DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.1971.tb00614.x]`
      - **The definitive comparative trial found no winner.** Seven partial-knowledge testing methods were applied to multiple-choice tests in four content areas in a between-persons design and evaluated against six criteria; the results "indicated that, on average, high ability examinees were better judges of their level of knowledge and, consequently, could benefit more from these methods," and that "systematic interactions were found between the testing method and the test content, indicating that no method was uniformly superior." *(Ben-Simon, Budescu & Nevo, 1997, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 21(1):65–88, DOI 10.1177/0146621697211006.)* `T2`
      - **Null result on score distribution.** In a certainty-based-marking study of a five-week gynaecology and obstetrics course, CBM "does not change the general distribution of the exam scores, since there is no significant differences between Certainty-Based Score (M = 80.4%, SD = 10.4%) and Accuracy (M = 79.8%, SD = 11.1%); t(176) = 0.8327, p = 0.4061," and CBM "does not discriminate any learner type (p = 0.932)." *(Smrkolj, Bančov & Smrkolj, 2022, *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* 19(3):1706, DOI 10.3390/ijerph19031706.)* `T2` `[Interest note: the authors evaluated CBM in their own course]`
      - **The fairness objection, quantified.** Integrating item response theory with cumulative prospect theory across six CBM examinations taken by 334 first-year physiotherapy students, "the results showed that with the given CBM scoring matrix, students' choices of a certainty level were affected by their risk attitudes. Students were generally risk averse and loss averse when they had a high success probability on an item, leading to an under-reporting of their certainty. Meanwhile, they were risk seeking in case of small success probabilities on the items, resulting in the over-reporting of certainty." *(Wu, Vanerum, Agten, Christiansen, Vandenabeele, Rigo & Janssen, 2021, *Psychometrika* 86(2):518–543, DOI 10.1007/s11336-021-09759-0.)* `T2` `[Interest note: the CBM examinations analysed were run by the authors' own institution]`
      - **Risk penalty transfers into scores along demographic lines.** In an experiment using SAT II history practice questions, "when no penalty is assessed for a wrong answer, all test takers answer every question. But, when there is a penalty for wrong answers, women answer significantly fewer questions than men. We see no differences in knowledge of the material or confidence in the test takers, and differences in risk preferences explain less than half of the observed gap." Skipping was costly: "test takers who skip questions do significantly worse on our test," conditional on knowledge. *(Baldiga, 2014, *Management Science* 60(2):434–448, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2013.1776.)* `T2`
      - **Counter-evidence on the direction of the gender effect.** An earlier study reported the opposite sign for a self-assessment (rather than penalty) format: "Results show that the difference between test scores of males and those of females was reduced when subjects were allowed to make self-assessments," with the authors framing self-assessment as a route to "a more comprehensive measurement of usable knowledge." *(Hassmén & Hunt, 1994, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 31(2):149–160, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1994.tb00440.x.)* `T2`
      - The main modern advocacy case is stated in terms of guessing control rather than validity: automated assessment "seldom makes use of information about how confident a student is in the answer given," and "often involves the construction of complex questions to ensure that students cannot get good marks by a combination of partial knowledge and guesswork," which are "time-consuming" to create. *(Gardner-Medwin, *Research in Learning Technology* 3(1), DOI 10.3402/rlt.v3i1.9597; the record carries a 2011 re-publication date for an article originally issued in 1995.)* `T2` `[COI: the author is the originator and long-term promoter of the certainty-based marking scheme and its associated courseware, and is evaluating his own method]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Confidence weighting has been tried continuously since the 1960s and has never established a durable advantage. The largest head-to-head comparison — seven methods, four content domains, six evaluation criteria — concluded that no method was uniformly superior and that the methods interacted with test content. The most recent controlled application found the certainty-weighted score statistically indistinguishable from plain accuracy on the same exam, which is the cleanest possible null: the added response cost bought no change in the distribution being used to make decisions. Meanwhile the fairness objection is not speculative arm-waving. A psychometric-plus-decision-theoretic analysis of real CBM exams shows that reported certainty is systematically distorted by risk attitude in *both* directions — under-reported when the student is likely right, over-reported when likely wrong — which means the certainty channel carries a risk-preference signal mixed in with the knowledge signal. When a scoring rule penalises wrong high-confidence answers, that risk-preference signal converts into score differences between demographic groups even when knowledge and confidence are equal. There is a genuine countervailing finding — one 1994 study found a self-assessment format *narrowed* a male–female score gap — but the mechanisms differ (self-assessment without penalty versus penalised certainty), and nothing located here reconciles them.
    - **Link to source:** [Ben-Simon, Budescu & Nevo 1997 comparative study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621697211006) · [Wu et al. 2021, CBM meets decision theory](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-021-09759-0) · [Baldiga 2014 on penalties and willingness to guess](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1776)

- **Subcategory 5.4: Incremental validity over accuracy alone — the honest effect sizes, and the raw-versus-partialled distinction**
  - **Source:** Stankov & Lee (2008); Ohtani & Hisasaka (2018), partly via Muncer et al. (2022); Dent & Koenka (2016), via Muncer et al. (2022); Stankov, Morony & Lee (2013); Veenman & Spaans (2005); Mathabathe & Potgieter (2014); Kolovelonis & Goudas (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The single most directly relevant result located.** Confidence ratings were collected during administration of the Reading and Listening sections of a large operational English-proficiency test to 824 native speakers, and correlated against accuracy on that test, an admissions test, high-school GPA, and personality and metacognition measures. "The results of factor analyses indicate that confidence is a separate psychological trait, somewhere between ability and personality," and "confidence is related to, but separate from, metacognition." *(Stankov & Lee, 2008, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 100(4):961–976, DOI 10.1037/a0012546.)* `T2` `[Potential COI, unverified: the analysis uses a large commercial testing organisation's operational instrument; the article's funding statement was not retrieved here]`
      - **The measured incremental validity, and it is explicitly described as small.** "The data show small incremental validity of the confidence scores above and beyond the accuracy scores in predicting performance on the TOEFL iBT as a whole, the Writing and Speaking sections of the TOEFL iBT, and a test of numeracy." *(Stankov & Lee, 2008, DOI 10.1037/a0012546.)* `T2`
      - **The matched null, from the same study and the same sample.** "Confidence does not show incremental validity for the SAT and HS-GPA." *(Stankov & Lee, 2008, DOI 10.1037/a0012546.)* `T2`
      - The same study reported group differences in the confidence signal itself: "Gender and ethnic differences in confidence are also reported, with men and African Americans showing higher overconfidence bias than women and Whites or Hispanics, respectively." *(Stankov & Lee, 2008, DOI 10.1037/a0012546.)* `T2`
      - **Meta-analytic magnitudes, raw.** A meta-analysis of 149 samples from 118 articles found metacognition correlated r = .28, 95% CI [.24, .31] with academic performance and r = .33, 95% CI [.26, .39] with intelligence; the same source reports online (concurrent, task-embedded) measures at r = .53, 90% CI [.45, .61] against offline (questionnaire) measures at r = .23, 90% CI [.20, .26]. *(Ohtani & Hisasaka, 2018, *Metacognition and Learning* 13(2):179–212, DOI 10.1007/s11409-018-9183-8, as reported and quoted in Muncer, Higham, Gosling, Cortese, Wood-Downie & Hadwin, 2022, *Educational Psychology Review* 34(1):301–334, DOI 10.1007/s10648-021-09620-x; Crossref issued date for the latter is 2021, online-first.)* `T2`
      - The originating meta-analysis' own abstract characterises the same numbers as weak and reports the partialled conclusion without a coefficient: "overall, metacognition weakly correlated with both academic performance and intelligence, and that these relationships were moderated by the type of measurement of metacognition. Furthermore, it was found that metacognition predicted academic performance when controlling for intelligence." *(Ohtani & Hisasaka, 2018, DOI 10.1007/s11409-018-9183-8.)* `T2`
      - A second, independent meta-analysis restricted to school-age children and adolescents (ages 6–19, 61 studies) reported metacognition–achievement at r = .20, 95% CI [.16, .24], with the same online-versus-offline split (online r = .39 [.34, .43]; offline r = .15 [.12, .18]). *(Dent & Koenka, 2016, *Educational Psychology Review* 28(3):425–474, DOI 10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8, as reported in Muncer et al., 2022, DOI 10.1007/s10648-021-09620-x.)* `T2`
      - **An example of the raw/incremental conflation to guard against.** A later paper reports that "confidence accounts for 46.3% of total variance in achievement, while measures of previous cognitive performance in combination with other non-cognitive variables account for 40.5% of the total variance." This is a variance-share statement about a set of predictors, not an estimate of confidence's unique contribution after accuracy is partialled out, and it is routinely cited as though it were the latter. *(Stankov, Morony & Lee, 2013, *Educational Psychology* 34(1):9–28, DOI 10.1080/01443410.2013.814194.)* `T2`
      - In the process-tracing tradition, metacognitive skillfulness assessed by think-aloud observation and computer logs among first- and third-year secondary students "outweighs intelligence as predictor of learning performance," supporting a "mixed model" in which metacognition is correlated with but partly independent of intellectual ability. *(Veenman & Spaans, 2005, *Learning and Individual Differences* 15(2):159–176, DOI 10.1016/j.lindif.2004.12.001, abstract as indexed by the publisher and ERIC.)* `T2` — *the widely circulated "17% unique variance for metacognition versus 10% for intelligence" figures could not be verified from the article itself; see evidence gaps.*
      - **Learning gain, positive but with a crucial ordering caveat.** In a pre/post stoichiometry study in a South African foundation programme (year 1 N = 91; replication year N = 300), students were grouped by whether they were overconfident (OC) or realistic (R) before and after instruction. In the replication year, mean normalised learning gains were 53% for OC→R, 43% for R→R, 32% for OC→OC and 15% for R→OC. *(Mathabathe & Potgieter, 2014, *Chemistry Education Research and Practice* 15(1):94–104, DOI 10.1039/C3RP00119A.)* `T1`
      - In that study the groups were established to be comparable beforehand: "The results indicated that there was no statistical difference among pretest performances of the subgroups at a 5% level of significance," and post hoc tests found no significant pairwise differences on the prerequisite module. Differences in posttest performance (p = 0.001) and learning gain (p < 0.001) were significant. *(Mathabathe & Potgieter, 2014, DOI 10.1039/C3RP00119A.)* `T1`
      - **But the pre-instruction calibration measurement — the one a screener could actually take — did not predict gain.** "An initial positive bias in performance evaluation may actually be beneficial to learning. Inaccuracy in self-evaluation in the pretest did not hamper learning for both the OC–OC and OC–R subgroups, but when overconfidence persisted despite teaching and learning (OC–OC) or developed upon exposure to subject content (R–OC) it had serious consequences." The discriminating variable was calibration *after* instruction, not before. *(Mathabathe & Potgieter, 2014, DOI 10.1039/C3RP00119A.)* `T1`
      - **A longitudinal test with small effects and one clear null.** In two studies of undergraduate physical education students (N = 68 and N = 112), regressions of achievement on local and global calibration accuracy gave R² = .10 (F(2, 67) = 3.47, p = .037) and R² = .12 (F(2, 67) = 4.49, p = .015) for mid- and end-of-semester knowledge tests, and R² = .09 (F(2, 101) = 4.98, p = .009) for degree grade four or more years later; calibration did not significantly predict time to graduation (F(2, 111) = 0.85, p = .428). *(Kolovelonis & Goudas, 2022, *Issues in Educational Research* 32(2), open-access PDF read in full, [https://www.iier.org.au/iier32/kolovelonis.pdf](https://www.iier.org.au/iier32/kolovelonis.pdf).)* `T3` — *no DOI; regional open-access journal; and critically, these regressions contain calibration terms only, with no prior-ability covariate, so they are raw not incremental estimates.*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Separate the three claims that get merged. **Raw association:** metacognition-type measures correlate with achievement somewhere around r = .20 to .28 overall, rising to about r = .39–.53 for task-embedded ("online") measures and falling to about r = .15–.23 for questionnaire measures — and note that the high figure belongs to the measurement mode that includes calibration-style indices, which are the ones most confounded with accuracy. **Partialled association:** the only study located that ran a clean incremental-validity test with confidence measured on the same items as accuracy found the increment "small" and, decisively, found it on some criteria and *exactly none* on others — no incremental validity at all over accuracy for the admissions test or for high-school GPA, in the same sample. That pattern is what you would expect if the confidence channel carries a modest amount of real extra signal that is mostly redundant with whatever the criterion already shares with accuracy. **Learning gain:** there is more here than in adjacent categories, but it points against a one-shot screener. In the best-controlled pre/post study located, groups with equivalent prior knowledge but different *pre-instruction* calibration learned equally well; what separated them was whether calibration improved *during* instruction. Overconfidence measured before teaching was explicitly described as not debilitating. In other words: the longitudinal signal appears to live in the *change* in calibration under instruction, which by construction cannot be captured by a single administration.
    - **Link to source:** [Stankov & Lee 2008 on confidence and incremental validity](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012546) · [Ohtani & Hisasaka 2018 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-018-9183-8) · [Mathabathe & Potgieter 2014 on calibration shift and learning gain](https://doi.org/10.1039/C3RP00119A)

- **Subcategory 5.5: Metacognition as a trait — the general-factor question and the reliability problem underneath it**
  - **Source:** Kelemen, Frost & Weaver (2000); Schraw, Dunkle, Bendixen & Roedel (1995); Rouault, McWilliams, Allen & Fleming (2018); Mazancieux, Fleming, Souchay & Moulin (2020); Eberhart, Murayama, Sakaki & Bryce (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The core negative result, and it is about reliability rather than theory.** Four common metacognitive tasks (ease-of-learning judgments, feeling-of-knowing judgments, judgments of learning, and text comprehension monitoring) were administered twice with a one-week interval, and alternate-forms correlations computed. "Although individual differences in memory and confidence were stable across both sessions and tasks, differences in metacognitive accuracy were not. These results pose considerable practical and theoretical challenges for metacognitive researchers." *(Kelemen, Frost & Weaver, 2000, *Memory & Cognition* 28(1):92–107, DOI 10.3758/BF03211579.)* `T2`
      - **A partial positive, with the same asymmetry.** Across eight different knowledge domains, "college students' performance and discrimination accuracy were not correlated across 8 different domains, whereas confidence and judgment bias were." With tests matched on all salient dimensions except content, everything correlated, and "confidence was correlated even after the effect of performance was removed." The authors describe this as "qualified support for the domain-general hypothesis." *(Schraw, Dunkle, Bendixen & Roedel, 1995, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 87(3):433–444, DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.87.3.433.)* `T2`
      - **Meta-analytic verdict: partly domain-general at best.** "A meta-analysis of behavioral studies found that perceptual metacognitive ability was correlated across different sensory modalities, but found no correlation between metacognition of perception and memory." The authors add that "evidence for domain-generality from behavioral data may suffer from a lack of power to identify correlations across model parameters indexing metacognitive efficiency." *(Rouault, McWilliams, Allen & Fleming, 2018, *Personality Neuroscience* 1, DOI 10.1017/pen.2018.16.)* `T2`
      - **Directly contradicting result, two years later.** Correlations in retrospective metacognitive sensitivity across tasks "were significant, even for pairs of tasks in which first-order performance was not correlated. This suggests that at least some components of metacognitive efficiency in retrospective confidence are domain general." *(Mazancieux, Fleming, Souchay & Moulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(9):1788–1799, DOI 10.1037/xge0000746.)* `T2`
      - **In children, the negative result reappears.** Among 53 elementary school children assessed with four monitoring tasks, "correlational analysis showed that there was no significant association between children's monitoring judgment accuracies," while the *frequency* of monitoring behaviours on two construction tasks was significantly positively associated. The authors conclude that "the processes underlying children's monitoring judgments may be task specific, whereas their tendency to engage in monitoring behaviours may be domain general." *(Eberhart, Murayama, Sakaki & Bryce, 2025, *Cognitive Development* 74:101561, DOI 10.1016/j.cogdev.2025.101561.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is a live and unresolved dispute about whether a general metacognitive ability exists, but the dispute is downstream of a more damaging fact: monitoring-accuracy scores are unreliable. When the same people were measured twice a week apart on four standard metacognitive tasks, their memory scores and their raw confidence levels replicated and their monitoring accuracy did not. Every claim about metacognition-as-a-trait has to survive that ceiling, and low reliability is by itself sufficient to produce weak cross-task correlations — which is exactly the pattern the meta-analytic review flags as a possible power artifact. The same asymmetry appears in three independent studies across twenty-five years and across adults and children: *confidence level* behaves like a stable person characteristic, while *confidence accuracy* does not. That is a specific and actionable finding. If a person-level readout is wanted, mean confidence is the psychometrically better-behaved quantity — but mean confidence is exactly the quantity that is a linear function of accuracy plus a bias term, and therefore the one least likely to add anything.
    - **Link to source:** [Kelemen, Frost & Weaver 2000, evidence against a general metacognitive ability](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03211579) · [Mazancieux et al. 2020, "Is there a G factor for metacognition?"](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000746)

- **Subcategory 5.6: High ability and the Dunning–Kruger literature — most of the effect is statistical**
  - **Source:** Kruger & Dunning (1999); Krueger & Mueller (2002); Gignac & Zajenkowski (2020); Nuhfer, Cogan, Fleischer, Gaze & Wirth (2016); Snyder, Nietfeld & Linnenbrink-Garcia (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The originating claim is stated in the paper's own title: "Unskilled and unaware of it: How difficulties in recognizing one's own incompetence lead to inflated self-assessments." *(Kruger & Dunning, 1999, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 77(6):1121–1134, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1121.)* `T2` — *bibliographic record and title verified; the article text was not obtained.*
      - The first major statistical rebuttal is likewise stated in its title and subtitle: "Unskilled, unaware, or both? The better-than-average heuristic and statistical regression predict errors in estimates of own performance." *(Krueger & Mueller, 2002, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 82(2):180–188, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.82.2.180.)* `T2` — *bibliographic record and title verified; the article text was not obtained.*
      - **The methodological critique, with a replacement test and a null.** "The most common method used to test the Dunning-Kruger hypothesis involves plotting the self-assessed and objectively assessed means across four categories (quartiles) of objective ability. However, this method has been argued to be confounded by the better-than-average effect and regression toward the mean." Using the Glejser test of heteroscedasticity and quadratic regression on 929 community participants who completed a self-assessment of intelligence and Advanced Raven's Progressive Matrices, "we failed to identify statistically significant heteroscedasticity, contrary to the Dunning-Kruger hypothesis. Additionally, the association between objectively measured intelligence and self-assessed intelligence was found to be essentially entirely linear, again, contrary to the Dunning-Kruger hypothesis." *(Gignac & Zajenkowski, 2020, *Intelligence* 80:101449, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2020.101449.)* `T2`
      - The same paper's conclusion: "although the phenomenon described by the Dunning-Kruger hypothesis may be to some degree plausible for some skills, the magnitude of the effect may be much smaller than reported previously." *(Gignac & Zajenkowski, 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2020.101449.)* `T2`
      - **The artifact is graphical as well as statistical.** Random-number simulations applied alongside paired self-assessed and demonstrated competence measures from 1,154 participants showed that "graphical conventions common in the self-assessment literature introduce artifacts that invite misinterpretation," specifically naming "(y minus x) vs. (x) scatterplots; (y minus x) vs. (x) column graphs aggregated as quantiles; line charts that display data aggregated as quantiles; and some histograms." *(Nuhfer, Cogan, Fleischer, Gaze & Wirth, 2016, *Numeracy* 9(1), DOI 10.5038/1936-4660.9.1.4.)* `T2` — *interest note: the authors advocate their own self-assessment instrumentation elsewhere.*
      - **The one direct high-ability result located, and it is partly null.** Comparing high school gifted (n = 44) and typical (n = 23) students across four biology exams in one semester: "Gifted students outperformed typical students on both local and global postdictive measures of calibration. However, there were no statistically significant differences in global predictive judgments or calibration bias. Local, item-by-item calibration accuracy partially mediated the relation between giftedness and exam performance." *(Snyder, Nietfeld & Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2011, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 55(3):181–193, DOI 10.1177/0016986211412769.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The intuition that better students know better what they know has some support, but the specific empirical shape usually invoked to support it does not survive proper statistical treatment. The classic quartile plot is confounded by regression to the mean and the better-than-average effect, and when the hypothesis is restated as a testable claim about heteroscedasticity or curvature in the ability–self-assessment relationship, a well-powered study of 929 people found neither: the relationship was essentially linear and homoscedastic. The graphical conventions that make the effect look dramatic generate the same pattern from random numbers. What survives is smaller and more specific: among high-school students identified as gifted, *retrospective, item-level* calibration was better than in typical peers, and it partially mediated the achievement difference — but *prospective* global judgments and calibration bias showed no group difference at all in the same study. If there is a real high-ability advantage in monitoring, the evidence points to it being retrospective and item-local, not a general "gifted students know themselves better" trait. Sample sizes in the direct giftedness work are small (n = 23 in the comparison group), and no replication was located.

- **Subcategory 5.7: Children — when confidence judgments become usable, and what still fails at school age**
  - **Source:** Lyons & Ghetti (2011); Destan, Hembacher, Ghetti & Roebers (2014); Vo, Li, Kornell, Pouget & Cantlon (2014); Schneider (2008); Baer, Malik & Odic (2021); Ohtani & Hisasaka (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Discrimination is present before school age.** Among 74 three-, four- and five-year-olds completing perceptual and lexical identification tasks with certain/uncertain reports, "even 3-year-olds provided confidence judgments that discriminated accurate from inaccurate responses, but this discrimination increased with age." The paper also found that "3-year-olds primarily rely on response latency to assess certainty, whereas older preschoolers do not." *(Lyons & Ghetti, 2011, *Child Development* 82(6):1778–1787, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8624.2011.01649.x.)* `T2`
      - **Monitoring saturates early; acting on it does not.** Among 101 children aged 5, 6 and 7, "all three age groups gave significantly higher CJs for correct answers compared with incorrect answers, with no age-related differences in the magnitude of this difference, suggesting robust metacognitive monitoring skills in children as young as 5 years." By contrast, "age-related improvements were found in children's use of CJs to guide their selection of answers," and the study-time link with judgments of learning appeared in 6- and 7-year-olds but not 5-year-olds. *(Destan, Hembacher, Ghetti & Roebers, 2014, *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* 126:213–228, DOI 10.1016/j.jecp.2014.04.001.)* `T2`
      - **Children's metacognition is domain-specific, and only the matched domain predicts achievement.** Using a nonverbal wagering task, "children as young as 5 years made metacognitive 'bets' on their numerical discriminations"; but "contrary to previous reports from adults, our results showed that children's metacognition is domain specific: Their metacognition in the numerical domain was unrelated to their metacognition in another domain (emotion discrimination). Moreover, children's metacognitive ability in only the numerical domain predicted their school-based mathematics knowledge." *(Vo, Li, Kornell, Pouget & Cantlon, 2014, *Psychological Science* 25(9):1712–1721, DOI 10.1177/0956797614538458.)* `T2`
      - **Developmental trends favour knowledge and control over monitoring.** "Whereas the findings for declarative metacognitive knowledge show steady improvement through childhood and adolescence, mainly due to increases in knowledge about strategies, the results are not similarly clear-cut for procedural metacognition. Age trends observed for this component of metacognition are significant for self-control activities but not pronounced for monitoring abilities." *(Schneider, 2008, *Mind, Brain, and Education* 2(3):114–121, DOI 10.1111/j.1751-228x.2008.00041.x.)* `T2`
      - **A large, careful null in the target age band.** Across four studies with 374 children aged 4–7 completing a confidence-reasoning task and a selective social-learning task designed to remove language and response-bias confounds, "in three of the four studies, we did not find that individual differences on the two tasks correlated, nor that decision accuracy explained performance." *(Baer, Malik & Odic, 2021, *Metacognition and Learning* 16(2):485–516, DOI 10.1007/s11409-021-09263-x.)* `T2`
      - The main meta-analysis in this space reports a developmental moderator with a caveat: a footnote states that "the child category also significantly differed from the secondary school category (p < .05)," while a second footnote reports that in a model additionally entering interview and accuracy measurement categories, "the measurement of metacognition was still significant, but both performance domain and overall developmental stage were non-significant." *(Ohtani & Hisasaka, 2018, *Metacognition and Learning* 13(2):179–212, DOI 10.1007/s11409-018-9183-8, footnotes 7 and 8 read from the article page.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Cross-task inconsistency of monitoring accuracy is documented in elementary-age children as well as in adults (see 5.5). Feasibility is not the obstacle. Three-year-olds already give confidence reports that discriminate their correct from their incorrect answers, five-year-olds do it robustly and can be measured nonverbally through wagering, and by five to seven the monitoring signal is present with no further age gain in its magnitude. What develops after that is the *control* side — using the judgment to allocate study time or to withhold a doubtful answer — not the monitoring itself. So a school-age screener can collect the signal. The problems are elsewhere and they are the same three problems as in adults, only sharper. First, children's monitoring accuracy is task-specific: measure it on one task and it does not tell you about another, and the only case where it predicted school achievement was when the metacognitive task and the achievement domain matched. Second, the cross-task correlations that would justify treating it as an ability are absent in the target age band, in a study of 374 children explicitly designed to remove the usual confounds. Third, the developmental review's own summary is that age trends in *monitoring* specifically are "not pronounced" — which means a monitoring score is not a maturational index either. The practical reading: with children you can measure calibration on the exact task you care about and get something; you cannot generalise it.
    - **Link to source:** [Lyons & Ghetti 2011 on uncertainty monitoring in preschoolers](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2011.01649.x) · [Vo et al. 2014, "Young children bet on their numerical skills"](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614538458) · [Baer, Malik & Odic 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-021-09263-x)

- **Subcategory 5.8: Practical cost and reactivity — asking the question changes the answer**
  - **Source:** Double, Birney & Walker (2018); Mitchum, Kelley & Fox (2016); Double, Goldwater & Birney (2025); Smrkolj et al. (2022); Echternacht (1972)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The meta-analytic headline is a null.** Across the judgment-of-learning reactivity literature, "the results indicate that overall providing JoL does not have a significant effect on memory performance (g = 0.054, 95% CI −0.027 to 0.135)." *(Double, Birney & Walker, 2018, *Memory* 26(6):741–750, DOI 10.1080/09658211.2017.1404111; Crossref issued date 2017, online-first.)* `T2`
      - **But the null hides real, material-dependent effects.** "Sub-groups analysis showed that this effect depends on the nature of the stimuli to be recalled, with moderate positive reactivity observed for related word pairs (g = 0.323, 95% CI 0.083 to 0.563) and word lists (g = 0.384, 95% CI 0.146 to 0.622) but no reactivity when pairs were unrelated or a mixture of related and unrelated pairs." *(Double, Birney & Walker, 2018, DOI 10.1080/09658211.2017.1404111.)* `T2`
      - Across five experiments, eliciting judgments of learning changed "how people allocate their study time to difficult versus easy items, and on what they will recall," leading the authors to conclude that "judgments of learning are partially constructed in response to the measurement question" and to counsel "incorporating control groups, creating alternative scales, and exploring other verbal reporting methods." *(Mitchum, Kelley & Fox, 2016, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 145(2):200–219, DOI 10.1037/a0039923.)* `T2`
      - **The most consequential reactivity finding for a learning-oriented instrument, and it is negative.** In a pre-registered study with N = 710 using a category-learning paradigm that separates memorisation from rule learning, "we show that eliciting confidence ratings caused a reduction in rule-based learning, even when compared to participants who made judgments of learning that asked them to reflect on their learning during the task as a whole." The authors attribute this to "a more conservative approach that prioritizes performance over mastery and ultimately hinders rule-based learning and knowledge transfer." *(Double, Goldwater & Birney, 2025, *Metacognition and Learning* 20(1), DOI 10.1007/s11409-025-09413-5.)* `T2`
      - The response-cost argument against confidence formats was stated in the founding review and has not been superseded: plain multiple-choice scoring means "more items can be administered in a given period of time using this method than by any other method requiring a more complicated response." *(Echternacht, 1972, *Review of Educational Research* 42(2):217–236, DOI 10.3102/00346543042002217.)* `T2` `[COI: as in 5.3]`
      - Practitioner-reported readiness cost: in the CBM course study, students wanted the format for revision but reported "more practice would be needed for CBM to be used in graded exams." *(Smrkolj, Bančov & Smrkolj, 2022, *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* 19(3):1706, DOI 10.3390/ijerph19031706.)* `T2` `[Interest note: as in 5.3]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The cost of a confidence prompt is not just the seconds it adds. Averaged over everything, asking for a judgment does not change memory performance — but that average is a mixture of a moderate positive effect on some material types and nothing on others, so "no reactivity" cannot be assumed for any specific task. More seriously for anything that claims to be about learning rather than recall, the one large pre-registered study of confidence-rating reactivity found the prompt *suppressed rule-based learning* relative to both no-judgment and judgment-of-learning controls, with a proposed mechanism — students shift toward playing it safe rather than toward mastery — that would apply to exactly the kind of exploratory, rule-inducing task a reasoning assessment is built from. Note the direction: this is not measurement noise, it is the instrument degrading the construct it wants to observe. On the plain time cost, no study located actually measured seconds-per-item added by a confidence prompt; the only sourced statement is the 1972 review's observation that simpler responses let you administer more items per unit time, which is a design argument rather than a measurement.

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Direct contradiction on whether metacognition is domain-general.** A 2018 meta-analysis reports perceptual metacognitive ability correlating across sensory modalities but *no* correlation between metacognition of perception and metacognition of memory. A 2020 study by an overlapping author group reports significant cross-task correlations in retrospective metacognitive sensitivity "even for pairs of tasks in which first-order performance was not correlated." Both cannot be straightforwardly right. The 2018 review pre-emptively supplies the reconciliation — behavioural studies may be underpowered to detect correlations across efficiency parameters — but that reconciliation also implies the earlier null literature is uninformative, which no one in this reading states outright.

2. **Domain-generality in adults versus domain-specificity in children.** Whatever the adult answer, two independent child studies point the other way: a nonverbal wagering study found numerical and emotional metacognition unrelated in five-year-olds, and a 2025 study of 53 elementary children found no association between monitoring accuracies across two tasks. If metacognition becomes more general with age, then the age band a school screener targets is precisely the band where a general metacognitive score is least defensible. No located source tests that developmental-convergence hypothesis directly.

3. **The reliability paradox.** Confidence *level* is stable within person across sessions and tasks; confidence *accuracy* is not. This is reported in 2000 for adults and echoed in 2025 for children. But the stable quantity is the one that is arithmetically tied to accuracy, and the unstable quantity is the one that would carry new information. Every proposal to use calibration as a person-level trait has to pick one horn of this.

4. **"Small incremental validity" and "no incremental validity" from the same dataset.** In the one clean test located, confidence added a small increment over accuracy for the test's own total score, two of its sections, and a numeracy measure — and added exactly nothing for an admissions test or high-school GPA. Papers citing this work in support of confidence measurement generally cite the first half. The pattern is more consistent with confidence adding method-shared variance to same-instrument criteria than with confidence carrying general predictive signal.

5. **Certainty-based marking is simultaneously "fair" and "risk-loaded."** One study reports 78% of students perceiving CBM as fair and finds no significant difference between certainty-based and accuracy scores; a psychometric analysis of six real CBM exams finds certainty reports systematically distorted by risk and loss aversion in opposite directions at high and low success probability. Both can be true — a risk-distorted score can still be distributionally similar in aggregate and still feel fair — but the second result means the certainty channel is not measuring only knowledge, and the first means it may not be measuring much of anything extra.

6. **Overconfidence is a red flag and a benign trait, depending on when you measure it.** The best-controlled learning-gain study located found that pre-instruction overconfidence "may actually be beneficial to learning" and did not hamper gain, while overconfidence that persisted or emerged after instruction was associated with markedly lower gain. The Dunning–Kruger framing treats overconfidence as a static deficit; this result treats it as informative only as a trajectory.

7. **A conflict-of-interest pattern worth naming.** Much of the confidence-scoring advocacy literature is written by the developers of the scoring scheme being advocated, or by faculty evaluating the format in their own courses; and the founding review of confidence testing was written from inside a large commercial testing organisation. In this case the incentives do not obviously run one way — the institutional self-evaluations produced the most prominent *null* — but the concentration of the evidence base in self-interested hands is real and is flagged inline throughout.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own — a connection between sources that looks load-bearing but has not been established by any single study cited here.*

- **Seed A — "Most reported calibration 'signal' is accuracy wearing a hat."** Signed bias is mean confidence minus proportion correct, a linear function of the score. Gamma systematically deviates from its true value. Type-II d′ and type-II ROC confound metacognition with first-order sensitivity, which is the stated reason meta-d′ was invented. The hard–easy effect, the field's most-cited bias phenomenon, largely vanishes under control for scale-end effects and linear dependency. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* an incremental-validity claim for calibration is only meaningful if the index has been de-confounded from accuracy — meta-d′/d′ or an equivalent — and essentially none of the educational calibration literature uses such an index. If that is right, the applied literature and the methodological literature are measuring different things, and the applied effect sizes cannot be taken at face value. Testing this requires checking which index each reported effect size was computed on, which the summaries above deliberately do not assume.

- **Seed B — "The stable part is useless and the useful part is unstable."** Raw confidence level replicates within person across sessions and across tasks; monitoring accuracy does not, in adults in 2000 and in children in 2025. But confidence level is the quantity mechanically tied to accuracy, and monitoring accuracy is the quantity that would add something. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* this is a formal trade-off, not an incidental measurement problem — the reliability of a derived monitoring index is bounded by the reliability of the difference between two noisy quantities, so the more you succeed in removing accuracy from a calibration index, the less reliable what remains must become. Whether that bound has been derived anywhere, or whether high-reliability de-confounded indices exist, is the thing to check before assuming the trade-off is inescapable.

- **Seed C — "Calibration predicts learning as a trajectory, not as a level."** In the best-controlled pre/post study located, subgroups with statistically indistinguishable prior knowledge but different pre-instruction calibration achieved similar learning gains; what separated the top group (53% normalised gain) from the bottom (15%) was whether calibration *improved* across instruction. Pre-instruction overconfidence was explicitly described as not debilitating. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* if the predictive signal is in the change in calibration under teaching, then a single-administration screener is structurally incapable of capturing it, and any instrument that wants this signal must be a two-point or repeated design. Note that the counter-reading is also available and untested here — posttest calibration is measured after learning has happened, so it may be an *outcome* of learning rather than a predictor of it, in which case the study shows nothing predictive at all. Both readings need to be tested against the paper's timing.

- **Seed D — "The confidence prompt is not a passive probe."** Judgments of learning change study-time allocation and what gets recalled, and are "partially constructed in response to the measurement question." A pre-registered study of 710 people found confidence ratings specifically reduced rule-based learning relative to both no-judgment and judgment-of-learning controls. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* for any instrument whose target construct is reasoning or rule induction rather than recall, adding a confidence prompt may trade a small measurement gain for a real degradation of the behaviour being measured — and the degradation falls on the higher-order component (transfer, rule use) rather than uniformly. If so, the reactivity literature is not a methodological footnote but a first-order design constraint, and the correct comparison is not "accuracy versus accuracy-plus-confidence" but "accuracy-without-prompt versus accuracy-with-prompt-plus-confidence," where the first term differs between conditions. No located study runs that comparison on a reasoning test.

- **Seed E — "The high-ability advantage, if it exists, is retrospective and local."** Gifted high-school students outperformed peers on local and global *postdictive* calibration and on nothing else — no difference in global predictive judgments, no difference in calibration bias — and only item-level calibration mediated the achievement relationship. Independently, delayed judgments beat immediate ones by g = 0.93, and item-level judgments outperform global ones in the wider literature. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* the two findings may be the same finding — both say that judgments made with more evidence in hand (after the answer, after a delay, per item) carry information that judgments made prospectively and globally do not. If that holds, then the only confidence measurement worth collecting is item-level and retrospective, and the common design of asking for an up-front global self-estimate is measuring something close to self-concept instead. Whether the giftedness result replicates at all is prior to this: n = 23 in the comparison group and no replication was located.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **Gap, and it is the headline one:** only a single study was located that measured confidence on the same items as accuracy and reported a proper incremental-validity test against external criteria. Its increment was described by its own authors as "small," and it was zero for two of the criteria tested. Everything else in the incremental-validity subcategory is either a raw correlation, a variance-share decomposition, or a partialled estimate whose coefficient was not obtainable. No meta-analysis of the *incremental* validity of confidence or calibration over accuracy was located.
- **Gap:** the Ohtani & Hisasaka (2018) statement that metacognition predicted academic performance "when controlling for intelligence" is verified from the article's abstract, but the size of that controlled effect was not obtained. The raw correlations quoted (r = .28 and r = .33) come from a quoting peer-reviewed source, not from the article's own tables.
- **Gap:** the widely repeated figures that metacognitive skillfulness uniquely accounts for 17% and intellectual ability 10% of variance in learning performance could not be verified. They were located here only in an AI-generated summary panel on a paper-hosting site, not in the article, and that same panel reported a sample size (N = 32) inconsistent with the study description. Deliberately excluded from the fact set.
- **Gap:** no source was located that measures the *added testing time or response burden* of a per-item confidence prompt in seconds or in items forgone. The only sourced statement is the 1972 review's general observation that simpler responses permit more items per unit time. Any claim about the time cost of confidence collection is currently unsupported.
- **Gap:** the algebraic form of the Brier score partition was not verified. The originating 1973 paper's bibliographic record resolves and its title matches, but its text was not obtained, so the decomposition's terms are not stated above.
- **Gap:** the 1984 *Psychological Bulletin* comparison of feeling-of-knowing accuracy measures — the source usually credited with establishing gamma as the field standard — is cited here on bibliographic record only. Its actual recommendation was not read.
- **Gap:** Kruger & Dunning (1999) and Krueger & Mueller (2002) are cited on record and title only. The critique in Gignac & Zajenkowski (2020) is verified from its own abstract; the original studies' numbers are not reproduced above and should not be sourced to this document.
- **Gap:** a study titled "Enhanced monitoring accuracy and test performance: Incremental effects of judgment training over and above repeated testing" exists and its record resolves (Händel, Harder & Dresel, 2020, *Learning and Instruction* 65:101245, DOI 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2019.101245), but no abstract or text was obtained, so it is excluded from the fact set. Note in any case that this is an intervention result — training calibration — and not evidence that a *measured* calibration score predicts anything.
- **Gap:** no evidence was located on the **test–retest reliability of a calibration index computed from a single short cognitive test in children** — the exact quantity a screener would produce. The available reliability evidence is from adults on multi-task memory batteries (unstable) and from cross-task correlations in children (near zero), both of which are informative but neither of which is the target statistic.
- **Partial answer, not a gap, on learning rate.** Unlike adjacent categories, this literature is *not* silent on learning gain — but what it contains does not support a one-shot screener. One pre/post study with prior-knowledge-matched subgroups found large learning-gain differences by calibration trajectory (53% versus 15% normalised gain) while explicitly reporting that pre-instruction miscalibration did not hamper learning; one longitudinal study found calibration explaining R² = .09–.12 of achievement up to four years later but with no ability covariate in the model and a null for time-to-graduation. Both are undergraduate samples. No study was located that tests whether a calibration score measured once in school-age children predicts subsequent learning rate with prior achievement controlled. That specific question remains unanswered.
- **Assumption, unverified:** that reactivity findings from word-pair and category-learning paradigms transfer to reasoning or figural items. The reactivity meta-analysis found effects that depended entirely on stimulus type, and the rule-learning suppression result used a categorisation paradigm. Neither used a reasoning test.
- **Assumption, unverified:** that findings from certainty-based marking in university medical and physiotherapy examinations transfer to children. Every confidence-scoring study located used undergraduates or older; no CBM or confidence-weighted scoring study in school-age children was found.
- **Conflict-of-interest caveat carried throughout:** the confidence-scoring literature is unusually concentrated among developers evaluating their own scheme and faculty evaluating their own courses, and the founding review came from a commercial testing organisation. Flagged inline where applicable.
