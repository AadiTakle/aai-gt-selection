### Category 2: Disengagement and effort as a measured signal

*General idea: Effort signals are unusual among behavioural measures because their value comes from subtraction, not addition — they identify item responses that carry no information about ability so those responses can be excluded, rather than adding a new source of variance to the estimate. The evidence base is unusually strong on detection and unusually weak on the part that matters: response-time methods reliably flag fast disengagement, but the best available external validation suggests they capture only a fraction of the total score distortion caused by disengagement, and the act of filtering can itself introduce bias when disengagement is related to ability or group membership.*

- **Subcategory 2.1: Rapid guessing and Response Time Effort — the construct and the threshold problem**
  - **Source:** Wise & Kong (2005); Kong, Wise & Bhola (2007); Wise (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Response Time Effort (RTE) is defined as the proportion of items on which an examinee exhibited "solution behavior" rather than rapid guessing, computed by comparing each item response time to an item-specific threshold; scores range from 0 to 1. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - In the originating study, item thresholds were set by visual inspection of item response-time distributions, which were described as similar in shape across items and bimodal, with a local maximum somewhere below 10 seconds. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - In that sample, RTE scores were highly negatively skewed; 299 examinees had RTE exactly 1.0 and the lowest observed RTE was .20. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - RTE scores correlated significantly with self-reported effort and with a person-fit index, but the authors stated that the magnitude of those correlations suggested the three measures were not interchangeable. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - Correlations between RTE scores and SAT subscale scores were reported as near zero, which the authors treated as evidence that RTE was not simply a proxy for academic ability. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - Filtering examinees on RTE produced greater increases in both mean test performance and the convergent-validity correlation with an external criterion than filtering on self-reported effort, even though fewer examinees were deleted. Specific magnitudes were reported in a table that could not be reliably extracted from the accessible copy. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2` — magnitude unverified
      - Four distinct procedures for setting the response-time threshold that separates solution behavior from rapid-guessing behavior were compared in a dedicated methodological study. *(Kong, Wise & Bhola, 2007, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 67(4):606–619, DOI 10.1177/0013164406294779.)* `T2`
      - A widely cited conceptual treatment argues that because a rapid guess does not reflect what a test taker knows and can do, it represents a choice to momentarily opt out of being measured, and therefore that including rapid guesses in scoring "makes little sense." *(Wise, 2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 36(4):52–61, DOI 10.1111/emip.12165.)* `T2` `[COI: author affiliated with a commercial assessment provider, NWEA]`
      - The same treatment identifies two directions of misclassification: fast but genuinely effortful responses may be wrongly flagged as rapid guesses, and some slower non-effortful responses go undetected. *(Wise, 2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 36(4):52–61, DOI 10.1111/emip.12165.)* `T2` `[COI: NWEA]`
      - A convention classifying an examinee as unmotivated when rapid guessing occurs on 10% or more of administered items (RTE below 0.90) originated in the 2005 RTE paper and has since been used extensively in applied research. *(Rios & Deng, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9(1), article 18, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00110-8.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The core signal is simple and behaviourally grounded: a response too fast to have permitted reading and consideration of the item is treated as non-informative, and the per-person index is just the proportion of items answered with genuine solution behavior. The founding study established that this index is roughly uncorrelated with ability while correlating with self-reported effort, which is what a nuisance-detector should look like, and that filtering on it moved both means and criterion correlations more than filtering on self-report did. Two features of that foundation deserve attention. First, the entire method rests on locating a threshold, and the original threshold was set by eye from distribution shapes. Second, the field's default cut-off for calling a person "unmotivated" is a round number chosen in 2005 that subsequent literature adopted by convention rather than by re-derivation.
    - **Link to source:** [Wise & Kong 2005](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2)

  - **Source:** Rios & Deng (2021) — meta-analysis of threshold procedures
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Threshold procedures were classified into three typologies: those using no empirical data (NED, e.g. an arbitrary common k-second limit or item surface features), those using response-time information only (RT, e.g. percentile and bimodal-distribution methods), and those combining response time with response accuracy (RTRA, e.g. setting the threshold where the correct-response rate begins to consistently exceed chance). *(Rios & Deng, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9(1), article 18, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00110-8.)* `T1`
      - Of 958 studies screened, 19 met eligibility criteria — a 2% hit rate — and 47% of the included studies were grey literature (dissertations, research reports, conference papers, and works in progress). *(Rios & Deng, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9(1), article 18, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00110-8.)* `T1`
      - Across 54 effect sizes, the average difference between threshold procedures in the proportion of rapid-guessing responses identified was Cohen's h = 0.26 (SD = 0.24), described by the authors as a small-to-moderate effect of threshold type. *(Rios & Deng, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9(1), article 18, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00110-8.)* `T1`
      - The authors concluded that for inferences about aggregate group performance the choice of threshold procedure mattered little, but that for individual-level score inferences the no-empirical-data typologies should be avoided because their conservatism produces false-negative classifications. *(Rios & Deng, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9(1), article 18, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00110-8.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The threshold problem is real but its consequences are use-dependent. Different threshold families disagree meaningfully about how many responses count as rapid guesses — a small-to-moderate standardised difference — yet that disagreement washes out when the target is a group mean. It does not wash out when the target is an individual's score or classification, where conservative arbitrary thresholds systematically miss disengaged responses. Two cautions about the evidence itself: the eligible literature was tiny relative to what was screened, and nearly half of it was unrefereed.
    - **Link to source:** [Rios & Deng 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-021-00110-8)

- **Subcategory 2.2: The measured payoff — prevalence of rapid guessing and the magnitude of score distortion**
  - **Source:** Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt (2022) — meta-analysis of score distortion
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The meta-analysis pooled 25 studies and 39 independent samples comprising 443,264 unique examinees who took group-administered low-stakes cognitive assessments with response-time-based identification of rapid guessing. *(Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022, *Educational Assessment* 27(4):356–373, DOI 10.1080/10627197.2022.2110465.)* `T1`
      - An average of 28.3% of examinees engaged in rapid guessing at all, 21% were judged to do so on a non-negligible number of items, and 6.89% of all item responses were classified as rapid guesses. *(Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022, *Educational Assessment* 27(4):356–373, DOI 10.1080/10627197.2022.2110465.)* `T1`
      - Across 100 effect sizes, rapid guessing negatively distorted aggregated test scores by an average of 0.13 standard deviations (95% CI 0.08 to 0.18), with the relationship moderated by test content area and by filtering procedure. *(Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022, *Educational Assessment* 27(4):356–373, DOI 10.1080/10627197.2022.2110465.)* `T1`
      - Heterogeneity was large, with I² = 92.25%; 95% of effect sizes showed negative distortion while 5% showed cases in which rapid guessing inflated observed performance. *(Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022, preprint of record, DOI 10.31234/osf.io/8jnsu; published as *Educational Assessment* 27(4):356–373, DOI 10.1080/10627197.2022.2110465.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the single most useful number in the literature for judging whether the signal earns its place. Disengagement is common — well over a quarter of examinees in low-stakes settings do it at least once — but its average effect on an aggregated score is about a tenth of a standard deviation, and the confidence interval excludes anything large. The heterogeneity statistic says the average is close to meaningless as a prediction for any particular assessment: context dominates. A small but real minority of estimates run the other way, meaning rapid guessing sometimes inflates scores rather than depressing them, which undercuts any assumption that filtering always corrects downward bias.
    - **Link to source:** [Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10627197.2022.2110465)

  - **Source:** Setzer, Wise, van den Heuvel & Ling (2013); Svetina Valdivia, Rutkowski, Rutkowski, Canbolat & Underhill (2023); Wise, Pastor & Kong (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - On a commercially available low-stakes instrument, rapid-guessing behavior was observed on an average of only 1% of item responses, and the amount was consistently very low across demographic subpopulations — substantially less than published results from locally developed instruments. *(Setzer, Wise, van den Heuvel & Ling, 2013, *Applied Measurement in Education* 26(1):34–49, DOI 10.1080/08957347.2013.739453.)* `T2` `[COI: authors affiliated with commercial testing organisations, ETS and NWEA]`
      - Even at that 1% level, the authors reported that a small amount of rapid-guessing behavior could affect institutional rankings. *(Setzer, Wise, van den Heuvel & Ling, 2013, *Applied Measurement in Education* 26(1):34–49, DOI 10.1080/08957347.2013.739453.)* `T2` `[COI: ETS, NWEA]`
      - In a state assessment of over 80,000 students, mean RTE under a normative 10%-of-mean threshold was 0.991 in grade 3 and 0.980 in grade 8; 86.28% of grade-3 and 76.85% of grade-8 students had RTE of exactly 1. *(Svetina Valdivia, Rutkowski, Rutkowski, Canbolat & Underhill, 2023, *Frontiers in Education* 8, article 1127644, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2023.1127644.)* `T2`
      - In the same assessment, 2.14% of grade-3 students but 5.74% of grade-8 students fell below the RTE 0.90 threshold for meaningful disengagement. *(Svetina Valdivia et al., 2023, *Frontiers in Education* 8, article 1127644, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2023.1127644.)* `T2`
      - Using hierarchical generalized linear modelling, items with more text and items occurring later in the test were associated with increased rapid guessing, while the inclusion of a graphic was associated with decreased rapid guessing; the only significant examinee-level predictor was SAT total score. *(Wise, Pastor & Kong, 2009, *Applied Measurement in Education* 22(2):185–205, DOI 10.1080/08957340902754650.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Prevalence is not a property of the signal, it is a property of the testing situation. Reported rates span from around 1% of responses on one commercial instrument to nearly 7% in the meta-analytic average, and the proportion of students showing meaningful disengagement on one large state assessment was under 6% even in the higher grade. This variability means a prevalence figure imported from another context predicts almost nothing, and a detector calibrated for a high-disengagement population may find nearly nothing to remove in a low-disengagement one. What does replicate is that disengagement is item-driven as much as person-driven: reading load and position in the sequence predict it, which makes it partly a design artefact rather than purely an attribute of the test taker.
    - **Link to source:** [Setzer et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957347.2013.739453)

- **Subcategory 2.3: Effort-moderated scoring and model-based treatment of non-effortful responding**
  - **Source:** Wise & DeMars (2006); Schnipke & Scrams (1997); Yamamoto (1995)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The effort-moderated IRT model incorporates item response time into both proficiency estimation and item parameter estimation by treating responses classified as rapid guesses as non-informative. *(Wise & DeMars, 2006, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 43(1):19–38, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x.)* `T2`
      - In two studies, one on real data and one simulated, the effort-moderated model outperformed the standard three-parameter logistic model on four criteria: model fit, accuracy of item parameter estimates, accuracy of estimated test information, and convergent validity of proficiency estimates. *(Wise & DeMars, 2006, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 43(1):19–38, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x.)* `T2`
      - The authors concluded that the effort-moderated model would be more appropriate than standard IRT models when even a modest amount of rapid-guessing behavior is present, giving 2% or more as an example. *(Wise & DeMars, 2006, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 43(1):19–38, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x.)* `T2`
      - A two-state mixture model of item response times was proposed earlier to measure speededness, on the grounds that when tests are number-right scored examinees rapidly guess rather than leave items blank, so the traditional not-reached measure underestimates speededness. *(Schnipke & Scrams, 1997, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 34(3):213–232, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1997.tb00516.x.)* `T2`
      - A HYBRID model characterising when each examinee switches from an ability-based response strategy to random responding produced more accurate IRT parameter estimates than IRT-only analysis; applied to an experimental form of a large language test, nearly 20% of examinees were affected by speededness after completing 80% of the test. *(Yamamoto, 1995, *ETS Research Report Series* 1995(1), DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.1995.tb01637.x.)* `T3` `[COI: test publisher research report, ETS]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There are two distinct model families. One classifies responses first using a time threshold and then estimates ability while treating flagged responses as missing; the other jointly models a mixture of engaged and guessing states so that the switch point is estimated rather than imposed. The threshold-and-exclude approach has the stronger applied evidence base and shows improvement on four separate criteria simultaneously, which is more convincing than an improvement on any one. The mixture and HYBRID traditions matter because they show that strategy switching mid-test is estimable and common enough to bias calibration, and because they locate the phenomenon in a time-pressure account rather than a motivation account.
    - **Link to source:** [Wise & DeMars 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x)

  - **Source:** Rios & Soland (2021a, 2021b); Rios & Deng (2025); Wise & Kingsbury (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The effort-moderated model rests on two assumptions: that non-effortful responding occurs randomly, and that it is unrelated to the underlying ability of examinees. *(Rios & Soland, 2021, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 81(3):569–594, DOI 10.1177/0013164420949896.)* `T2`
      - In simulation, item parameter estimates were robust to violation of the randomness assumption, but bias exceeded 0.20 SD when the ability-independence assumption was violated — still lower than the two-parameter logistic model's bias. *(Rios & Soland, 2021, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 81(3):569–594, DOI 10.1177/0013164420949896.)* `T2`
      - For mean ability estimates, the effort-moderated and two-parameter logistic models performed equally across conditions, and both were biased by more than 0.25 SD when the ability-independence assumption was violated; an accompanying empirical study suggested this occurred under extreme conditions that may not be present in some operational settings. *(Rios & Soland, 2021, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 81(3):569–594, DOI 10.1177/0013164420949896.)* `T2`
      - At the individual level, including 10% non-effortful responses in three-parameter logistic scoring produced average bias in ability estimates of up to 0.15 SD and misclassification rates of up to 7%; these were mitigated by the effort-moderated model, particularly when its assumptions were met, and its advantage deteriorated but did not vanish when assumptions were violated. *(Rios & Soland, 2021, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 45(6):391–406, DOI 10.1177/01466216211013896.)* `T2`
      - Recoding rapid-guessing item scores as missing is described as the most researched scoring approach and the only known method employed in operational settings, but it is susceptible to non-convergence and to increased standard errors in ability estimation as rapid-guessing rates rise. *(Rios & Deng, 2025, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 85(1):134–155, DOI 10.1177/00131644241246749.)* `T2`
      - Prior research has demonstrated a negative relationship between rapid-guessing propensity and ability in certain testing contexts, meaning lower-ability examinees are more likely to engage in rapid guessing — which is precisely the condition under which the model's ability-independence assumption fails. *(Rios & Deng, 2025, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 85(1):134–155, DOI 10.1177/00131644241246749.)* `T2`
      - Excluding rapid guesses in an adaptive achievement test yielded estimates that were less distorted by disengagement but carried higher standard errors. *(Wise & Kingsbury, 2016, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 53(1):86–105, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12102.)* `T2` `[COI: NWEA]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the most important body of evidence for judging incremental value honestly, and it says something uncomfortable. The model that dominates operational practice buys its improvement by deleting data, which means it trades bias for variance: cleaner point estimates, wider uncertainty, and a risk of non-convergence when there is a lot to delete. Its central assumption — that disengagement is unrelated to ability — is contradicted by findings that lower-ability test takers disengage more, and when that assumption fails the advantage over doing nothing collapses for group means specifically, while surviving for item parameters and individual classifications. The magnitudes are worth holding onto: roughly 0.15 SD of individual-level bias and seven percentage points of misclassification from a 10% contamination rate is a real problem worth correcting, but it is not a large one.
    - **Link to source:** [Rios & Soland 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164420949896)

- **Subcategory 2.4: The detection ceiling — slow and passive disengagement that response times cannot see**
  - **Source:** Wise & Kuhfeld (2021) — retest-based external validation
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using achievement test data from test takers who were quickly retested and showed differing degrees of disengagement between the two sittings, standard effort-moderated scoring accounted for roughly one-third of the score distortion attributable to differential disengagement. *(Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 58(1):130–149, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12275.)* `T2` `[COI: NWEA]`
      - A modified effort-moderated method using more liberal time thresholds performed better, accounting for two-thirds or more of the distortion. *(Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 58(1):130–149, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12275.)* `T2` `[COI: NWEA]`
      - The authors interpreted the residual unexplained distortion as evidence for the additional presence of non-rapid item responses that reflect less-than-full engagement by some test takers. *(Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 58(1):130–149, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12275.)* `T2` `[COI: NWEA]`
      - The design identified over 5,000 cases in which students were retested within one day, showing 10% or more rapid guesses on the first testing and zero rapid guesses on the retest. *(Wise & Kuhfeld, 2020, conference presentation abstract for the same study, National Council on Measurement in Education virtual conference; published as *Journal of Educational Measurement* 58(1):130–149, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12275.)* `T3` `[COI: NWEA]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the closest thing the literature has to a criterion test of effort filtering, because the same students provide an engaged and a disengaged measurement of themselves within a day. The result is the sharpest available answer to how much of the problem the standard method solves: about a third with conventional thresholds, and up to two-thirds when thresholds are loosened enough to catch borderline-fast responses. The remainder is the part the method structurally cannot reach — responses slow enough to look like solution behavior but not effortful enough to reflect ability. Notably, the authors of a widely used commercial implementation published this limitation themselves.
    - **Link to source:** [Wise & Kuhfeld 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12275)

  - **Source:** Nagy, Ulitzsch & Lindner (2023); Lehman, Sparks & Steinberg (2024); Şahin & Colvin (2020); Jerez, Mazzullo & Bulut (2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Combining IRT models for rapid guessing with models for test-taking persistence in an assessment where items were randomly distributed across positions for each student, response times did not fully explain disengagement: item responses reflected test-taking persistence even after rapid responses were accounted for. *(Nagy, Ulitzsch & Lindner, 2023, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 39(3):751–766, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12719.)* `T2`
      - Estimated test-taking persistence — the duration for which a person's initial level of performance is maintained — showed a strong correlation with decreases in self-reported test-taking effort, and persistence models could account for the effect of low effort even when response times were unavailable. *(Nagy, Ulitzsch & Lindner, 2023, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 39(3):751–766, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12719.)* `T2`
      - Six categories of threshold-setting method have been developed and evaluated in low-stakes contexts: common k-second threshold, surface features, visual inspection, mixture models, combined response time and accuracy, and normative threshold. *(Lehman, Sparks & Steinberg, 2024, *ETS Research Report Series* 2024(1):1–15, DOI 10.1002/ets2.12384.)* `T3` `[COI: test publisher research report, ETS]`
      - The majority of these methods attempt to locate the boundary between engaged and disengaged response-time distributions, and that boundary is defined by a particular type of disengaged response — a rapid guess with insufficient time for an engaged interaction; disengaged interactions of longer duration such as mind-wandering and off-task behavior are possible but have received less research attention on identification. *(Lehman, Sparks & Steinberg, 2024, *ETS Research Report Series* 2024(1):1–15, DOI 10.1002/ets2.12384.)* `T3` `[COI: ETS]`
      - Methods enhanced with logged response behaviors (in addition to response times) performed better than response-time-only methods for detecting rapid disengagement in some cases, but no clear pattern was observed as to when such improvement occurs. *(Şahin & Colvin, 2020, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 8(1), article 5, DOI 10.1186/s40536-020-00082-1.)* `T2`
      - Slow responding in international large-scale assessments has received far less attention than rapid guessing; sequence analysis of log data from one complex problem-solving item identified two distinct clusters among slow responders, one with long pauses interspersed with repeated inefficient attempts and one with shorter pauses coupled with inefficient exploratory actions. *(Jerez, Mazzullo & Bulut, 2026, *Computers* 15(1):64, DOI 10.3390/computers15010064.)* `T2`
      - In an adaptive test, the item-selection consequences of a burst of rapid guessing persisted long after the disengagement itself had ceased, because the depressed provisional ability estimate caused subsequent items to be mistargeted; treating rapid guesses as missing when computing provisional estimates was proposed as the remedy. *(Wise, 2023, *Journal of Computerized Adaptive Testing* 10(2), DOI 10.7333/2305-1002022.)* `T3` `[COI: NWEA]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The detection ceiling is structural, not a tuning problem. Response-time thresholds work by finding a gap between two distributions, and the only disengagement that lives in that gap is the fast kind. Everything else — mind-wandering, partial reasoning abandoned midway, a gradual decline in effort across a session — produces latencies indistinguishable from genuine work. Two independent research programmes now show that the missed portion is measurable by other means: modelling declining solution probability across randomised item positions recovers a persistence dimension that response times do not explain and that tracks self-reported effort decline, and sequence analysis of interaction logs shows slow responders are not one group but several. A separate consequence is specific to adaptive delivery: a disengagement episode contaminates the item-selection trajectory for the remainder of the session, so the damage outlasts the behaviour and is not repaired by simply discarding the offending responses at scoring time.
    - **Link to source:** [Nagy, Ulitzsch & Lindner 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12719)

- **Subcategory 2.5: Motivation and stakes — why effort is a validity threat, and how large the effect actually is**
  - **Source:** Duckworth, Quinn, Lynam, Loeber & Stouthamer-Loeber (2011), retracted 2025
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A widely cited meta-analysis of random-assignment experiments reported that material incentives increased intelligence-test scores by an average of g = 0.64 (95% CI 0.39 to 0.89) across 46 independent samples totalling 2,008 participants, with larger effects for individuals with lower baseline scores. *(Duckworth, Quinn, Lynam, Loeber & Stouthamer-Loeber, 2011, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 108(19):7716–7720, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1018601108. **This article was retracted on 1 May 2025.**)* `T1` — retracted
      - The retraction notice states that a 1978 article contributing three large effect sizes and 24% of the meta-analytic sample size was itself retracted for research misconduct; removing it reduced the pooled estimate from g = 0.64 to g = 0.47, which remained statistically significant. *(Retraction notice, 2025, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 122(18):e2508040122, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2508040122.)* `T1`
      - The notice further states that whereas only one of four publication-bias tests had been positive in the original paper, after removal of the fraudulent study there was strong evidence of publication bias on all four tests; trim-and-fill adjustment reduced the estimate to g = 0.16 with a 95% CI of −0.02 to 0.34, which includes zero. *(Retraction notice, 2025, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 122(18):e2508040122, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2508040122.)* `T1`
      - The authors retracted the article at their own request despite the second, longitudinal study within it not requiring correction. *(Retraction notice, 2025, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 122(18):e2508040122, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2508040122.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The most frequently cited number for "how much motivation moves ability scores" no longer exists. What replaced it is an estimate roughly a quarter of the original size whose confidence interval crosses zero. This should change how the effort-as-validity-threat argument is made: it can no longer lean on a large incentive effect on measured ability from the experimental literature. It also functions as a live warning about the citation practices of this whole area — the original figure was propagated for over a decade and the correction is recent enough that most secondary sources still repeat the pre-retraction value.

    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **The average distortion is small, but the practice is treated as essential.** The meta-analytic average effect of rapid guessing on aggregated scores is 0.13 SD (Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022), and the conceptual literature nonetheless argues that including rapid guesses in scoring "makes little sense" as a matter of principle (Wise, 2017). Both can be true — a 0.13 SD average with I² above 92% means some contexts are badly affected and most are barely affected — but the strength of the normative claim is not proportional to the average magnitude of the problem.

2. **Filtering can overcorrect by more than the error it removes.** Careless responding depressed a group mean by about 0.20 SD, while listwise deletion of unmotivated examinees inflated it by up to 0.42 SD when ability and disengagement were related (Rios, Guo, Mao & Liu, 2017). Since a negative relationship between rapid-guessing propensity and ability is documented in real testing contexts (Rios & Deng, 2025), the harmful condition is not a corner case.

3. **The model's key assumption is contradicted by the model's own literature.** Effort-moderated scoring assumes non-effortful responding is unrelated to ability (Rios & Soland, 2021). Independent work states that lower-ability examinees are more likely to rapid-guess in certain testing contexts (Rios & Deng, 2025), and a separate modelling study found that the sole significant examinee-level predictor of rapid guessing was an ability test score — though the direction of that association was not confirmed in the material reviewed here (Wise, Pastor & Kong, 2009). Under assumption violation the model's advantage over doing nothing disappears for mean ability estimates specifically.

4. **The method's own developers report that it recovers only a minority of the distortion.** Standard effort-moderated scoring accounted for roughly one-third of disengagement-driven score distortion in a retest design (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021), yet the same threshold family is described elsewhere as validated and operationally sufficient. The gap is closed only by loosening thresholds — which increases false-positive removal of fast, genuinely effortful responders (Wise, 2017; Şahin & Colvin, 2020).

5. **Prevalence estimates differ by more than an order of magnitude.** Rapid guessing was found on about 1% of item responses on one commercial low-stakes instrument (Setzer et al., 2013) versus 6.89% in the meta-analytic average (Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022). The threshold procedure used partly determines this (Rios & Deng, 2021), so prevalence is not cleanly separable from measurement decisions.

6. **Detecting a behaviour and improving an estimate are demonstrably different achievements.** Help-seeking feedback durably changed the targeted behaviour and produced no learning gain (Aleven et al., 2016). Among four measured off-task behaviours, three had no relationship with learning once prior knowledge was controlled (Baker et al., 2004). The engagement-detection literature is not entitled to assume that a validated detector implies a better ability estimate.

7. **The headline motivation effect is retracted, but the validity-threat framing built on it persists.** The incentive effect on ability scores fell from g = 0.64 to a publication-bias-corrected g = 0.16 whose interval includes zero (retraction notice, 2025). Meanwhile individual experiments report effects several times larger (Liu, Rios & Borden, 2015). Whether motivation moves measured ability a lot or barely at all is currently unsettled at the level of pooled evidence.

8. **Fairness cuts both ways and the literature does not resolve which way dominates.** Differential engagement can manufacture spurious differential item functioning in up to 18% of items when true item parameters are identical (DeMars & Wise, 2010) and bias latent mean comparisons by 0.18 SD at a 7% differential rate (Rios, 2021) — arguments for adjustment. Yet substantial subgroup differences in rapid guessing mostly failed to change achievement-gap interpretations in a large empirical study (Soland, 2018) — an argument that adjustment changes little. Filtering is itself a group-differential deletion process.

9. **A large body of the strongest applied evidence is produced by parties with a commercial interest in it.** The retest validation, the operational implementation, the proctor-notification evaluation, the grade and time-of-day patterns, and the achievement-gap analysis are all authored by researchers affiliated with commercial assessment providers. The findings include self-critical results, which is reassuring, but independent replication of the retest-based recovery estimate does not appear to exist.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each item below is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. Each is stated so that it could be shown wrong.*

1. **Starter hypothesis — "Effort filtering is better understood as a data-quality guarantee than as an accuracy improvement."** The evidence for filtering is strong where the claim is "these particular responses carry no information" and weak where the claim is "the resulting estimate is closer to the truth." Supporting strands: excluding rapid guesses reduces distortion but raises standard errors (Wise & Kingsbury, 2016) and risks non-convergence at higher rates (Rios & Deng, 2025); it recovers about a third of the distortion (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021); the meta-analytic average distortion is 0.13 SD (Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022); deletion can overcorrect by 0.42 SD (Rios, Guo, Mao & Liu, 2017). *What would falsify it:* a study showing filtering improves criterion-related validity or classification accuracy by more than it inflates uncertainty, in a design where the criterion is measured independently of engagement. The author should check whether the individual-level result of 0.15 SD bias and seven points of misclassification (Rios & Soland, 2021, *Applied Psychological Measurement*) already constitutes such a demonstration.

2. **Starter hypothesis — "Because response-time detection is definitionally restricted to fast disengagement, the residual it leaves behind is not noise but a second, separately estimable dimension."** Three independent lines converge: retest analysis attributes unexplained distortion to non-rapid, less-than-fully-engaged responses (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021); persistence modelled from declining solution probability across randomised positions explains disengagement beyond response times and correlates with self-reported effort decline (Nagy, Ulitzsch & Lindner, 2023); slow responders cluster into distinct interaction-pattern subtypes rather than forming one group (Jerez, Mazzullo & Bulut, 2026). *What would falsify it:* evidence that the residual is unsystematic, or that persistence estimates are simply ability or speed under another name. The author should also confirm whether persistence has ever been shown to improve an ability estimate as opposed to explaining variance in the response process.

3. **Starter hypothesis — "Cleaning and fairness are in direct tension, and the literature has not chosen between them."** Differential engagement both distorts group comparisons and, when corrected by deletion, produces group-differential missingness. Supporting strands: subgroup differences in rapid-guessing rates by sex and race (Soland, 2018; Wise, Ma, Kingsbury & Hauser, 2010) and by administration mode (Kröhne, Deribo & Goldhammer, 2020); spurious differential item functioning from differential guessing alone (DeMars & Wise, 2010); overcorrection when disengagement tracks ability (Rios, Guo, Mao & Liu, 2017). *What would falsify it:* a filtering or model-based approach demonstrated to reduce spurious bias signals without introducing ability-correlated missingness. The author should treat the divergence between Soland's mostly-null empirical gap result and Rios's non-trivial simulated bias as the specific question to resolve, since these are different designs answering slightly different questions.

4. **Starter hypothesis — "Disengagement is substantially a design outcome, which makes 'filter it out' a second-best response to it."** Rapid guessing is predicted by item text volume, item position, and elapsed testing time (Wise, Pastor & Kong, 2009; Lindner, Lüdtke & Nagy, 2019), by time of day (Wise, Ma, Kingsbury & Hauser, 2010), and by whether administration is proctored (Kröhne, Deribo & Goldhammer, 2020). If the signal is largely produced by measurable features of the encounter, prevention is available where correction is only partial. *What would falsify it:* the meta-analytic finding that interventions modifying assessment design had negligible impact on effort and performance, while external incentives had the largest effect (Rios, 2021) — which points the other way. The author needs to reconcile "design predicts disengagement" with "design changes did not reduce it," and should check whether the design interventions in that meta-analysis actually manipulated the features shown to be predictive.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **The one-third recovery figure has a single source.** The retest-based estimate of how much distortion effort-moderated scoring recovers (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021) appears not to have been independently replicated, and it was produced by researchers affiliated with the provider whose operational method it evaluates. Everything downstream that treats response-time filtering as partial rather than sufficient rests on this one study.
- **No evidence located on whether filtering improves estimates of learning rate or growth.** All identified evidence concerns single-occasion ability or proficiency estimates, group means, item parameters, and classification accuracy. Whether removing disengaged responses improves the estimation of change across occasions — where a disengaged episode at one time point could distort a slope in either direction — was not found addressed in the sources reviewed.
- **Young children are largely absent.** The youngest group with published rapid-guessing evidence in these sources is grade 3, and the finding is that disengagement is *less* common there than in later grades (Svetina Valdivia et al., 2023; Wise, Ma, Kingsbury & Hauser, 2010). No evidence was located on rapid-guessing threshold validity below grade 3, on pre-readers, or on individually administered or short-session assessment of young children. Whether response-time thresholds derived from older test takers are even interpretable for young children is an open question, not a settled one.
- **The age gradient has no established mechanism.** The increase in rapid guessing across grade with a jump in early adolescence is documented, but the explanation offered in the source is explicitly a common belief about adolescent compliance rather than a tested account. Competing explanations — cumulative testing experience, changing perceptions of stakes, reading load of grade-appropriate items — were not distinguished in the sources reviewed.
- **Answer changes and navigation patterns are unsupported here.** Despite being commonly proposed as engagement indicators, no study was located in this search establishing that answer-change behaviour or within-test navigation adds incremental validity to an ability estimate. Item-level abandonment and minimal-action patterns are established as observable and as characteristic of disengagement (Şahin & Colvin, 2020) but not as validated ability-estimate improvements. Treat these as uninvestigated rather than as investigated and null.
- **Threshold conventions rest on convenience.** The RTE 0.90 cut-off for classifying a person as unmotivated originated in 2005 and propagated through applied research by adoption (Rios & Deng, 2021). No source located re-derives it against an external criterion.
- **Un-verified magnitudes, explicitly flagged.** Two numbers in this document could not be confirmed against the primary text: the specific mean-performance and criterion-correlation changes from motivation filtering in the founding RTE study (reported in a table that did not extract reliably), and the 61% / 0.34 SD / 0.38 SD proctor-notification magnitudes, which are taken from a later secondary account by one of the original authors rather than from the primary article. Both are marked at point of use.
- **Publication and grey-literature exposure.** Nearly half the studies in the threshold-comparison meta-analysis were unrefereed (Rios & Deng, 2021), and the collapse of the incentive meta-analysis under publication-bias correction (retraction notice, 2025) demonstrates that this literature's pooled estimates have proven fragile at least once.
- **Cross-cultural and language-minority evidence is thin in what was reviewed.** One study reports rapid guessing being consistently very low across demographic subpopulations (Setzer et al., 2013) and another reports rates differing across student groups (Kröhne, Deribo & Goldhammer, 2020), without a synthesis reconciling them for language-minority test takers specifically.
