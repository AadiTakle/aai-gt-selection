### Category 4: Evaluating a screening decision — classification accuracy, base rates, and utility

*General idea: A screener does not produce a correlation, it produces a decision, so the statistics that govern whether it is any good are decision statistics — sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, net benefit, and utility under a base rate and a selection ratio — not the validity coefficient. This category assembles the verified machinery for evaluating a single screening decision, for comparing a candidate instrument against an incumbent, and for defending the cut score where the decision is actually made, together with the documented criticisms of each method.*

- **Subcategory 4.1: The 2×2 confusion matrix and which operating characteristics are invariant to prevalence**
  - **Source:** Altman & Bland (1994a, 1994b)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sensitivity is defined as the proportion of people *with* the condition who are correctly identified by the test, and specificity as the proportion *without* the condition who are correctly excluded; both are conditioned on true status. *(Altman & Bland, 1994a, *BMJ* 308(6943):1552, DOI 10.1136/bmj.308.6943.1552.)* `T2`
      - Predictive values answer the reverse conditional question — the probability that a person actually has the condition given the test result — and the authors treat them in a separate note precisely because they are a different quantity from sensitivity and specificity. *(Altman & Bland, 1994b, *BMJ* 309(6947):102, DOI 10.1136/bmj.309.6947.102.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two of the four rates in the 2×2 table read *down the columns* (conditioning on true status: sensitivity, specificity, and their complements the false-negative and false-positive rates), and two read *across the rows* (conditioning on the test result: positive and negative predictive value). Only the column-wise quantities are properties of the instrument in isolation. The row-wise quantities are properties of the instrument *and* the population it is applied to. Reporting a screener's "accuracy" without saying which of the two families is meant is therefore ambiguous by construction.
    - **Link to source:** [Altman & Bland, sensitivity/specificity](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.308.6943.1552)
  - **Source:** Kerr, Wang, Janes, McClelland, Psaty & Pepe (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a two-risk-category setting, the components of the net reclassification index are algebraically the same quantities as the changes in the true-positive and false-positive rates, and the authors "advocate the use of true- and false-positive rates and suggest it is more useful for investigators to retain the existing, descriptive terms." *(Kerr et al., 2014, *Epidemiology* 25(1):114–121, DOI 10.1097/EDE.0000000000000018.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A methodological review of a newer competing metric arrives back at the plain 2×2 vocabulary as the recommended reporting language. That is a useful discipline: novel summary indices frequently turn out to be repackagings of true- and false-positive rates, and the repackaging usually costs interpretability without buying information.
    - **Link to source:** [Kerr et al. critical review](https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000000018)
  - **Source:** McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In the specific context of gifted identification, the authors note that specificity "tends to be very high in even poor-performing gifted identification systems because only a small proportion of students are typically identified," and for that reason they shift their analysis to the *incorrect identification rate* — the probability that a non-gifted student is identified. *(McBee, Peters & Miller, 2016, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 60(4):258–278, DOI 10.1177/0016986216656256.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** When the selected fraction is small, specificity is pinned near 1.00 almost regardless of instrument quality, so it carries essentially no discriminating information between a good screener and a bad one. This is a concrete instance of the general warning in 4.2: at low prevalence, the headline-friendly metrics saturate while the decision-relevant ones do not.
    - **Link to source:** [McBee, Peters & Miller (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256)

- **Subcategory 4.2: Base rates, Bayes' theorem, and the collapse of positive predictive value at low prevalence**
  - **Source:** Meehl & Rosen (1955)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Meehl and Rosen's paper is titled "Antecedent probability and the efficiency of psychometric signs, patterns, or cutting scores" and appeared in *Psychological Bulletin* in 1955. *(Meehl & Rosen, 1955, *Psychological Bulletin* 52(3):194–216, DOI 10.1037/h0048070.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The base-rate problem for cutting scores is not a recent discovery from clinical epidemiology; it was posed inside psychological measurement seventy years ago, framed exactly as a question about whether a psychometric sign or cut score improves on the antecedent probability. Any modern screener argument that ignores the base rate is re-litigating a settled point.
    - **Link to source:** [Meehl & Rosen (1955)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048070)
  - **Source:** Casscells, Schoenberger & Graboys (1978); Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Casscells, Schoenberger and Graboys published "Interpretation by physicians of clinical laboratory results" in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1978. *(Casscells, Schoenberger & Graboys, 1978, *NEJM* 299(18):999–1001, DOI 10.1056/NEJM197811022991808.)* `T2`
      - Gigerenzer and Hoffrage's "How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: Frequency formats" appeared in *Psychological Review* in 1995. *(Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995, *Psychological Review* 102(4):684–704, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is a documented literature showing that trained professionals systematically misread a positive screening result as though it were a statement about the person rather than a statement conditioned on the base rate, and a documented remedy — presenting the same information as natural frequencies over a concrete cohort rather than as probabilities. Both facts matter for how a screener's performance should be *communicated*, not only how it is computed.
    - **Link to source:** [Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684)
  - **Source:** *Illustrative computation by the present author* (arithmetic shown; not an empirical finding)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Illustration, not evidence.** Take a hypothetical cohort of 10,000 and a criterion defined so that 2% qualify (200 qualifying cases, 9,800 non-qualifying) — the kind of prevalence a threshold-defined construct has *by construction*. With sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.90: TP = 0.90 × 200 = 180; FN = 20; FP = 0.10 × 9,800 = 980; TN = 8,820. PPV = 180 / (180 + 980) = 180/1160 = **0.155**. NPV = 8,820 / (8,820 + 20) = 8820/8840 = **0.9977**. Screen-positive rate = 1,160/10,000 = 11.6%. `T3`
      - **Illustration, continued.** Holding sensitivity at 0.90 but raising specificity to 0.99: FP = 0.01 × 9,800 = 98; PPV = 180 / (180 + 98) = 180/278 = **0.647**. Holding sensitivity fixed and cutting the false-positive rate from 10% to 1% moves PPV from 0.155 to 0.647 — a gain of 49.2 percentage points from a change in specificity alone. `T3`
      - **Illustration, continued.** The identical sensitivity/specificity pair (0.90/0.90) applied at 50% prevalence yields PPV = 4,500/(4,500 + 500) = **0.90**. PPV therefore falls from 0.90 to 0.155 with no change whatsoever in the instrument, purely from the base rate. `T3`
      - **Cross-check via the odds form of Bayes' theorem.** Positive likelihood ratio LR+ = sensitivity/(1 − specificity) = 0.90/0.10 = 9. Prior odds = 0.02/0.98 = 0.020408. Posterior odds = 0.020408 × 9 = 0.183673. PPV = 0.183673/1.183673 = **0.15517**, matching the frequency table above. With specificity 0.99, LR+ = 90, posterior odds = 1.83673, PPV = 1.83673/2.83673 = **0.64748**, again matching. `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two things follow from the arithmetic and can be checked line by line. First, PPV is not a property of the screener; it is a property of the screener crossed with the population, and at low prevalence it is dominated by the false-positive rate rather than by sensitivity. Second, the two standard routes to the same number — a frequency table over a concrete cohort, and prior odds multiplied by the likelihood ratio — agree exactly, which is the practical reason to compute both as a self-check. The uncomfortable implication for any rare-by-construction criterion is that a screener can be genuinely excellent on the metrics that describe the instrument while most of the people it flags do not meet the criterion.
    - **Link to source:** [Bayes' theorem in diagnostic form (Altman & Bland, predictive values)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.309.6947.102)

- **Subcategory 4.3: ROC analysis, AUC, the concordance interpretation, and partial AUC**
  - **Source:** Hanley & McNeil (1982)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The area under an ROC curve "represents the probability that a randomly chosen diseased subject is (correctly) rated or ranked with greater suspicion than a randomly chosen non-diseased subject." *(Hanley & McNeil, 1982, *Radiology* 143(1):29–36, DOI 10.1148/radiology.143.1.7063747.)* `T2`
      - That probability "is the same quantity that is estimated by the already well-studied nonparametric Wilcoxon statistic." *(Hanley & McNeil, 1982, *Radiology* 143(1):29–36, DOI 10.1148/radiology.143.1.7063747.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** AUC is a *ranking* statistic with a precise meaning: the probability that a randomly drawn qualifying case outscores a randomly drawn non-qualifying case. It is identical to the Wilcoxon/Mann–Whitney statistic. Everything AUC can tell you is about pairwise ordering across the entire score range — which is exactly why it cannot, on its own, answer a question about a decision made at one threshold.
    - **Link to source:** [Hanley & McNeil (1982)](https://doi.org/10.1148/radiology.143.1.7063747)
  - **Source:** Swets (1988)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Swets published "Measuring the accuracy of diagnostic systems" in *Science* in 1988. *(Swets, 1988, *Science* 240(4857):1285–1293, DOI 10.1126/science.3287615.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The canonical general-science statement of ROC methodology as a way to separate *discrimination* (how well the scores order cases) from *decision criterion* (where the threshold is placed). That separation is the conceptual foundation for everything downstream in this category: a screener can have identical discrimination to an incumbent and a completely different decision profile, or vice versa.
    - **Link to source:** [Swets (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3287615)
  - **Source:** McClish (1989); Jiang, Metz & Nishikawa (1996)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - McClish's paper states that "when comparing two ROC curves, though, problems arise when interest does not lie in the entire range of false-positive rates (and hence the entire area)," and proposes numerical integration over a portion of the curve with derived variance estimates. *(McClish, 1989, *Medical Decision Making* 9(3):190–195, DOI 10.1177/0272989X8900900307.)* `T2`
      - A standardized (rescaled) partial AUC index was proposed in that same paper to improve interpretability of the partial area. *(McClish, 1989, *Medical Decision Making* 9(3):190–195, DOI 10.1177/0272989X8900900307.)* `T2`
      - Jiang, Metz and Nishikawa published a partial-area index specifically for the high-sensitivity region of the ROC curve. *(Jiang, Metz & Nishikawa, 1996, *Radiology* 201(3):745–750, DOI 10.1148/radiology.201.3.8939225.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Partial AUC exists because full AUC integrates over operating points nobody would ever adopt. If a program can only afford to send, say, the top 10% for full assessment, then performance at a 60% false-positive rate is irrelevant to the decision and should not be allowed to contribute to the headline number. Two variants matter: restricting the false-positive-rate range (McClish) and restricting the *sensitivity* range (Jiang et al.), and which one you want depends on whether the binding constraint is capacity or missed cases.
    - **Link to source:** [McClish (1989)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X8900900307)
  - **Source:** Cook (2007); Lobo, Jiménez-Valverde & Real (2008); Hand (2009); Saito & Rehmsmeier (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Cook argues the c statistic "may not be optimal in assessing models that predict future risk or stratify individuals into risk categories," that calibration is equally important in that setting, and that "use of the c statistic for model selection could thus naively eliminate established risk factors." *(Cook, 2007, *Circulation* 115(7):928–935, DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.106.672402.)* `T2`
      - Cook further states that perfectly calibrated models for complex outcomes "can, in fact, only achieve values for the c statistic well below the theoretical maximum of 1." *(Cook, 2007, *Circulation* 115(7):928–935, DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.106.672402.)* `T2`
      - Lobo, Jiménez-Valverde and Real published a paper titled "AUC: a misleading measure of the performance of predictive distribution models." *(Lobo, Jiménez-Valverde & Real, 2008, *Global Ecology and Biogeography* 17(2):145–151, DOI 10.1111/j.1466-8238.2007.00358.x.)* `T2`
      - Hand published "Measuring classifier performance: a coherent alternative to the area under the ROC curve," arguing for a replacement measure. *(Hand, 2009, *Machine Learning* 77(1):103–123, DOI 10.1007/s10994-009-5119-5.)* `T2`
      - Saito and Rehmsmeier report that "the precision-recall plot is more informative than the ROC plot when evaluating binary classifiers on imbalanced datasets." *(Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015, *PLOS ONE* 10(3):e0118432, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0118432.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The case against AUC-as-verdict comes from four independent directions and is unusually consistent. It ignores calibration; it is insensitive to changes that materially move individuals across a decision boundary; Hand's critique is that it implicitly averages over a *different* misclassification-cost distribution for each classifier being compared, so the comparison is not apples-to-apples; and under class imbalance — the defining condition of a rare-criterion screener — the precision-recall plot exposes performance differences that the ROC plot visually flattens. A defensible evaluation reports AUC as one line item, not as the finding.
    - **Link to source:** [Cook (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.106.672402)

- **Subcategory 4.4: Statistically comparing two instruments' discrimination — DeLong, bootstrap, and NRI with its documented criticisms**
  - **Source:** DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson (1988); Hanley & McNeil (1983)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "When two or more empirical curves are constructed based on tests performed on the same individuals, statistical analysis on differences between curves must take into account the correlated nature of the data"; the paper supplies a nonparametric approach "by using the theory on generalized U-statistics to generate an estimated covariance matrix." *(DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson, 1988, *Biometrics* 44(3):837–845, DOI 10.2307/2531595.)* `T2`
      - Hanley and McNeil's earlier treatment of the same problem assumes underlying Gaussian (binormal) distributions and supplies a table converting observed correlations in paired ratings into a correlation between the two ROC areas; the correction for pairing "can produce a considerable increase in the statistical sensitivity (power) of the comparison." *(Hanley & McNeil, 1983, *Radiology* 148(3):839–843, DOI 10.1148/radiology.148.3.6878708.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** If both instruments are administered to the same people — which is the natural design for a head-to-head screener comparison — the two AUC estimates are correlated, and treating them as independent inflates the standard error of their difference and throws away power. DeLong's method is the distribution-free solution and is the field default; Hanley & McNeil's is the binormal-parametric predecessor. The practical consequence is that the paired design is *cheaper*, not just cleaner.
    - **Link to source:** [DeLong et al. (1988)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2531595)
  - **Source:** Pencina, D'Agostino, D'Agostino & Vasan (2008)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The paper introduces two measures — the net reclassification improvement (NRI), based on reclassification tables, and the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), based on integrated sensitivity and specificity — and develops "simple asymptotic tests of significance" for them. *(Pencina et al., 2008, *Statistics in Medicine* 27(2):157–172, DOI 10.1002/sim.2929.)* `T2`
      - The authors' stated recommendation is that these measures be considered "in addition to the AUC," not instead of it. *(Pencina et al., 2008, *Statistics in Medicine* 27(2):157–172, DOI 10.1002/sim.2929.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** NRI was designed to answer precisely the question "does adding this new marker move people across decision-relevant boundaries in the right direction," which is closer to the screener-replacement question than ΔAUC is. That motivation is legitimate. The execution is where the literature turns hostile — see the four critiques below, which should be read as a package with the original.
    - **Link to source:** [Pencina et al. (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.2929)
  - **Source:** Hilden & Gerds (2014); Pepe, Fan, Feng, Gerds & Hilden (2015); Kerr et al. (2014); Leening et al. (2014) and Gerds & Hilden (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "If IDI and NRI are used to measure gain in prediction performance, then poorly calibrated models may appear advantageous, and in a simulation study, even the model that actually generates the data (and hence is the best possible model) can be improved on without adding measured information." *(Hilden & Gerds, 2014, *Statistics in Medicine* 33(19):3405–3414, DOI 10.1002/sim.5804; published online 2013.)* `T2`
      - The same paper contrasts NRI and IDI with AUC and the Brier score, noting that "unlike IDI and NRI, these traditional measures have the characteristic that prognostic performance cannot be accidentally or deliberately inflated." *(Hilden & Gerds, 2014, *Statistics in Medicine* 33(19):3405–3414, DOI 10.1002/sim.5804.)* `T2`
      - Pepe and colleagues "demonstrate the alarming result that the NRI statistic calculated on a large test dataset using risk models derived from a training set is likely to be positive even when the new marker has no predictive information," and provide a theoretical example in which an incorrect risk function including an uninformative marker is *proven* to yield a positive NRI. *(Pepe, Fan, Feng, Gerds & Hilden, 2015, *Statistics in Biosciences* 7(2):282–295, DOI 10.1007/s12561-014-9118-0.)* `T2`
      - The same paper reports a simulated case in which NRI = 56.21% while ΔAUC = −0.59%, ΔBrier = −1.10%, and the change in standardized net benefit = −1.49% — that is, NRI declared a large improvement while every other measure declared a small deterioration. *(Pepe et al., 2015, *Statistics in Biosciences* 7(2):282–295, DOI 10.1007/s12561-014-9118-0.)* `T2`
      - Kerr et al. recommend reporting net reclassification indices separately for cases and controls; recommend *against* the index when there are three or more risk categories "because they do not account for clinically important differences in shifts among risk categories"; state that the category-free NRI "can mislead investigators by overstating the incremental value of a biomarker, even in independent validation data"; state that for hypothesis testing "the well-established tests for coefficients in the regression model are superior to the net reclassification index"; state that confidence intervals "should be calculated using bootstrap methods rather than published variance formulas"; and conclude that "the preferred single-number summary of the prediction increment is the improvement in net benefit." *(Kerr et al., 2014, *Epidemiology* 25(1):114–121, DOI 10.1097/EDE.0000000000000018.)* `T1`
      - The dispute was carried out in print: Leening, Steyerberg, Van Calster, D'Agostino and Pencina replied that NRI and IDI "require calibrated models," and Gerds and Hilden rejoined that "calibration of models is not sufficient to justify NRI." *(Leening et al., 2014, *Statistics in Medicine* 33(19):3415–3418, DOI 10.1002/sim.6133; Gerds & Hilden, 2014, *Statistics in Medicine* 33(19):3419–3420, DOI 10.1002/sim.6212.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** NRI is the single most dangerous statistic in this category for someone trying to show a new screener beats an incumbent, because it is biased *in the direction the analyst wants*. The mechanism is documented and not subtle: the index is not a proper scoring rule, so a miscalibrated or overfit new model can post a large positive NRI purely from the miscalibration, and this survives evaluation on independent test data. The published defence — that both models must be well calibrated — was itself contested in the same journal issue. The methodological review's own conclusion is that if you want one number for "is the new instrument better," use the improvement in net benefit (subcategory 4.5), and if you want a hypothesis test, use a regression coefficient test or a DeLong-type comparison. Reporting NRI without these caveats should be read as a red flag about the analyst, not as evidence about the instrument.
    - **Link to source:** [Pepe et al. (2015), NRI misleading](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12561-014-9118-0)
  - **Source:** Pepe (2004)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Pepe and colleagues published "Limitations of the odds ratio in gauging the performance of a diagnostic, prognostic, or screening marker" in the *American Journal of Epidemiology*. *(Pepe et al., 2004, *American Journal of Epidemiology* 159(9):882–890, DOI 10.1093/aje/kwh101.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A marker can have a large, highly significant odds ratio and still be nearly useless as a classifier. This is the association-versus-classification gap in its cleanest form, and it is why a regression table showing that a new instrument "significantly predicts" the criterion is not, by itself, an answer to whether it should replace the incumbent.
    - **Link to source:** [Pepe et al. (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwh101)

- **Subcategory 4.5: Decision curve analysis and net benefit — evaluating across threshold preferences instead of at one arbitrary cut**
  - **Source:** Vickers & Elkin (2006)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The paper's stated premise is that "diagnostic and prognostic models are typically evaluated with measures of accuracy that do not address clinical consequences," while decision-analytic techniques "often require collection of additional information and may be cumbersome to apply." *(Vickers & Elkin, 2006, *Medical Decision Making* 26(6):565–574, DOI 10.1177/0272989X06295361.)* `T2`
      - The method assumes "that the threshold probability of a disease or event at which a patient would opt for treatment is informative of how the patient weighs the relative harms of a false-positive and a false-negative prediction"; this relationship is used to derive net benefit across threshold probabilities, and "plotting net benefit against threshold probability yields the 'decision curve.'" *(Vickers & Elkin, 2006, *Medical Decision Making* 26(6):565–574, DOI 10.1177/0272989X06295361.)* `T2`
      - The method "requires only the data set on which the models are tested" — no external utility elicitation. *(Vickers & Elkin, 2006, *Medical Decision Making* 26(6):565–574, DOI 10.1177/0272989X06295361.)* `T2`
      - Decision curve analysis identified, in the authors' worked application, "the range of threshold probabilities in which a model was of value, the magnitude of benefit, and which of several models was optimal." *(Vickers & Elkin, 2006, *Medical Decision Making* 26(6):565–574, DOI 10.1177/0272989X06295361.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Net benefit converts the false-positive count into true-positive-equivalents using an exchange rate implied by the threshold, then subtracts. Sweeping the threshold across its plausible range produces a curve, and the curve is compared not just against the rival model but against the two default strategies — treat everyone and treat no one. That last comparison is the part people skip and the part that most often kills a screener: an instrument can beat its rival on AUC and still fail to beat "screen everybody" or "screen nobody" over the threshold range anyone would actually adopt.
    - **Link to source:** [Vickers & Elkin (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X06295361)
  - **Source:** Vickers, Van Calster & Steyerberg (2016, 2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors published "Net benefit approaches to the evaluation of prediction models, molecular markers, and diagnostic tests" in the *BMJ* in 2016. *(Vickers, Van Calster & Steyerberg, 2016, *BMJ* 352:i6, DOI 10.1136/bmj.i6.)* `T2`
      - The same three authors published "A simple, step-by-step guide to interpreting decision curve analysis" in *Diagnostic and Prognostic Research* in 2019. *(Vickers, van Calster & Steyerberg, 2019, *Diagnostic and Prognostic Research* 3, article 18, DOI 10.1186/s41512-019-0064-7.)* `T2`
      - The TRIPOD+AI reporting statement for clinical prediction models was published in the *BMJ* in 2024 by Collins and colleagues. *(Collins et al., 2024, *BMJ* 385:e078378, DOI 10.1136/bmj-2023-078378.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Net benefit is not a fringe proposal; it has a *BMJ* methods paper, an interpretation guide, and endorsement by the field's dominant reporting standard for prediction models. Its main limitation is honest and worth stating: the threshold range you plot is a value judgement about the relative cost of a miss versus a false alarm, and if reasonable stakeholders disagree about that range, the decision curve will not adjudicate for you — it will show you exactly where they diverge, which is arguably the more useful output.
    - **Link to source:** [Vickers et al. (2019) DCA guide](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41512-019-0064-7)
  - **Source:** Wynants, van Smeden, McLernon, Timmerman, Steyerberg & Van Calster (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors published "Three myths about risk thresholds for prediction models" in *BMC Medicine*. *(Wynants et al., 2019, *BMC Medicine* 17, article 192, DOI 10.1186/s12916-019-1425-3.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The critique that belongs alongside DCA rather than against it: thresholds are commonly treated as if they were empirical discoveries ("the optimal cutpoint") rather than value choices, and are commonly assumed to be a single number rather than a defensible range. Any screener evaluation that reports one "optimal" cut without saying whose preferences generated it is making a values claim in statistical clothing.
    - **Link to source:** [Wynants et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-019-1425-3)

- **Subcategory 4.6: Classical selection utility — Taylor–Russell, Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser, and expectancy tables**
  - **Source:** Taylor & Russell (1939)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The authors frame their question as: "If a given per cent of the present employee group is considered successful and if a given proportion of the applicant group is to be selected, what proportion will be successful in the new group selected by means of the test?" *(Taylor & Russell, 1939, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 23(5):565–578, DOI 10.1037/h0057079.)* `T2`
      - Their stated motivation is that "the emphasis in current literature upon the k and E methods of interpreting correlation coefficients has led to some unwarranted pessimism … concerning the practical usefulness in an employment situation of validity coefficients in the range of those usually obtained," and they set out to show "the very considerable improvement in selection efficiency which may be obtained with small correlation coefficients." *(Taylor & Russell, 1939, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 23(5):565–578, DOI 10.1037/h0057079.)* `T2`
      - The model has three inputs — the validity coefficient, the selection ratio, and the base rate — and returns the success ratio among those selected. *(Taylor & Russell, 1939, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 23(5):565–578, DOI 10.1037/h0057079.)* `T2`
      - A CRAN package `TaylorRussell` implements the original model and generalizes it to multiple predictors; its documentation states that users can specify any three of the four parameters (base rate, selection ratio, criterion validity, positive predictive value) and estimate the fourth. *(Waller, `TaylorRussell` package, CRAN, DOI 10.32614/CRAN.package.taylorrussell.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Taylor–Russell is the industrial-psychology twin of the Bayes calculation in 4.2, and it was published sixteen years earlier than Meehl & Rosen. Its "success ratio" *is* positive predictive value; its "base rate" *is* prevalence; its "selection ratio" is the screen-positive rate. The reason it deserves separate treatment is the parameter it adds: the selection ratio is under institutional control in a way that prevalence is not, and the model makes explicit that the same validity coefficient buys wildly different practical value depending on how selective you can afford to be. Note the argumentative direction, which is the opposite of the modern diagnostic literature — Taylor and Russell were arguing that *low* validity coefficients are more useful than people thought, whereas 4.2 warns that good sensitivity/specificity are less useful than people think. Both are correct; they differ in which parameter they hold fixed.
    - **Link to source:** [Taylor & Russell (1939)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0057079)
  - **Source:** Brogden (1949); Cronbach & Gleser (1965)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Brogden's "When testing pays off" appeared in *Personnel Psychology* in 1949. *(Brogden, 1949, *Personnel Psychology* 2(2):171–183, DOI 10.1111/j.1744-6570.1949.tb01397.x.)* `T2`

- **Subcategory 4.7: Standard setting — how a cut score is defensibly established, and how much the method changes it**
  - **Source:** Angoff (1971); Kane (1994)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Angoff's "Scales, norms, and equivalent scores" is Chapter 15 of the second edition of *Educational Measurement*, edited by R. L.       - Kane published "Validating the performance standards associated with passing scores" in the *Review of Educational Research*. *(Kane, 1994, *Review of Educational Research* 64(3):425–461, DOI 10.3102/00346543064003425.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Angoff method — panellists estimate, item by item, the probability that a minimally qualified candidate answers correctly, and the summed estimates become the cut — traces to a chapter that is otherwise about scaling and equating, which is itself a small indictment of how casually cut scores entered practice. Kane's contribution is the reframing that governs the modern literature: a passing score is not "found," it is a *claim* that requires its own validity argument, comprising procedural, internal, and external evidence.
    - **Link to source:** [Kane (1994)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064003425)
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014), Standards 5.21–5.23
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Standard 5.21: "When proposed score interpretations involve one or more cut scores, the rationale and procedures used for establishing cut scores should be documented clearly." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 5.21, pp. 107–108.)* `T1`
      - The comment to Standard 5.21 states that "adequate precision in regions of score scales where cut scores are established is prerequisite to reliable classification of examinees into categories," and requires documentation of panellist selection and qualifications, training, feedback, and opportunities to confer; "where applicable, variability over participants should be reported." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 5.21 comment, pp. 107–108.)* `T1`

      - The comment to Standard 5.23 states: "In general, one would not expect to find a sharp difference in levels of the criterion variable between those just below and those just above the cut score, but evidence should be provided, where feasible, of a relationship between test and criterion performance over a score interval that includes or approaches the cut score." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 5.23 comment, p. 109.)* `T1`
      - The comment to Standard 5.21 draws an explicit distinction: when a cut score exists to select a fixed number of candidates, "little further documentation may be needed concerning the specific question of how the cut scores are established," whereas when cut scores classify examinees into distinct categories with no pre-established quota, "the standard-setting method must be documented in more detail." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 5.21 comment, pp. 107–108.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The professional standard already anticipates the two failure modes. It demands an estimate of *panel-replication variance* in the cut score — that is, it treats the cut score as an estimate with a standard error attributable to who happened to be in the room. And it warns against the fiction of a sharp criterion discontinuity at the cut. Note the exemption in 5.21: a capacity-driven "top N" rule is held to a *lower* documentation bar than a criterion-referenced "meets the standard" rule, because the former makes no claim about what the score means. Which of those two things a screening cut is claiming to be therefore determines how much standard-setting evidence is owed.

      - Applying the Angoff, Patient Safety, Borderline Group and Contrasting Groups methods to the same clinical skills assessment, "the cut scores ranged from 65.9% to 86.1%, with the Angoff method resulting in the lowest cut score and the Borderline Group method resulting in the highest cut score." *(Tappan et al., 2025, *The Clinical Teacher* 22(5):e70198, DOI 10.1111/tct.70198.)* `T2`
      - Applying those four cut scores to the same historical dataset (n = 92) "resulted in pass rates ranging from 60.9% to 98.9%." *(Tappan et al., 2025, *The Clinical Teacher* 22(5):e70198, DOI 10.1111/tct.70198.)* `T2`
      - The authors state directly that standard-setting methods "lead to varied cut scores, and guidance for method selection is limited." *(Tappan et al., 2025, *The Clinical Teacher* 22(5):e70198, DOI 10.1111/tct.70198.)* `T2`
      - In an independent comparison on a 32-item laboratory skills test with two separate 12-member expert panels, "the obtained cut-scores was 17.67 for Angoff and 18.8 for bookmark," with pass rates of 55.78% and 41.36% respectively. *(Yousefi Afrashteh, 2021, *BMC Medical Education* 21, article 1, DOI 10.1186/s12909-020-02436-3.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest empirical fact in the subcategory and it is uncomfortable: on the same instrument and the same examinees, the choice of standard-setting method moved the pass rate from 61% to 99% in one study, and from 41% to 56% in another. The cut score is not a property of the test. It is a property of the procedure used to set it, and the procedure is a defensible-but-contestable choice. Any claim that a screener "identifies X% of qualifying candidates" is therefore partly a claim about a methodological decision that could reasonably have gone otherwise — which is exactly why Standard 5.21 demands that the replication variance be estimated and reported.

- **Subcategory 4.8: Cut-score error — conditional SEM, classification consistency, and unreliability near the boundary**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014), Standards 2.13–2.16
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Standard 2.14: "When possible and appropriate, conditional standard errors of measurement should be reported at several score levels unless there is evidence that the standard error is constant across score levels. Where cut scores are specified for selection or classification, the standard errors of measurement should be reported in the vicinity of each cut score." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.14, p. 45.)* `T1`
      - Standard 2.16: "When a test or combination of measures is used to make classification decisions, estimates should be provided of the percentage of test takers who would be classified in the same way on two replications of the procedure." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.16, p. 46.)* `T1`
      - The comment to Standard 2.16 states that "the standard error of measurement at or near the cut scores has important implications for the trustworthiness of these decisions," but that "the standard error cannot be translated into the expected percentage of consistent or accurate decisions without strong assumptions about the distributions of measurement errors and true scores." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.16 comment, p. 46.)* `T1`
      - Standard 2.15 requires investigation and reporting when there is credible evidence that conditional standard errors or test information functions "will differ substantially for various subgroups." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.15, pp. 45–46.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The professional standard says three separable things. Measurement error varies by score level, so the average SEM understates uncertainty in some regions and overstates it in others — and the region you must report is the one around the cut. Decision consistency is a distinct quantity from SEM and must be estimated separately, because converting one into the other requires distributional assumptions the standard explicitly flags as strong. And if conditional error differs by subgroup, the *same* cut score delivers systematically different misclassification rates to different groups, which is a fairness question arising directly out of a reliability statistic.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (2014 edition, publicly posted copy)](http://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)
  - **Source:** Livingston & Lewis (1995)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Livingston and Lewis published "Estimating the consistency and accuracy of classifications based on test scores" in the *Journal of Educational Measurement*. *(Livingston & Lewis, 1995, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 32(2):179–197, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The standard operational method for the quantity Standard 2.16 demands, and one that distinguishes two things routinely conflated: classification *consistency* (would a second administration put this person in the same category?) and classification *accuracy* (does the category match the person's true status?). - **Subcategory 4.9: Sequential and multi-stage screening — how combining instruments changes the error profile**
  - **Source:** Marshall (1989); Fanshawe, Nicholson, Perera & Oke (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Marshall's analysis concludes that "unless the disease and disease-free test associations are highly asymmetric, the believe-the-positive rule will lead to higher negative and lower positive predictive value. Conversely, the believe-the-negative rule will increase the positive and decrease the negative predictive value." *(Marshall, 1989, *Biometrics* 45(4):1213–1222, DOI 10.2307/2531772.)* `T2`
      - The same paper states that these combination rules "do not necessarily have better predictive values than a single test; whether they do depends on the association between test outcomes." *(Marshall, 1989, *Biometrics* 45(4):1213–1222, DOI 10.2307/2531772.)* `T2`
      - A methods review names the two rules explicitly: the "OR" rule (also "believe the positive" or "Any+"), where a positive is declared if either test is positive, and the "AND" rule (also "believe the negative" or "Both+"), where both must be positive; a third, "believe the extreme," applies the second test only to those with an indeterminate first result. *(Fanshawe, Nicholson, Perera & Oke, 2024, *Diagnostic and Prognostic Research* 8, article 8, DOI 10.1186/s41512-024-00175-3.)* `T1`
      - The same review states that in most realistic scenarios the OR rule decreases PPV and increases NPV and the AND rule does the reverse, "although this tendency is not guaranteed and may not occur if the index tests are conditionally highly correlated, with differential strength of correlation among disease-positive and disease-negative cases." *(Fanshawe et al., 2024, *Diagnostic and Prognostic Research* 8, article 8, DOI 10.1186/s41512-024-00175-3.)* `T1`
      - The review notes that closed-form expressions for the combined sensitivity and specificity involve conditional terms of the form P(X₂ | X₁, T), so the conditional dependence between the two tests directly determines the performance of the sequence. *(Fanshawe et al., 2024, *Diagnostic and Prognostic Research* 8, article 8, DOI 10.1186/s41512-024-00175-3.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Requiring both instruments to pass raises specificity and lowers sensitivity; requiring either raises sensitivity and lowers specificity. That much is folklore and it is correct in the typical case. The non-folklore part, which the primary source and the review both stress, is that the size and even the *direction* of the effect depends on how correlated the two instruments are conditional on true status — and that correlation is usually not estimated, because doing so requires administering both instruments to the same people including those the first stage screened out. A two-stage system evaluated only on those who passed stage one cannot recover the quantity it needs.
    - **Link to source:** [Fanshawe et al. (2024) sequential testing review](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41512-024-00175-3)
  - **Source:** *Illustrative computation by the present author* (arithmetic shown; not an empirical finding)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Illustration, not evidence; assumes conditional independence, which Marshall and Fanshawe et al. both indicate is usually violated.** Two instruments, each with sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.90, applied to the 2%-prevalence cohort of 10,000 from 4.2. Under the AND rule, combined sensitivity = 0.90 × 0.90 = 0.81 and combined specificity = 1 − (0.10 × 0.10) = 0.99. Then TP = 0.81 × 200 = 162; FN = 38; FP = 0.01 × 9,800 = 98; PPV = 162/(162 + 98) = 162/260 = **0.623**. Cross-check by odds: LR+ = 0.81/0.01 = 81; posterior odds = 0.020408 × 81 = 1.65306; PPV = 1.65306/2.65306 = **0.62308**. `T3`
      - **Illustration, continued.** Under the OR rule with the same two instruments, combined sensitivity = 1 − (0.10 × 0.10) = 0.99 and combined specificity = 0.90 × 0.90 = 0.81. Then TP = 198; FN = 2; FP = 0.19 × 9,800 = 1,862; PPV = 198/(198 + 1,862) = 198/2060 = **0.096**; screen-positive rate = 2,060/10,000 = 20.6%. `T3`

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The trade is not marginal, it is severe, and it runs in both directions at once. Under these illustrative numbers the conjunctive rule buys a large gain in the precision of the flagged group at the price of missing almost twice as many qualifying people, and the disjunctive rule almost eliminates misses at the price of a flagged group in which fewer than one in ten qualify. Neither is "more accurate"; they are different answers to the question of which error is worse — which is precisely the question decision curve analysis (4.5) is designed to make explicit rather than to settle by default.

  - **Source:** McBee, Peters & Miller (2016); McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014); Lakin (2018); Card & Giuliano (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - "Results showed that in nearly all conditions, identification systems that require a nomination before testing result in a large proportion of gifted students being missed.       - The same authors examined "the consequences of the conjunctive ('and'), disjunctive/complementary ('or'), and compensatory ('mean') models for combining scores from multiple assessments," considering the impact of rule choice on the size of the identified population, its ability heterogeneity, and the system's psychometric performance. *(McBee, Peters & Waterman, 2014, *Gifted Child Quarterly* 58(1):69–89, DOI 10.1177/0016986213513794.)* `T2`

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Taylor–Russell and the diagnostic literature argue in opposite directions from the same equation.** Taylor and Russell wrote to counter "unwarranted pessimism" and to show that modest validity coefficients deliver "very considerable improvement in selection efficiency" *(1939)*. The base-rate literature from Meehl & Rosen *(1955)* onward warns that apparently strong instruments deliver disappointing predictive value. Both are computing positive predictive value under a base rate. They differ in the comparator: Taylor–Russell compares the test against *random selection*, and the diagnostic tradition compares the test against *certainty*. A screener argument can be made to look good or bad purely by choosing which comparator goes unstated.

2. **The field's own recommended replacement for NRI is itself threshold-dependent.** Kerr et al. *(2014)* conclude that "the preferred single-number summary of the prediction increment is the improvement in net benefit," but net benefit is defined only relative to a threshold probability, and Wynants et al. *(2019)* argue that thresholds are widely misunderstood as empirical rather than value-laden. So the recommended escape from a biased summary statistic is a statistic that requires an explicit value judgement. That is arguably progress — the judgement becomes visible rather than smuggled — but it is not the clean single number the phrasing suggests.

3. **Partial AUC is the right restriction and it costs you the standard test.** Section 4.3 gives strong reasons to evaluate only the operating region anyone would adopt. Section 4.10 documents that `pROC` errors out if you ask for DeLong on a partial AUC, because DeLong's U-statistic covariance result applies to the full area. The methodologically preferable quantity and the methodologically standard test are not compatible without resampling.

4. **The AND rule looks like rigour and behaves like exclusion.** Requiring a candidate to clear both an incumbent and a new instrument raises PPV substantially (0.155 → 0.623 in the illustration in 4.9) and is easy to defend as conservative. It also nearly doubles the number of qualifying people missed, and McBee, Peters & Miller *(2016)* document false-negative rates above 60% in real multi-stage identification systems. "Conservative" describes the direction of the error, not its size.

5. **Standard 5.21 exempts capacity-driven cuts from the documentation burden that criterion-referenced cuts carry.** A "select the top N" rule needs "little further documentation" of how the cut was established; a "meets the standard" rule needs the full standard-setting record. Screening cuts are frequently justified with criterion-referenced language while being operationally set by capacity, which claims the interpretive authority of one regime under the evidentiary burden of the other.

6. **Reliability evidence and fairness evidence are the same evidence, read twice.** Standard 2.15 requires investigation when conditional SEM differs by subgroup. The moment it does, an identical cut score produces unequal misclassification rates by subgroup — a differential-error finding that arrives through a psychometric door rather than a fairness-audit door, and that a subgroup-blind accuracy report will not surface.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. They are cross-source connections that appear to hold, offered as things to attack.*

- **Seed A — "The comparison statistic the field recommends is the one the default tooling does not ship, and this is not a coincidence."** Kerr et al. *(2014)* name improvement in net benefit as the preferred single-number summary of a prediction increment; TRIPOD+AI *(2024)* endorses decision-analytic evaluation; yet section 4.10 documents that scikit-learn ships AUC, precision-recall, Brier score and threshold tuning while shipping neither DeLong nor net benefit, and that the two R decision-curve packages are a 2018-frozen one and one maintained by the method's institutional home. The hypothesis to test is that reported evaluation practice is shaped less by what the methods literature recommends than by what has a one-line function call — and if so, that the marginal return on tooling exceeds the marginal return on further methodological argument. *To interrogate: is this actually a tooling-availability effect, or does it merely correlate with one? What would count as disconfirming evidence?*

- **Seed B — "The screener-versus-incumbent question may be structurally unanswerable as usually posed, because the incumbent defines the criterion."** Section 4.4 supplies precise machinery for comparing two instruments' discrimination against a criterion. But where the operative criterion is itself a threshold on the incumbent instrument, the incumbent has an AUC of 1.00 against it by construction, and every comparison is rigged. Card & Giuliano *(2016)* sidestep this by holding eligibility fixed and varying only the *referral gate*, comparing who gets identified rather than who scores higher. The hypothesis is that when the criterion is instrument-defined, the only well-posed comparison is between *systems* at matched selection ratios — evaluated on stage-one sensitivity, subgroup composition, and net benefit — rather than between *instruments* on discrimination. *To interrogate: does this dissolve the question or merely relocate it? What criterion would make the instrument-level comparison well posed, and is it obtainable?*

- **Seed C — "Cut-score uncertainty is at least as large as instrument uncertainty, and only one of the two is routinely reported."** Tappan et al. *(2025)* found cut scores from 65.9% to 86.1% and pass rates from 60.9% to 98.9% on the same instrument and the same examinees, varying only the standard-setting method. Standard 5.21 already demands an estimate of the panel-replication variance in the cut. Meanwhile the entire apparatus of sections 4.3–4.4 and 4.11 is devoted to confidence intervals and power for the *instrument's* discrimination. The hypothesis is that for a fixed-threshold screening decision, the dominant term in total decision uncertainty is the cut score rather than the AUC — and that a confidence interval on the cut, propagated through to the classification table, would be more informative than a tighter interval on the AUC. *To interrogate: is this true in general or only when discrimination is already good? Can the two uncertainties be put on a common scale, and what happens to the comparison when they are?*

- **Seed D — "Combination rules are cost-shifting decisions dressed as accuracy decisions."** Marshall *(1989)* and Fanshawe et al. *(2024)* establish that AND and OR rules trade PPV against NPV with direction and magnitude governed by conditional dependence between the tests. Cronbach and Gleser *(1965)* devote a chapter to two-stage sequential selection precisely because staging exists to reduce testing cost. McBee et al. *(2016)* show the sensitivity cost of the resulting gate can exceed 60%. The hypothesis is that multi-stage screening is almost never adopted for accuracy reasons — it is adopted for throughput — and that framing it as an accuracy design choice systematically hides who absorbs the cost, since false negatives are borne by individuals and are invisible to the institution while false positives are borne by the institution and are visible in its budget. *To interrogate: is the asymmetry of visibility the actual mechanism, or a just-so story? Does the Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser utility model, which prices testing cost explicitly, already handle this — and if so, why is it not used here?*

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **The illustrative computations in 4.2 and 4.9 are arithmetic, not evidence.** They use invented sensitivity, specificity and prevalence values chosen for legibility. Every step is shown and cross-checked by a second method (frequency table and odds-form Bayes), so the arithmetic can be verified, but no empirical claim about any real instrument follows from them.
- **The AND/OR illustration assumes conditional independence between the two instruments, which is almost certainly false in practice.** Marshall *(1989)* and Fanshawe et al. *(2024)* both state that conditional dependence can change the magnitude and sometimes the direction of the effect. The illustrative numbers should be read as an upper bound on the benefit of combining, not an estimate.
- **I did not verify a peer-reviewed statement of the specific claim that "requiring both tests to pass raises specificity but lowers sensitivity" as a universal.** What is verified is Marshall's and Fanshawe's *predictive-value* formulation, which is explicitly hedged. The sensitivity/specificity version follows algebraically under conditional independence but I did not locate a primary source asserting it unconditionally, and I do not believe one exists, because it is not unconditionally true.
- **The bookmark standard-setting method's original source was not verified.** The method is discussed in the comparison studies cited in 4.7 (Tappan et al. 2025; Yousefi Afrashteh 2021), but I did not fetch and confirm the original Lewis/Mitzel/Green conference paper or the Mitzel et al. book chapter, so those primary citations are deliberately absent rather than asserted from memory.
- **Cizek & Bunch's standard-setting textbook is referenced by third parties in the sources I fetched but I did not verify it directly**, so it is not cited above.
- **The MedCalc sample-size figure (399 total) is a vendor manual illustration, not a peer-reviewed result**, and is highly sensitive to the assumed between-instrument correlation, which is typically unknown at design time.
- **Page numbers for Standards 2.14 and 2.16 (pp. 45–46) are inferred from the document's own table of contents ("Cluster 5. Standard Errors of Measurement … 45"), not read off a printed page marker.** The standard numbers and the quoted text are verbatim from the retrieved document and are reliable; treat the page numbers as approximate.
- **Hilden & Gerds appeared online in 2013 and in print in 2014** (*Stat Med* 33(19):3405–3414); Pencina et al. appeared online in 2007 and in the 2008 issue (27(2):157–172); Pepe et al. appeared online in 2014 and in the 2015 issue (7(2):282–295). Year-of-publication citations to these three vary legitimately across sources.
- **No source in this category addresses what to do when the criterion itself is proprietary or unobservable** — the situation Seed B is about. Every method here assumes a criterion that can be scored for both cases and non-cases. Methods for partially verified or verification-biased samples exist but were not searched for and are not represented above.
- **Subgroup-specific accuracy comparison (whether a new instrument's advantage over an incumbent is uniform across groups) is a distinct methodological literature that this category does not cover.** Standard 2.15 and Card & Giuliano *(2016)* point at it; the statistical machinery for testing subgroup differences in AUC or net benefit was not searched.
