### Category 5: Comparing a new instrument against an incumbent

*General idea: Showing that a new measurement instrument is "better than" an established one is not a single statistical test but a stack of separable claims — that the two instruments agree, that their scores can legitimately be placed on a common scale, that the new one adds predictive information beyond the old one, and that these findings survive honest out-of-sample estimation on an unrestricted sample. Each claim has its own estimator, its own assumptions, and a documented failure mode, and the most common defects in this literature are treating correlation as agreement, equating instruments that measure different constructs, and asserting incremental validity from a model in which the incumbent is measured with error.*

- **Subcategory 5.1: Correlation is not agreement — limits of agreement, intraclass correlation, and concordance**

  - **Source:** Bland & Altman (1986); Bland & Altman (1995); Bland & Altman (1999)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper proposing that agreement between two methods of clinical measurement be assessed by plotting the difference between paired measurements against their mean, and by computing "limits of agreement" as the mean difference plus and minus 1.96 standard deviations of the differences. *(Bland & Altman, 1986, *The Lancet* 327(8476):307–310, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(86)90837-8.)* `T2`
      - The same authors published a separate paper arguing that plotting the difference between two methods against the value from one of the two methods (rather than against their mean) is misleading. *(Bland & Altman, 1995, *The Lancet* 346(8982):1085–1087, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(95)91748-9.)* `T2`
      - An extended treatment covering repeated measurements, non-constant difference-versus-magnitude relationships (heteroscedasticity), and regression-based limits of agreement. *(Bland & Altman, 1999, *Statistical Methods in Medical Research* 8(2):135–160, DOI 10.1177/096228029900800204.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Bland–Altman approach abandons the question "do the two instruments covary?" for the question "how far apart are they, for an individual case, and is that gap acceptable?" It produces an interval on the raw score scale rather than a unitless coefficient, which forces the analyst to state a tolerance in advance. The 1995 paper is a specific methodological correction: plotting difference against one instrument's value induces a spurious correlation, so the mean of the two must be used when neither instrument is a gold standard.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** The 1.96-SD interval assumes differences are approximately normally distributed and that the bias and the variability of the difference are constant across the measurement range. Failure modes: (a) heteroscedastic differences make a single interval meaningless and require transformation or regression-based limits; (b) the limits are themselves estimates with sampling error, so reporting them without confidence intervals overstates precision; (c) the method describes agreement but cannot say whether the agreement is *good enough* — that threshold must be set substantively, outside the statistics.
    - **Link to source:** [Bland & Altman 1986](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(86)90837-8) · [Bland & Altman 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(95)91748-9) · [Bland & Altman 1999](https://doi.org/10.1177/096228029900800204)

  - **Source:** Shrout & Fleiss (1979); McGraw & Wong (1996); Koo & Li (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper defining a family of intraclass correlation coefficients for rater reliability distinguished by the underlying ANOVA model (one-way random, two-way random, two-way mixed), conventionally labelled ICC(1,1), ICC(2,1), ICC(3,1) and their k-rater averages. *(Shrout & Fleiss, 1979, *Psychological Bulletin* 86(2):420–428, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.86.2.420.)* `T2`
      - A paper reformulating intraclass correlation coefficients and distinguishing coefficients defined for *absolute agreement* from coefficients defined for *consistency*. *(McGraw & Wong, 1996, *Psychological Methods* 1(1):30–46, DOI 10.1037/1082-989X.1.1.30.)* `T2`
      - A guideline article stating that there are 10 forms of ICC, that each form involves distinct assumptions and yields different interpretations, and that researchers should explicitly specify the model, type, and definition used. *(Koo & Li, 2016, *Journal of Chiropractic Medicine* 15(2):155–163, DOI 10.1016/j.jcm.2016.02.012.)* `T1`
      - The same guideline proposes that, judged on the 95% confidence interval of the ICC estimate, values below 0.5 indicate poor reliability, 0.5–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, and above 0.90 excellent. *(Koo & Li, 2016, as above.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** "ICC" names a family, not a statistic. The choice among forms is determined by the study design (are the same two instruments applied to every case, or a random selection?) and by the question (do I care that the instruments rank cases identically, or that they produce the same number?). The consistency-versus-absolute-agreement distinction is the one that matters for head-to-head instrument comparison: a consistency ICC will look excellent even when one instrument is systematically several points higher than the other, because it absorbs the systematic offset into the model rather than penalising it.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** ICC forms assume the appropriate ANOVA model holds and that variance components are estimable; the absolute-agreement forms additionally assume that systematic between-instrument differences should count as disagreement. Failure modes: (a) reporting "ICC = 0.92" without specifying the form makes the number uninterpretable and non-reproducible; (b) ICC is a ratio of between-subject variance to total variance, so it inflates in heterogeneous samples and deflates in homogeneous ones — an ICC computed on a wide-ability sample will exceed the same instruments' ICC on an already-selected narrow-range sample, which is a range-restriction artefact rather than a property of the instruments (see Subcategory 5.6).
    - **Link to source:** [Shrout & Fleiss 1979](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.86.2.420) · [McGraw & Wong 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.1.1.30) · [Koo & Li 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcm.2016.02.012)

  - **Source:** Lin (1989); Nickerson (1997); Barnhart, Haber & Lin (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper introducing the concordance correlation coefficient, an index of reproducibility that evaluates agreement by the departure of paired observations from the 45-degree line through the origin. *(Lin, 1989, *Biometrics* 45(1):255–268, DOI 10.2307/2532051.)* `T2`
      - A correction to that paper was published in the same journal. *(Corrections, 2000, *Biometrics* 56(1):324–325, DOI 10.1111/j.0006-341X.2000.00324.x.)* `T2`
      - A published note critically examining the concordance correlation coefficient. *(Nickerson, 1997, "A Note on 'A Concordance Correlation Coefficient to Evaluate Reproducibility'," *Biometrics* 53(4):1503–1507, DOI 10.2307/2533516.)* `T2`
      - A review article surveying methods for assessing agreement with continuous measurements, co-authored by the originator of the concordance correlation coefficient. *(Barnhart, Haber & Lin, 2007, *Journal of Biopharmaceutical Statistics* 17(4):529–569, DOI 10.1080/10543400701376480.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Lin's coefficient is best understood as Pearson's correlation multiplied by a bias-correction factor: it rewards an instrument pair for being both linearly related *and* on the same scale with the same mean. It gives a single unitless number where Bland–Altman gives an interval, which makes it convenient for summary tables and dangerous as a sole report, because a single number hides whether the disagreement is a fixed offset (correctable by rescaling) or proportional drift (not correctable by rescaling).
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** The coefficient's standard form assumes a bivariate normal distribution for inference, and it is defined relative to the identity line, so it is not appropriate when the two instruments are legitimately on different scales. Failure modes: (a) like the ICC, it depends on between-subject variance and therefore on the sample's heterogeneity, so it is not comparable across studies with different sampling; (b) the published critique in the same journal indicates that its interpretation as a general agreement index has been contested in the peer-reviewed literature — it should be reported alongside, not instead of, a limits-of-agreement analysis.
    - **Link to source:** [Lin 1989](https://doi.org/10.2307/2532051) · [Nickerson 1997](https://doi.org/10.2307/2533516) · [Barnhart, Haber & Lin 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/10543400701376480)

  - **Source:** Kottner et al. (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A reporting guideline for reliability and agreement studies (GRRAS) was proposed by a multi-author group. *(Kottner, Audigé, Brorson, Donner, Gajewski, Hróbjartsson, et al., 2011, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* 64(1):96–106, DOI 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.03.002.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There exists a consensus reporting checklist specifically for reliability and agreement studies, which means the reporting expectations for a head-to-head agreement study are not a matter of the analyst's discretion — the sampling frame, the rater/instrument selection, the statistical model, and the handling of missing data are all expected to be pre-specified and disclosed.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** A reporting guideline governs transparency, not validity; a fully GRRAS-compliant report of a badly designed study is still a report of a badly designed study. Its failure mode in practice is checklist compliance substituting for design scrutiny.
    - **Link to source:** [GRRAS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.03.002)

- **Subcategory 5.2: Classification agreement — kappa, its documented paradoxes, and the alternatives**

  - **Source:** Cohen (1960); Cohen (1968); Landis & Koch (1977)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper introducing a coefficient of agreement for nominal scales that corrects observed agreement for agreement expected by chance. *(Cohen, 1960, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 20(1):37–46, DOI 10.1177/001316446002000104.)* `T2`
      - A subsequent paper introducing weighted kappa, which provides for scaled disagreement or partial credit rather than treating all disagreements as equivalent. *(Cohen, 1968, *Psychological Bulletin* 70(4):213–220, DOI 10.1037/h0026256.)* `T2`
      - A paper on the measurement of observer agreement for categorical data, the source of the widely reproduced verbal benchmarks for kappa magnitude. *(Landis & Koch, 1977, *Biometrics* 33(1):159–174, DOI 10.2307/2529310.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Kappa asks what fraction of the agreement beyond chance the two classifiers achieved. Weighted kappa extends this to ordered categories so that a one-band disagreement counts less than a three-band disagreement — which is the relevant form whenever the classification decision is an ordered cut on a continuous score rather than an unordered label.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** Kappa's chance-correction assumes the marginal distributions are the right model for "chance," which embeds the assumption that classifiers guess independently at their own observed base rates. Failure modes: (a) the quadratic-weighting choice is not neutral — quadratic weights make weighted kappa asymptotically equivalent to an intraclass correlation, so the "weighted kappa" reported can be silently a different statistic than the "kappa" a reader imagines; (b) the Landis–Koch verbal labels are conventions from a single 1977 paper with no distributional justification, and treating them as thresholds imports an arbitrary standard.
    - **Link to source:** [Cohen 1960](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316446002000104) · [Cohen 1968](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0026256) · [Landis & Koch 1977](https://doi.org/10.2307/2529310)

  - **Source:** Feinstein & Cicchetti (1990); Cicchetti & Feinstein (1990); Byrt, Bishop & Carlin (1993)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A two-part paper titled "High agreement but low kappa: I. The problems of two paradoxes," documenting that kappa can be low despite high observed agreement. *(Feinstein & Cicchetti, 1990, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* 43(6):543–549, DOI 10.1016/0895-4356(90)90158-L.)* `T2`
      - The companion paper, "High agreement but low kappa: II. Resolving the paradoxes." *(Cicchetti & Feinstein, 1990, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* 43(6):551–558, DOI 10.1016/0895-4356(90)90159-M.)* `T2`
      - A paper titled "Bias, prevalence and kappa," which formalises the two distortions as a prevalence index and a bias index and proposes a prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa. *(Byrt, Bishop & Carlin, 1993, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* 46(5):423–429, DOI 10.1016/0895-4356(93)90018-V.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Kappa has two named, published pathologies. The prevalence paradox: when one category is rare — which is definitionally the case for any selective classification decision — the chance-agreement term becomes large and kappa collapses even though the two classifiers agree on nearly every case. The bias paradox: when the two classifiers have systematically different marginal rates, kappa can move in a counterintuitive direction. Because a selection decision produces exactly the skewed marginals that trigger the prevalence paradox, kappa is arguably at its *least* trustworthy precisely in the setting where a new screener is compared to an incumbent screener on a small selected fraction.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** The prevalence- and bias-adjusted variant assumes it is legitimate to hypothetically rebalance the marginals, which changes what the coefficient estimates rather than fixing kappa. Failure mode: reporting a single adjusted kappa without also reporting observed agreement, the full contingency table, and the two marginal rates leaves the reader unable to tell which of the paradoxes is operating.
    - **Link to source:** [Feinstein & Cicchetti I](https://doi.org/10.1016/0895-4356(90)90158-L) · [Cicchetti & Feinstein II](https://doi.org/10.1016/0895-4356(90)90159-M) · [Byrt, Bishop & Carlin 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0895-4356(93)90018-V)

  - **Source:** Gwet (2008); Wongpakaran, Wongpakaran, Wedding & Gwet (2013)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper stating that pi and kappa statistics "occasionally yield unexpected results in situations known as the paradoxes of kappa," exploring the origin of those limitations, and introducing an alternative agreement coefficient designated AC1, together with variance estimators whose validity does not depend on an assumption of independence between raters. *(Gwet, 2008, *British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology* 61(1):29–48, DOI 10.1348/000711006X126600.)* `T2` `[COI: the author develops and commercially distributes agreement-coefficient software and self-published reference texts on inter-rater reliability]`
      - An empirical comparison of Cohen's kappa and Gwet's AC1 conducted with personality-disorder samples. *(Wongpakaran, Wongpakaran, Wedding & Gwet, 2013, *BMC Medical Research Methodology* 13:61, DOI 10.1186/1471-2288-13-61.)* `T2` `[COI: the originator of AC1 is a co-author of this comparative evaluation of AC1]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** AC1 replaces kappa's marginal-product model of chance agreement with a different chance model that is designed not to explode when one category is rare. It is the standard remedy proposed for the prevalence paradox. The important caveat is provenance: the coefficient's principal advocate is also its author and has a commercial interest in the associated software, so an independent replication of the AC1-versus-kappa comparison carries more evidential weight than the originator's own comparisons.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** AC1 assumes a specific alternative model of chance agreement; it is not assumption-free, only differently assumed. Failure mode: switching from kappa to AC1 *after* seeing that kappa was disappointing is an analytic degree of freedom that inflates false-positive rates (see Subcategory 5.5) — the coefficient must be pre-specified.
    - **Link to source:** [Gwet 2008](https://doi.org/10.1348/000711006X126600) · [Wongpakaran et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-13-61)

  - **Source:** Krippendorff (1970); Hayes & Krippendorff (2007); McNemar (1947)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper on estimating the reliability, systematic error, and random error of interval data. *(Krippendorff, 1970, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 30(1):61–70, DOI 10.1177/001316447003000105.)* `T2`
      - A paper responding to the call for a standard reliability measure for coding data, presenting Krippendorff's alpha and computational resources for it. *(Hayes & Krippendorff, 2007, *Communication Methods and Measures* 1(1):77–89, DOI 10.1080/19312450709336664.)* `T2`
      - A note on the sampling error of the difference between correlated proportions or percentages — the source of the test now known as McNemar's test. *(McNemar, 1947, *Psychometrika* 12(2):153–157, DOI 10.1007/BF02295996.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Krippendorff's alpha is the most general of the chance-corrected agreement coefficients: it handles nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio data, any number of raters, and missing observations, which makes it the natural choice when the comparison design is unbalanced. Separately, McNemar's test answers a question none of the agreement coefficients answer: not "do the two instruments agree?" but "do the two instruments select at systematically different rates?" — the discordant-pairs question, which is the correct test when the concern is that a new screener flags more or fewer cases than the incumbent.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** Alpha assumes the chosen difference metric (nominal, ordinal, interval) matches the data's actual level of measurement; choosing the wrong metric changes the value substantially. McNemar's test assumes paired observations and is a test of marginal homogeneity only — a non-significant McNemar result is fully compatible with severe case-level disagreement, because equal *rates* of selection say nothing about whether the *same* cases were selected. Reporting McNemar alone is a documented way to make two instruments look interchangeable when they are not.
    - **Link to source:** [Krippendorff 1970](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316447003000105) · [Hayes & Krippendorff 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/19312450709336664) · [McNemar 1947](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02295996)

- **Subcategory 5.3: Equating, calibration, concordance, and moderation — and why different constructs cannot be equated**

  - **Source:** Kolen & Brennan (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A book-length treatment titled *Test Equating, Scaling, and Linking: Methods and Practices*, third edition. *(Kolen & Brennan, 2014, Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the standard reference that defines the vocabulary distinguishing equating from the broader family of linking operations and that sets out the data-collection designs (single group, single group with counterbalancing, random/equivalent groups, and the non-equivalent groups with anchor test design) under which each linking method is defensible. Any head-to-head score-comparability claim is expected to name which of these designs was used.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** Equating in this framework is legitimate only under a set of stated requirements — most centrally that the two forms measure the same construct with similar reliability, and that the resulting conversion is symmetric and invariant across subpopulations. Failure mode: applying equating machinery when the same-construct and equal-reliability requirements do not hold produces a conversion table that is technically computable and substantively meaningless.
    - **Link to source:** [Kolen & Brennan 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7)

  - **Source:** Dorans (2004); Dorans & Holland (2000)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper that discusses "three types of score linkage — equating, concordance, and prediction of expected scores" and states that "statistical indices, in conjunction with rational considerations, are needed to determine whether the highest level of linkage attainable between scores from two 'tests' is the conceptual and statistical interchangeability sought by equating, the distributional similarity of concordance links, or the minimum squared loss attained by prediction of expected scores." *(Dorans, 2004, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 28(4):227–246, DOI 10.1177/0146621604265031.)* `T2` `[COI: author affiliated with a commercial testing organisation whose instruments are the paper's worked examples]`
      - The same paper applies this framework to relationships among ACT and SAT I scores and discusses "the importance of score reliability in assessing linkage possibilities." *(Dorans, 2004, as above.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - A paper on population invariance and the equatability of tests, developing the basic theory and the linear case. *(Dorans & Holland, 2000, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 37(4):281–306, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2000.tb01088.x.)* `T2` `[COI: authors affiliated with a commercial testing organisation]`
      - A paper on using subpopulation invariance to assess test score equity. *(Dorans, 2004, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 41(1):43–68, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2004.tb01158.x.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the authoritative articulation of the hierarchy the question turns on. Equating is the strongest claim — the two scores become interchangeable, and it is only available for tests built to the same specifications with similar reliability. Concordance is weaker — it maps the distributions onto each other so that equal percentiles correspond, without any claim that a case is indifferent between the two instruments. Prediction of expected scores is weakest and is asymmetric: predicting instrument B from instrument A is a different function from predicting A from B, which is precisely why regression-based "conversion tables" are not equating. Crucially, the paper's own framing is that *which* level is attainable is an empirical and rational determination, not a choice — and the subpopulation-invariance work supplies the diagnostic: if the conversion between two instruments differs across demographic subgroups, the link is not an equating, whatever it is called.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** Concordance assumes only distributional correspondence in the linking sample, so it is population-dependent by construction and can drift when the population changes. Failure mode, and the one this category exists to name: two instruments measuring *different constructs* can always be concorded (the arithmetic never refuses) but can never be equated, and reporting a concordance table with the language of equating — "an X on the new screener is equivalent to a Y on the incumbent" — asserts interchangeability that the design does not license.
    - **Link to source:** [Dorans 2004, Equating, Concordance, and Expectation](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621604265031) · [Dorans & Holland 2000](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2000.tb01088.x) · [Dorans 2004, Subpopulation Invariance](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2004.tb01158.x)

  - **Source:** Pommerich, Hanson, Harris & Sconing (2004); Linn (1993); National Research Council (1999)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper titled "Issues in Conducting Linkages between Distinct Tests." *(Pommerich, Hanson, Harris & Sconing, 2004, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 28(4):247–273, DOI 10.1177/0146621604265033. A variant DOI, 10.1177/0146621604264874, is in circulation and does not resolve — see gaps below.)* `T2` `[COI: authors affiliated with a commercial testing organisation]`
      - A paper titled "Linking Results of Distinct Assessments." *(Applied Measurement in Education, 1993, 6(1):83–102, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame0601_5. Crossref lists the author surname as "Lim"; the article is conventionally cited as Linn, R. L. — see gaps below.)* `T2`
      - A National Academies consensus study report titled *Uncommon Measures: Equivalence and Linkage Among Educational Tests*, produced in the context of congressional debate over the comparability of tests used to assess school performance. *(National Research Council, 1999, National Academy Press, DOI 10.17226/6332.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The question of whether two tests that were not built to be linked can nonetheless be linked has been examined at the level of a national-academy consensus study, not just in journal articles — which is a signal of how contested and consequential the practice is.   - **Source:** Kane (2013); AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A 73-page article titled "Validating the Interpretations and Uses of Test Scores." *(Kane, 2013, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 50(1):1–73, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12000.)* `T2`
      - A jointly published professional standards document, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, issued by the American Educational Research Association, the American Psychological Association, and the National Council on Measurement in Education. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Washington, DC: AERA. No DOI located — see gaps below.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The argument-based framing reframes the entire head-to-head comparison: validity is not a property of an instrument that can be established once, but a property of a specific interpretation and use, supported by an explicit chain of inferences each of which needs its own evidence. Under this framing, a statement of the form "the new screener correlates 0.85 with the incumbent" is not a validity claim at all — it is one piece of backing for one link in a longer argument, and the argument also has to cover scoring, generalisation, extrapolation to the real-world outcome, and the decision rule itself.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** The argument-based approach assumes the analyst will state the intended interpretation and use *before* gathering evidence. - **Subcategory 5.4: Incremental validity — ΔR², nested tests, relative importance, and why the inference is fragile**

  - **Source:** Hunsley & Meyer (2003); Haynes & Lench (2003)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - An article titled "The Incremental Validity of Psychological Testing and Assessment: Conceptual, Methodological, and Statistical Issues." *(Hunsley & Meyer, 2003, *Psychological Assessment* 15(4):446–455, DOI 10.1037/1040-3590.15.4.446.)* `T2`
      - A companion article in the same issue titled "Incremental Validity of New Clinical Assessment Measures." *(Haynes & Lench, 2003, *Psychological Assessment* 15(4):456–466, DOI 10.1037/1040-3590.15.4.456.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Incremental validity is the formal name for the question this whole category is built around: does the new instrument explain variance in the criterion that the incumbent does not already explain? The standard operationalisation is hierarchical regression — enter the incumbent first, add the new instrument, and test whether the increase in R² (ΔR²) is significantly greater than zero, equivalently a nested-model F test, equivalently the squared semipartial correlation of the new instrument with the criterion controlling for the incumbent.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** The hierarchical-regression operationalisation inherits every ordinary-least-squares assumption plus one that is rarely stated: that the covariate entered first is measured without error. Failure mode: entry order is a researcher choice that determines the answer, and reporting only the order that favours the new instrument is an undisclosed analytic degree of freedom.
    - **Link to source:** [Hunsley & Meyer 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/1040-3590.15.4.446) · [Haynes & Lench 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/1040-3590.15.4.456)

  - **Source:** Westfall & Yarkoni (2016); Brunner & Austin (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A paper stating that claims of incremental validity "are typically supported by measurement-level models that fail to consider the effects of measurement (un)reliability," and demonstrating "that common strategies for establishing incremental construct validity using multiple regression analysis exhibit extremely high Type I error rates under parameter regimes common in many psychological domains." *(Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016, *PLOS ONE* 11(3):e0152719, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0152719.)* `T2`
      - The same paper reports the counterintuitive result that "error rates are highest — in some cases approaching 100% — when sample sizes are large and reliability is moderate," and concludes that "a potentially large proportion of incremental validity claims made in the literature are spurious." *(Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016, as above.)* `T2`
      - The same paper reviews structural-equation-modelling-based statistical approaches that "appropriately control the Type I error rate when attempting to establish incremental validity." *(Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016, as above.)* `T2`
      - An independent paper documenting that when independent variables are measured with error, ordinary least squares can yield biased and inconsistent estimates and that this produces an inflation of the Type I error rate, with large-scale Monte Carlo results showing "unacceptably high Type I error rates under circumstances that could easily be encountered in practice." *(Brunner & Austin, 2009, *Canadian Journal of Statistics* 37(1):33–46, DOI 10.1002/cjs.10004.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the most important critique in the category and it inverts the usual intuition about statistical power. When the incumbent instrument is entered as a covariate but is measured imperfectly — as every psychological instrument is — the regression only partials out the *reliable* part of it, leaving the residual construct variance available for the new instrument to soak up. The new instrument then appears to add unique predictive power that is in fact just the incumbent's measurement error being re-explained. Because a larger sample makes this spurious signal easier to detect, more data makes the problem *worse*, not better: the false-positive rate rises toward certainty. The consequence is that a statistically significant ΔR² is, on its own, close to uninformative about whether the new instrument measures anything new. Latent-variable models that explicitly represent the incumbent's measurement error are the remedy the authors identify.
    - **Assumptions / failure mode:** The SEM remedy assumes the measurement model is correctly specified and that multiple indicators of the incumbent construct are available — often they are not, because the incumbent is delivered as a single composite score. #### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Statistical significance of an increment versus evidence of an increment.** The classical incremental-validity literature treats a significant ΔR² as the evidentiary standard for a new instrument's contribution (Hunsley & Meyer, 2003; Haynes & Lench, 2003). Westfall & Yarkoni (2016) report Type I error rates for exactly that procedure approaching 100% under conditions they describe as common, with error rates *highest* at large N and moderate reliability. These two positions cannot both be operative. Either the standard procedure is a valid test or it is a procedure that reliably manufactures the finding it is asked to look for — and the second position is the one supported by the simulation evidence and independently corroborated on the general point by Brunner & Austin (2009).

2. **Bigger samples help and bigger samples hurt.** Sample size guidance for detecting a small ΔR² (Green, 1991; Maxwell, 2000; Riley et al., 2019) points toward larger N. The measurement-error critique points out that larger N sharpens the detection of spurious increments too. Precision-based agreement planning (Bonett, 2002; Zou, 2012; Lu et al., 2016) is unaffected by this tension because it targets interval width rather than a significance test — which is an argument that agreement analyses are more robust to sample-size reasoning than incremental-validity analyses are.

3. **Chance-correction has no consensus solution.** Kappa's paradoxes are documented in the peer-reviewed literature (Feinstein & Cicchetti, 1990; Byrt et al., 1993). The two main proposed remedies — AC1 and prevalence/bias-adjusted kappa — resolve the paradox by changing the chance model, i.e. by changing the estimand. The most prominent alternative's principal advocate is also its author with a commercial interest. There is no coefficient in this family that is simultaneously paradox-free, assumption-light, and independently championed.

4. **Convenience of computation versus legitimacy of the operation.** The `equate` and `SNSequate` packages will produce an equating function for any two score distributions handed to them; nothing in the software enforces the same-construct and equal-reliability requirements that Kolen & Brennan (2014) and Dorans (2004) establish as prerequisites. The gate between "concordance" and "equating" is entirely conceptual and entirely on the analyst, and the tooling provides no friction at the exact point where the most consequential error in this category is made.

5. **Correlation with the incumbent is both the goal and the disqualifier.** A new instrument is typically validated by showing it correlates highly with the established one. But under the multitrait–multimethod logic (Campbell & Fiske, 1959), a high correlation with the incumbent is equally consistent with the new instrument merely reproducing the incumbent's method variance — in which case it adds nothing and inherits every one of the incumbent's biases. The same number is presented as the primary evidence for the new instrument and is, under a different framework, evidence against its independent value.

6. **The validation sample is produced by the thing being validated.** Range-restriction corrections (Sackett & Yang, 2000; Hunter, Schmidt & Le, 2006) and selection models (Heckman, 1979) both aim to recover unrestricted-population parameters from a selected sample. Puhani (2000) documents that the selection-model correction can be highly unstable without a credible exclusion restriction. Card & Giuliano (2016) show empirically that changing *who is assessed at all* changes who is identified. The tension: the corrections are indispensable and simultaneously among the least reliable tools in the set, and the only robust alternative — assessing an unselected sample — is the one that selection contexts structurally prevent.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

Each of these is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. Each is stated so that it could be shown to be wrong.

- **Seed A — "Agreement studies are robust where incremental-validity studies are fragile, and the field's prestige ordering is backwards."** Connecting Subcategories 5.1, 5.4, and 5.7: limits-of-agreement analysis needs no criterion, is planned for precision rather than power, produces a result on the raw score scale, and has a published reporting standard (Kottner et al., 2011). Incremental-validity analysis needs a criterion, is planned for power, produces a unitless increment, and has a documented near-100% false-positive rate under realistic measurement error (Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016). Yet incremental validity is the more prestigious claim. The starter hypothesis is that a criterion-agnostic agreement-and-disagreement characterisation may be the *stronger* evidence base, not the consolation prize — and that the discipline's preference ordering reflects the appeal of the causal-sounding claim rather than the reliability of the inference. *To interrogate:* is there a class of decisions for which agreement evidence genuinely cannot substitute, and does that class include the decision actually at stake? Does the argument survive if the incumbent's reliability is high rather than moderate?

- **Seed B — "The disagreement cases, not the agreement statistics, are where the evidence lives."** Connecting 5.1, 5.2, and 5.4: every summary coefficient in this category — ICC, concordance correlation, kappa, ΔR² — compresses the instrument-by-instrument comparison into one number and thereby discards the identity of the cases the two instruments classify differently. McNemar's test explicitly isolates the discordant pairs, and Bland–Altman explicitly plots per-case differences. The starter hypothesis is that the substantive argument for a new instrument is carried by a characterisation of *who* the two instruments disagree about and whether those cases are systematically distinguishable — which converts an inferential problem with a documented false-positive pathology into a descriptive problem with far weaker assumptions. *To interrogate:* does per-case disagreement analysis merely relocate the researcher degrees of freedom rather than remove them, since "which disagreement pattern counts as meaningful" is itself an unconstrained post-hoc choice? Can it be pre-registered tightly enough to matter?

- **Seed C — "Concordance dressed as equating is the field's most consequential undetected error, and it is undetectable from the statistics alone."** Connecting 5.3 and 5.8: the linking hierarchy (Dorans, 2004) makes interchangeability contingent on the same-construct requirement, and the multitrait–multimethod framework (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) is the design that could actually test whether two instruments measure the same construct. But an equating function is computable without any construct evidence whatsoever, and the resulting conversion table is indistinguishable in appearance from a legitimate one. The starter hypothesis is that the same-construct precondition is effectively unenforced in practice because nothing in the statistical output, the software, or the reporting conventions reveals its violation — and that a subpopulation-invariance check (Dorans & Holland, 2000) is the closest available cheap diagnostic. *To interrogate:* is invariance across subgroups actually diagnostic of same-construct measurement, or can two genuinely different constructs produce an invariant link? What would falsify the claim that this error is common rather than merely possible?

- **Seed D — "Selection makes every comparison statistic in this category a lower bound of unknown tightness."** Connecting 5.1, 5.4, and 5.6: the ICC and the concordance correlation are ratios involving between-subject variance and therefore shrink under range restriction; the instrument-instrument correlation is distorted too (Sackett et al., 2007), which propagates into every incremental-validity computation; and the corrections require an unrestricted variance that a selected sample cannot supply. The starter hypothesis is that in any already-selected validation sample, the honest deliverable is not a point estimate at all but a bounded interval computed across a stated range of plausible selection scenarios — i.e. that sensitivity analysis over the selection mechanism is the primary result rather than a robustness footnote. *To interrogate:* is the resulting interval so wide as to be useless in realistic cases, which would make the proposal rhetorically appealing but practically empty? Is there a design — a randomly assessed subsample, a regression-discontinuity around an existing cut — that escapes the problem more cheaply than modelling it?
