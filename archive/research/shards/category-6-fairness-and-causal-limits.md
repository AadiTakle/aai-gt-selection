### Category 6: Fairness methodology, and the boundary between prediction and program effect

*General idea: Fairness in testing is a set of falsifiable statistical claims — about items, about factor structure, about prediction, and about error rates — and these claims are mathematically distinct from one another, sometimes provably incompatible, and routinely conflated in practice. Separately, none of them, however well satisfied, establishes that the program a score routes people into actually caused the outcomes those people later achieve; that is a different question requiring a different design.*

- **Subcategory 6.1: Differential item functioning — the detection machinery**
  - **Source:** Holland & Thayer (1988); Swaminathan & Rogers (1990); Rogers & Swaminathan (1993); Shealy & Stout (1993); Raju (1988, 1990); Thissen, Steinberg & Wainer (1993)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The Mantel–Haenszel approach to DIF forms K two-by-two tables, one per level of the matching score, and compares the odds of a correct response for reference- and focal-group members conditional on that matching score. *(Holland & Thayer, 1988, in Wainer & Braun (eds.), *Test Validity*, Hillsdale NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum, pp. 129–145. No DOI; book chapter.)* `T2`
      - Holland and Thayer expressed the Mantel–Haenszel result on the ETS delta scale of item difficulty as MH D-DIF = −2.35 · ln(α̂_MH), where α̂_MH is the Mantel–Haenszel estimate of the constant odds ratio. *(Holland & Thayer, 1988; formula and delta-scale rationale restated in Zwick, *A Review of ETS Differential Item Functioning Assessment Procedures*, ETS Research Report, ERIC EJ1109842.)* `T3` *(tier reflects the ETS report used to verify the formula, not the original chapter)*
      - An MH D-DIF value of −1 corresponds to α̂_MH = 1.530, meaning the odds of a correct response for the reference group are more than 50% higher than for comparable members of the focal group at the same matching score. *(Zwick, ETS Research Report, ERIC EJ1109842, p. 4.)* `T3`
      - A logistic regression model for DIF regresses item response on the matching score, group, and their interaction; a group main effect indicates uniform DIF and a score-by-group interaction indicates non-uniform DIF. *(Swaminathan & Rogers, 1990, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 27(4):361–370, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00754.x.)* `T2`
      - In simulation, the logistic regression procedure was more powerful than Mantel–Haenszel for detecting non-uniform DIF and as powerful for uniform DIF. *(Swaminathan & Rogers, 1990, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 27(4):361–370, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00754.x.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up simulation found the logistic-regression DIF test statistic did *not* follow its expected distribution for very difficult and highly discriminating items. *(Rogers & Swaminathan, 1993, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 17(2):105–116, DOI 10.1177/014662169301700201.)* `T2`
      - SIBTEST is a model-based modification of the standardization index, built on a multidimensional IRT bias-modelling approach, that detects and estimates DIF for several items at once. *(Shealy & Stout, 1993, *Psychometrika* 58(2):159–194, DOI 10.1007/BF02294572.)* `T2`
      - Shealy and Stout state that SIBTEST detects bias/DIF "without the usual Type 1 error inflation due to group target ability differences," and that it performs comparably to Mantel–Haenszel in the single-item case. *(Shealy & Stout, 1993, *Psychometrika* 58(2):159–194, DOI 10.1007/BF02294572.)* `T2`
      - SIBTEST extends to differential test functioning (DTF), allowing study of whether item-level DIF *amplifies* or *cancels* at the total-score level. *(Shealy & Stout, 1993, *Psychometrika* 58(2):159–194, DOI 10.1007/BF02294572; empirical demonstration in Nandakumar, 1993, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 30(4):293–311, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1993.tb00428.x.)* `T2`
      - Raju derived formulas for the exact signed and unsigned area between two item characteristic curves, and proved that when the lower asymptote (c) parameters are unequal in the three-parameter model, the area between the two curves is *infinite*. *(Raju, 1988, *Psychometrika* 53(4):495–502, DOI 10.1007/BF02294403.)* `T2`
      - Raju subsequently derived asymptotic sampling distributions (means and variances) for the estimated signed and unsigned areas, for the Rasch and two-parameter models and for the three-parameter model with fixed lower asymptotes. *(Raju, 1990, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 14(2), DOI 10.1177/014662169001400208.)* `T2`
      - IRT likelihood-ratio DIF compares the fit of a model with item parameters constrained equal across groups against a model in which the studied item's parameters are free, using non-DIF items as anchors. *(Thissen, Steinberg & Wainer, 1993, "Detection of differential item functioning using the parameters of item response models," in Holland & Wainer (eds.), *Differential Item Functioning*, Hillsdale NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum. Chapter verified via reference lists; page range not independently confirmed.)* `T2`
      - One implemented hybrid runs ordinal logistic regression DIF using IRT theta estimates as the conditioning variable, iterating until the flagged item set is stable across two consecutive iterations, with the graded response model and a likelihood-ratio χ² at α = 0.01 as defaults. *(Choi, Gibbons & Crane, 2011, *Journal of Statistical Software* 39(8):1–30, DOI 10.18637/jss.v039.i08; defaults per the package documentation.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** DIF detection asks a narrow question — *conditional on the same level of the matched trait, do two groups respond differently to this item?* — and there are at least four families of answers. Mantel–Haenszel is a conditional odds-ratio test on the observed score, cheap and interpretable but insensitive by construction to DIF that reverses direction across the trait range. Logistic regression adds an interaction term and thereby separates uniform from non-uniform DIF, at the cost of a test statistic that misbehaves for very hard, highly discriminating items. SIBTEST replaces the observed matching score with a model-based correction, which is what buys it protection against the Type I error inflation that arises purely from group differences in true ability, and it uniquely scales up to whether item-level effects cancel or compound at the test level. IRT-based methods (likelihood-ratio tests and Raju's area measures) work in the parameter space rather than the score space, which is more principled but introduces its own pathology — Raju's area is literally infinite when guessing parameters differ. The choice among them is not cosmetic: they answer subtly different questions and disagree in identifiable circumstances.
    - **Link to source:** [Swaminathan & Rogers 1990](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00754.x) · [Shealy & Stout 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02294572) · [Raju 1988](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02294403)

- **Subcategory 6.2: Effect sizes, "item impact" versus "item bias," and the multiplicity problem**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Dorans & Holland (1992); Zwick (ETS); Jodoin & Gierl (2001); Thissen, Steinberg & Kuang (2002); Benjamini & Hochberg (1995); Shealy & Stout (1993)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* define bias as "construct underrepresentation or construct-irrelevant components of test scores that differentially affect the performance of different groups of test takers and consequently the reliability/precision and validity of interpretations and uses of their test scores." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Washington DC: AERA, p. 216, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6. Page-specific quotation verified via a technical report quoting the Standards; ISBN and chapter pagination verified in the Standards PDF itself.)* `T1`
      - Shealy and Stout explicitly propose "a distinction between DIF and bias," treating DIF as the statistical signal and bias as a substantive interpretation of it. *(Shealy & Stout, 1993, *Psychometrika* 58(2):159–194, DOI 10.1007/BF02294572.)* `T2`
      - Under the ETS classification scheme, an item is category A (negligible) if the MH χ² is not significant at the 5% level *or* |MH D-DIF| < 1.0; category C (large) if |MH D-DIF| ≥ 1.5 *and* significantly greater than 1.0 at the 5% level; and category B otherwise. *(Dorans & Holland, 1992, ETS Research Report, DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.1992.tb01440.x; also stated in Zwick, ERIC EJ1109842.)* `T3`
      - Under the ETS scheme, category C items "are to be selected only if essential to meet test specifications," and category B items may be used but should be deprioritised when equivalent alternatives exist. *(ETS classification as described in Zieky/ETS practice, restated in an ETS DIF analysis report, DOI 10.1002/j.2330-8516.1988.tb00322.x.)* `T3`
      - For the logistic-regression DIF procedure, an R²-change effect-size classification derived from SIBTEST conventions treats ΔR² < 0.035 as negligible, 0.035–0.070 as moderate, and > 0.070 as large. *(Jodoin & Gierl, 2001, *Applied Measurement in Education* 14(4):329–349, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1404_2; measure originally proposed by Zumbo & Thomas, 1996.)* `T2`
      - Adding an effect-size requirement to the logistic-regression DIF procedure substantially reduced Type I error rates at large sample sizes, but also reduced power. *(Jodoin & Gierl, 2001, *Applied Measurement in Education* 14(4):329–349, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1404_2.)* `T2`
      - The Benjamini–Hochberg step-up procedure controls the false discovery rate rather than the familywise error rate, and yields substantially greater power than Bonferroni. *(Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995, *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B* 57(1):289–300, DOI 10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x.)* `T2`
      - The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure has been adopted for reporting results from the National Assessment of Educational Progress, and can be implemented in ordinary spreadsheet software. *(Thissen, Steinberg & Kuang, 2002, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 27(1):77–83, DOI 10.3102/10769986027001077.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two conflations do most of the damage in this area. The first is treating a group difference in raw scores as evidence of item bias; the professional standard is explicit that bias is a *validity* claim about construct-irrelevant components, not an observation about means, and SIBTEST's authors were careful to name the gap between the statistic (DIF) and the interpretation (bias). The second is treating statistical significance as the flagging criterion. Because DIF is tested item by item, a 60-item instrument generates 60 tests per group contrast per grade band, and at conventional alpha a handful of items will flag by chance alone; both major traditions therefore attach an effect-size gate — ETS's delta-scale A/B/C bands, or the ΔR² bands for logistic regression — and the empirical work shows this trade is real, buying large Type I error reductions at the cost of power. Where many comparisons genuinely must be made, false-discovery-rate control is the standard-of-practice answer and is used in national assessment reporting, but it is a different guarantee from familywise control and should be named as such.
    - **Link to source:** [Jodoin & Gierl 2001](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1404_2) · [Thissen, Steinberg & Kuang 2002](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986027001077) · [Standards 2014 (free PDF)](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/9780935302356.pdf)

- **Subcategory 6.3: Measurement invariance via multi-group confirmatory factor analysis**
  - **Source:** Cheung & Rensvold (2002); Chen (2007); Putnick & Bornstein (2016); Rosseel (2012)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Measurement invariance is conventionally tested in nested steps — configural (same pattern of loadings), metric/weak (equal loadings), scalar/strong (equal loadings and intercepts), and residual/strict (additionally equal residual variances). *(Putnick & Bornstein, 2016, *Developmental Review* 41:71–90, DOI 10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004.)* `T1`
      - Putnick and Bornstein state that non-invariance means "the construct cannot be meaningfully tested or construed across groups," and that assessing invariance is essential *prior to* testing mean differences across groups or differential relations of the construct across groups. *(Putnick & Bornstein, 2016, *Developmental Review* 41:71–90, DOI 10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004.)* `T1`
      - Cheung and Rensvold examined 20 goodness-of-fit indexes based on the minimum fit function under a two-group simulation and recommended ΔCFI, ΔGamma hat, and ΔMcDonald's Noncentrality Index, because these three are independent of both model complexity and sample size and are uncorrelated with overall fit. *(Cheung & Rensvold, 2002, *Structural Equation Modeling* 9(2):233–255, DOI 10.1207/S15328007SEM0902_5.)* `T2`
      - Cheung and Rensvold's widely used criterion is that a change in CFI of ≤ |−0.01| indicates the stricter-invariance hypothesis should not be rejected. *(Cheung & Rensvold, 2002, *Structural Equation Modeling* 9(2):233–255, DOI 10.1207/S15328007SEM0902_5; criterion restated in numerous applications.)* `T2`
      - Chen's Monte Carlo studies found SRMR more sensitive to lack of invariance in factor loadings than in intercepts or residual variances, while CFI and RMSEA were approximately equally sensitive to all three. *(Chen, 2007, *Structural Equation Modeling* 14(3):464–504, DOI 10.1080/10705510701301834.)* `T2`
      - Chen found that changes in fit statistics were larger when group sample sizes were equal than when they were unequal, across all three levels of invariance. *(Chen, 2007, *Structural Equation Modeling* 14(3):464–504, DOI 10.1080/10705510701301834.)* `T2`
      - Chen's recommended cutoffs are sample-size conditional: with n > 300 per group and equal group sizes, ΔCFI ≤ −0.010 supplemented by ΔRMSEA ≥ 0.015 or ΔSRMR ≥ 0.030 (loadings) / ≥ 0.010 (intercepts or residuals); with n ≤ 300 and unequal groups, ΔCFI ≤ −0.005 supplemented by ΔRMSEA ≥ 0.010 or ΔSRMR ≥ 0.025 (loadings) / ≥ 0.005 (intercepts or residuals). *(Chen, 2007, *Structural Equation Modeling* 14(3):464–504, p. 501, DOI 10.1080/10705510701301834. Cutoff table verified via two independent secondary sources quoting p. 501, not from the paywalled original.)* `T2`
      - In Putnick and Bornstein's literature review, most invariance tests reported configural, metric, and scalar steps, with residual invariance reported for fewer; alternative fit indices were used as the criterion in the vast majority of tests; partial invariance was reported for about one-third of tests; and reporting alternative fit indices was associated with higher levels of *achieved* invariance. *(Putnick & Bornstein, 2016, *Developmental Review* 41:71–90, DOI 10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004.)* `T1`
      - Putnick and Bornstein note that the commonly cited partial-invariance guideline — that ideally more than half the items on a factor should be invariant — was proposed without supporting empirical studies. *(Putnick & Bornstein, 2016, *Developmental Review* 41:71–90, DOI 10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The invariance ladder is a licensing structure, not a checklist. Configural invariance licenses only the claim that the same items load on the same factors in both groups. Metric invariance licenses comparison of *relationships* — correlations, regression slopes — across groups. Scalar invariance is the rung that licenses comparison of *means*, which is precisely the operation a cut score performs, so a screener that has not demonstrated at least scalar invariance across the groups it will be applied to has not earned the comparison it is making. Strict invariance additionally equates error variances and is rarely achieved or required. The judgment criteria are contested in a specific, tractable way: the two most cited sources give different thresholds, Chen's are explicitly conditional on sample size and group balance in a direction that penalises the unbalanced samples typical of equity analyses, and the review evidence shows that choosing a more permissive index makes invariance easier to "achieve" — an incentive worth naming out loud, alongside the fact that the standard fallback of partial invariance rests on a rule of thumb its own reviewers describe as unsupported.
    - **Link to source:** [Cheung & Rensvold 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15328007SEM0902_5) · [Chen 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705510701301834) · [Putnick & Bornstein 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004)

- **Subcategory 6.4: Differential prediction (the Cleary model), its critics, and the invariance/prediction duality**
  - **Source:** Cleary (1968); Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce (2010); Mattern & Patterson (2013); Millsap (1995, 1997, 2007); Meredith & Millsap (1992)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The Cleary model evaluates test bias by comparing the regression of a criterion on test scores across groups; systematic over- or under-prediction of the criterion for one group's members constitutes bias. *(Cleary, 1968, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 5(2):115–124, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1968.tb00613.x.)* `T2`
      - Aguinis, Culpepper and Pierce characterise the "established conclusion" they set out to test as the view that test bias in preemployment testing is nonexistent, and that where it exists it consists only of intercept-based differences favouring minority group members. *(Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce, 2010, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 95(4):648–680, DOI 10.1037/a0018714.)* `T2`
      - Their Monte Carlo design spanned 3,185,000 unique parameter combinations — varying true intercept and slope bias, total sample size, minority-group proportion, predictor and criterion reliability, range restriction, predictor–group correlation, and predictor mean differences — yielding results from over 15.9 billion samples. *(Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce, 2010, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 95(4):648–680, DOI 10.1037/a0018714.)* `T2`
      - They developed an analytic proof, alongside the simulations, showing that methodological and statistical artifacts affect the *relative* accuracy of intercept- and slope-based bias assessment, and concluded these artifacts call the established conclusion into question. *(Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce, 2010, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 95(4):648–680, DOI 10.1037/a0018714.)* `T2`
      - Responding with data on over 475,000 students entering college between 2006 and 2008 and applying corrections for statistical artifacts, Mattern and Patterson found minimal differential prediction: ΔR² for intercept ranged from .004 to .032 and ΔR² for slope from .001 to .013, depending on corrections and comparison groups. *(Mattern & Patterson, 2013, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 98(1):134–147, DOI 10.1037/a0030610.)* `T2` `[COI: both authors were College Board research scientists; the SAT is a College Board product]`
      - In the same analysis, regression plots showed college grades were consistently *over*-predicted for Black and Hispanic students and *under*-predicted for female students. *(Mattern & Patterson, 2013, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 98(1):134–147, DOI 10.1037/a0030610.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - Millsap proved a theorem describing the conditions under which measurement invariance is consistent with predictive invariance in the linear case, and showed that "paradoxically, these two forms of invariance are shown to be inconsistent under realistic conditions." *(Millsap, 1995, *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 30(4):577–605, DOI 10.1207/s15327906mbr3004_6.)* `T2`
      - Millsap states that this duality "may force a reinterpretation of common empirical findings of test criterion regression slope invariance, and of invariance in test validities." *(Millsap, 1995, *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 30(4):577–605, DOI 10.1207/s15327906mbr3004_6.)* `T2`
      - Millsap extended the analysis to the single-factor case, and later revisited it in *Psychometrika*. *(Millsap, 1997, *Psychological Methods* 2(3):248–260, DOI 10.1037/1082-989X.2.3.248; Millsap, 2007, *Psychometrika* 72(4), DOI 10.1007/s11336-007-9039-7.)* `T2`
      - Meredith and Millsap separately argued against inferring measurement bias from manifest (observed) variables alone. *(Meredith & Millsap, 1992, *Psychometrika* 57(2):289–311, DOI 10.1007/BF02294510.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The Cleary model turns "is the test fair?" into "does the same score predict the same criterion outcome for members of both groups?" — a genuinely testable question, and the one the employment- and admissions-testing literatures have mostly asked. Two things complicate it. First, the design is fragile: the most exhaustive simulation of it to date argues that low power and criterion unreliability make slope differences hard to detect and inflate apparent intercept differences, so the field's stable empirical finding — no slope bias, small intercept bias, over-prediction for minority groups — may partly be an artifact of the method. The strongest counterargument, from a very large corrected admissions sample, is that the finding survives artifact correction; that counterargument comes from the test publisher's own researchers, which does not make it wrong but does mean an independent replication carries different weight. Second, and more fundamental: Millsap proved that measurement invariance and predictive invariance are, under realistic conditions, *mutually inconsistent*. Passing a DIF and invariance screen does not imply predictive fairness, and observing predictive invariance does not imply the test measures the same construct in both groups. Anyone reporting both should expect them to disagree and should say in advance which one their claim rests on.
    - **Link to source:** [Cleary 1968](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1968.tb00613.x) · [Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018714) · [Mattern & Patterson 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030610) · [Millsap 1995](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327906mbr3004_6)

- **Subcategory 6.5: Algorithmic fairness criteria and the impossibility results**
  - **Source:** Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2017); Chouldechova (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan formalise three fairness conditions for a risk score: calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class. *(Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan, 2017, *8th Innovations in Theoretical Computer Science Conference (ITCS 2017)*, LIPIcs vol. 67, 43:1–43:23, DOI 10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2017.43.)* `T2`
      - Balance for the positive class requires that the average score assigned to members of the positive class be the same across groups; balance for the negative class requires the same for the negative class. *(Definitions as restated in Chouldechova, 2017, *Big Data* 5(2):153–163, DOI 10.1089/big.2016.0047, §"related work," summarising Kleinberg et al.)* `T2`
      - They prove that except in highly constrained special cases, no method can satisfy all three conditions simultaneously, and that even satisfying all three *approximately* requires the data to lie in an approximate version of one of those special cases. *(Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan, 2017, LIPIcs vol. 67, 43:1–43:23, DOI 10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2017.43.)* `T2`
      - The constrained special cases are equal base rates across groups, or perfect prediction. *(Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan, 2017, LIPIcs vol. 67, 43:1–43:23, DOI 10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2017.43.)* `T2`
      - Chouldechova defines predictive parity as equality of positive predictive value across groups at a given threshold, and error rate balance as equality of false positive and false negative rates across groups. *(Chouldechova, 2017, *Big Data* 5(2):153–163, DOI 10.1089/big.2016.0047.)* `T2`
      - Chouldechova derives the identity FPR = (p / (1 − p)) · ((1 − PPV) / PPV) · (1 − FNR), where p is the base rate in the group; it follows directly that if PPV is equalised across groups but base rates differ, false positive and false negative rates cannot both be equal. *(Chouldechova, 2017, *Big Data* 5(2):153–163, eq. 2.6, DOI 10.1089/big.2016.0047.)* `T2`
      - Chouldechova enumerates exactly three tuning options under unequal base rates: allow unequal false negative rates to keep equal PPV and equal false positive rates; allow unequal false positive rates to keep equal PPV and equal false negative rates; or allow unequal PPV in order to equalise both error rates. *(Chouldechova, 2017, *Big Data* 5(2):153–163, §4, DOI 10.1089/big.2016.0047.)* `T2`
      - Well-calibrated scores can nonetheless fail predictive parity at a given threshold, because the relationship depends on the conditional distribution of the score given the outcome, which can differ across groups. *(Chouldechova, 2017, *Big Data* 5(2):153–163, DOI 10.1089/big.2016.0047.)* `T2`
      - The Fairlearn Python library's own documentation states that "there are many quantitative fairness metrics which cannot all be satisfied simultaneously," and frames its goal as enabling humans to make scenario-appropriate trade-offs. *(Weerts, Dudík, Edgar, Jalali, Lutz & Madaio, 2023, *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 24(257):1–8; quotation from the project README.)* `T2` `[COI: five of the six authors were affiliated with Microsoft or Google at publication]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** These are theorems, not opinions, and they apply to any scored screener with a threshold. If two groups have different base rates of whatever the score is predicting — and in gifted identification, measured base rates on almost any criterion differ across income, language, and racial groups — then a score cannot simultaneously be calibrated within groups and produce equal error rates across them. Chouldechova's version is the more operationally legible: fix positive predictive value across groups and the false positive and false negative rates *must* diverge; fix the error rates and predictive value must diverge. There is no engineering fix, no better model, and no additional feature that escapes this; there is only a choice about which fairness property to preserve, whose costs to accept, and whether to say so publicly. The correct move is to state the chosen criterion in advance, report the criteria that were consequently sacrificed, and defend the choice on the merits of the decision being made — over-inclusion in a low-cost enrichment program has a very different cost structure from over-inclusion in a rationed placement.
    - **Link to source:** [Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan 2017](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2017.43) · [Chouldechova 2017](https://doi.org/10.1089/big.2016.0047)

- **Subcategory 6.6: Fairness in gifted identification — who is missed, and what actually changes it**
  - **Source:** Card & Giuliano (2016, PNAS); Grissom & Redding (2016); Peters, Rambo-Hernandez, Makel, Matthews & Plucker (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In the Florida district studied, before universal screening, only 28% of gifted third graders were Black or Hispanic, against 60% of all district students; thirteen regular elementary schools had no gifted third graders in 2004 or 2005, while the gifted rate approached 10% at the thirteen schools with the lowest share of free/reduced-price-lunch students. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - State law set a 130-point IQ threshold for gifted eligibility, with a lower "Plan B" threshold — 116 in this district — for English language learners and free/reduced-price-lunch participants. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - Beginning in spring 2005, all second graders took the Naglieri Nonverbal Ability Test during regular class time, administered by their own teachers in under an hour, with scores rescaled to mean 100 and SD 15; students above the relevant threshold were referred for individual IQ testing. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - Gifted classification rose from 5.1% to 6.6% among Plan A (non-disadvantaged) students and from 1.4% to 4.3% among Plan B students, with no change in the eligibility standards themselves. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - Trend-adjusted logit estimates imply the screening program raised the odds of gifted identification by 174% for Plan B students versus 11% for Plan A students; by 74% for Black students, 118% for Hispanic students, and 12% for White students, with the Black/Hispanic combined estimate differing significantly from the White estimate (P < 0.01) while Black and Hispanic estimates did not differ from each other (P = 0.44). *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, Table 2, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - The difference-in-differences estimate against matched comparison schools in other Florida districts was 1.6 percentage points (SE 0.3). *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - Among students newly identified because of screening ("compliers"), 23% were Black and 46% Hispanic, versus 12% and 16% respectively among students who would have been identified anyway; 8% of compliers were White versus 61% of always-takers; 29% of compliers had English-speaking parents versus 74%. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, Table 3, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - Twenty percent of Plan A students qualified for gifted status on the basis of a privately purchased IQ test, versus under 1% of Plan B students; a typical private evaluation cost $500–$1,000 in 2013. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`
      - Facing a 2007 budget crisis the district cut the testing-staff overtime budget and suspended the screening program in 2010; by 2011 the gifted share of third graders had returned to its 2004–2005 level. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113.)* `T2`

Fordham Institute.)* `T3` `[COI: advocacy-oriented commentary written by the study's own authors]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The empirical case that referral-based identification systematically misses students is strong and comes from two independent designs: a within-district natural experiment where switching from parent/teacher nomination to universal screening — with no change whatsoever to the eligibility bar — nearly tripled Plan B identification, and nationally representative data showing that high-scoring Black students are less likely to be placed in gifted services, with teacher race predicting referral after conditioning on scores. First, the private-testing channel shows the identification system was already stratified by ability to pay before any screener was involved, so a screener operates inside an existing sorting process rather than replacing it. Second, and most instructive, the gains were entirely reversible: the district cut testing overtime in 2007 and suspended screening in 2010, and identification rates reverted within a year. Norm reference is the other high-leverage, low-technology lever — building-level norms move representation substantially while state norms move it essentially not at all — and it is a policy choice about the comparison population, entirely separable from which test is used.

- **Subcategory 6.8: Prediction is not program effect — designs, power, and the interpretation of nulls**
  - **Source:** Thistlethwaite & Campbell (1960); Lee & Lemieux (2010); Calonico, Cattaneo & Titiunik (2014); McCrary (2008); Cattaneo, Jansson & Ma (2020); Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983); Stuart (2010); Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014); Card & Giuliano (2016, AER); Barnett, van der Pols & Dobson (2005); Gelman & Carlin (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Regression-discontinuity analysis was introduced as "a method of testing causal hypotheses" in situations where the investigator cannot randomly assign subjects, illustrated with near-winners of merit certificates. *(Thistlethwaite & Campbell, 1960, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 51(6):309–317, DOI 10.1037/h0044319.)* `T2`
      - Regression to the mean becomes more noticeable as measurement error increases *and* when follow-up measurements are examined only on a sub-sample selected using a baseline value. *(Barnett, van der Pols & Dobson, 2005, *International Journal of Epidemiology* 34(1):215–220, DOI 10.1093/ije/dyh299.)* `T2`
      - Regression to the mean is a consequence of random error, not systematic bias, and should always be considered as a possible cause of an observed change in repeated data. *(Barnett, van der Pols & Dobson, 2005, *International Journal of Epidemiology* 34(1):215–220, DOI 10.1093/ije/dyh299.)* `T2`
      - Lee and Lemieux's methodological survey covers when RD is likely to be valid or invalid given the incentives facing agents, and the limitations of interpreting RD estimates. *(Lee & Lemieux, 2010, *Journal of Economic Literature* 48(2):281–355, DOI 10.1257/jel.48.2.281.)* `T2`
      - Standard sufficient conditions for RD identification are continuity of the conditional expectation of counterfactual outcomes in the running variable, and these may not be plausible if agents can manipulate the running variable. *(McCrary, 2008, *Journal of Econometrics* 142(2):698–714, DOI 10.1016/j.jeconom.2007.05.005.)* `T2`
      - McCrary's manipulation test estimates the discontinuity at the cutoff in the density of the running variable in two steps — a finely gridded histogram, then local linear smoothing separately on each side — and implements a Wald test of the null that the discontinuity is zero. *(McCrary, 2008, *Journal of Econometrics* 142(2):698–714, DOI 10.1016/j.jeconom.2007.05.005.)* `T2`
      - Cattaneo, Jansson and Ma introduce a boundary-adaptive local polynomial density estimator that requires no prebinning or other data transformation, and use it to construct a newer discontinuity-in-density (manipulation) test. *(Cattaneo, Jansson & Ma, 2020, *Journal of the American Statistical Association* 115(531):1449–1455, DOI 10.1080/01621459.2019.1635480.)* `T2`
      - Conventional RD confidence intervals can be seriously hampered by sensitivity to the bandwidth; available bandwidth selectors typically yield a "large" bandwidth, producing intervals that may be biased with empirical coverage well below nominal. *(Calonico, Cattaneo & Titiunik, 2014, *Econometrica* 82(6):2295–2326, DOI 10.3982/ECTA11757.)* `T2`
      - MSE-optimal bandwidths are by construction *not* valid for conventional RD confidence intervals but are valid with the robust bias-corrected approach, which in a special case amounts to running a quadratic rather than a linear local regression. *(Calonico, Cattaneo & Titiunik, 2014, *Econometrica* 82(6):2295–2326, DOI 10.3982/ECTA11757.)* `T2`
      - The propensity score is the conditional probability of treatment assignment given a vector of *observed* covariates; adjustment for this scalar is sufficient to remove bias due to all observed covariates. *(Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983, *Biometrika* 70(1):41–55, DOI 10.1093/biomet/70.1.41.)* `T2`
      - Matching methods aim to replicate a randomized experiment as closely as possible by obtaining treated and control groups with similar covariate distributions, reducing bias due to those covariates. *(Stuart, 2010, *Statistical Science* 25(1):1–21, DOI 10.1214/09-STS313.)* `T1`
      - Evaluating gifted and talented programs with a regression discontinuity at the eligibility threshold plus a randomized magnet-school lottery, Bui, Craig and Imberman found that achievement for marginal students neither improved nor worsened from gifted services in the short run, despite substantially higher peer achievement and a more advanced curriculum; lottery winners performed better only in science; and a bounding analysis could not rule out zero, though no significant negative effects were found. *(Bui, Craig & Imberman, 2014, *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy* 6(3):30–62, DOI 10.1257/pol.6.3.30.)* `T2`
      - Using a rank-based regression discontinuity design plus between-school/cohort comparisons in a large urban district, Card and Giuliano found that placement in a "gifted/high achiever" classroom raised fourth-grade reading and math scores by about 0.5 standard deviations for Black and Hispanic participants, with gains persisting through sixth grade and no evidence of spillovers on non-participants. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *American Economic Review* 106(10):2783–2816, DOI 10.1257/aer.20150484.)* `T2`
      - In that study, teacher quality and peer effects together explained only about 10% of the test-score gains for minority participants. *(Card & Giuliano, 2016, *American Economic Review* 106(10):2783–2816, DOI 10.1257/aer.20150484; mechanism decomposition reported in the NBER working paper version, DOI 10.3386/w22104.)* `T2`
      - Gelman and Carlin argue that conventional power analysis is flawed in placing statistical significance at the centre of design, and recommend two additional design quantities: the Type S (sign) error rate — the probability that a statistically significant estimate has the wrong sign — and the Type M (magnitude) error or exaggeration ratio — the expected factor by which a statistically significant estimate overstates the true effect. *(Gelman & Carlin, 2014, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 9(6):641–651, DOI 10.1177/1745691614551642.)* `T2`
      - They state that in noisy, small-sample settings, statistically significant results can often be misleading, and that meaningful Type S and exaggeration-ratio problems begin to arise as power falls. *(Gelman & Carlin, 2014, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 9(6):641–651, DOI 10.1177/1745691614551642.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A screener that forecasts later success tells you the score carries information about a trajectory; it says nothing about whether the program the score routes students into changed that trajectory, because the students who score high would have been on a different trajectory regardless. Two specific mechanisms guarantee the naive before/after comparison will look favourable even under a completely inert program: selection on the criterion, and regression to the mean, whose magnitude — as the epidemiological treatment makes explicit — grows with measurement error and with the very act of following up only on a sub-sample chosen by a baseline score. That is exactly the structure of a screening program. What can support a causal claim is the design that a cut score hands you for free: regression discontinuity at the threshold, which compares students just above and just below a bar they did not fully control. Its assumptions are checkable and should be checked — continuity of counterfactual outcomes, non-manipulation of the running variable (McCrary's density test or the newer local-polynomial version), and inference that is robust to bandwidth choice rather than dependent on it. Matching and propensity-score weighting are weaker fallbacks whose central limitation is stated in the founding paper: they remove bias due to *observed* covariates only. Randomization, where ethically available — an oversubscription lottery is the usual honest opportunity — remains strongest. Finally, the two best RD studies of gifted programming reach opposite conclusions, and the discipline of this literature is that both published their designs and both reported their nulls: an underpowered study that finds a large effect has very likely exaggerated it, and a well-bounded null is a real finding, not a failed one.

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Measurement invariance and predictive invariance are provably not the same claim — and can be mutually exclusive.** Millsap's theorem (1995, 1997, 2007) shows that under realistic conditions a test cannot generally be invariant in both senses simultaneously. Yet in practice DIF/invariance evidence and differential-prediction evidence are presented interchangeably as "the fairness evidence." They license different conclusions and can point in opposite directions. This is arguably the single most under-appreciated result in the category.

2. **The formal fairness criteria are jointly unsatisfiable whenever base rates differ, and base rates always differ.** Kleinberg et al. (2017) and Chouldechova (2017) prove this from opposite directions. There is no version of a scored screener that is simultaneously calibrated within groups and error-rate-balanced across them under unequal base rates. Every deployed screener therefore embodies an unstated choice, and the honest options are to state it or to be caught not having noticed it.

3. **Is a group difference in scores evidence of anything about the test?** SIBTEST's authors built an entire method around separating true group ability differences from item bias precisely because conditioning on an observed score contaminated by group differences inflates Type I error. The professional standards define bias as a construct-validity property, not a mean difference. But the popular and policy discourse routinely treats a representation gap as prima facie evidence of a biased instrument — and Card & Giuliano's evidence that referral, not the instrument, was the binding constraint suggests the instrument is often the wrong suspect.

4. **Does the field's stable differential-prediction finding survive scrutiny?** Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce (2010) argue that low power and criterion unreliability manufacture the canonical result (no slope bias, small intercept bias favouring minority groups). Mattern & Patterson (2013) reply with 475,000+ students and artifact corrections and reproduce it. The strongest rebuttal comes from the test publisher's own researchers, which does not settle the question either way but does mean the replication is not independent.

5. **Do nonverbal tests reduce disparities?** Naglieri & Ford (2003, 2005) say yes; Lohman (2005) and Lohman, Korb & Lakin (2008) say the supporting sample was unrepresentative, the national norms on two of three widely used nonverbal instruments are materially wrong, one has twice the measurement error of the others, and none predicts achievement well for English learners. The first position is advanced by the instrument's author. And yet the best evidence for universal screening's equity effect used a nonverbal screener — so the two literatures are not in as much conflict as they appear if the operative mechanism is universality of administration rather than nonverbality of content.

6. **Does gifted programming work?** Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014), using RD at the eligibility threshold plus a magnet lottery, find no achievement effect for marginal students and cannot rule out zero. Card & Giuliano (2016, AER), using a rank-based RD, find ~0.5 SD gains concentrated in Black and Hispanic high achievers, persisting two years. Both are credible designs. They estimate effects for different populations (students at the gifted cut score versus high achievers placed in a tracked classroom) and different treatments (gifted services versus classroom composition), which is the likeliest reconciliation — but it means "gifted programs work" has no single truth value, and the population and treatment must be named with any claim.

7. **Equity gains from screening were real and were also fully reversible within one budget cycle.** The same study that provides the strongest evidence for universal screening also documents the district cutting testing overtime in 2007, suspending screening in 2010, and identification rates reverting to baseline by 2011. Whatever produced the gain lived in operations, not in the instrument.

8. **The invariance judgment criteria are themselves contested and incentive-compatible with the wrong answer.** Cheung & Rensvold's ΔCFI ≤ |−0.01|, Chen's sample-size-conditional variants, and Meade et al.'s much stricter ΔCFI > .002 give different verdicts on the same data — and Putnick & Bornstein's review found that reporting alternative fit indices was *associated with higher levels of achieved invariance*. Pre-registering the criterion is the only real defence.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. Each connects sources across subcategories and each is falsifiable — which is the point.*

- **Seed A — "Fairness evidence" is four incompatible claims wearing one word, and a screener should be required to declare which one it is making.** Subcategories 6.1–6.5 describe four distinct, formally separable properties: item-level invariance (DIF), structural invariance (MG-CFA), predictive invariance (Cleary), and error-rate/calibration properties (Kleinberg, Chouldechova). Millsap proves two of them are generally inconsistent; Kleinberg and Chouldechova prove several of the others are jointly unsatisfiable under unequal base rates. The starter hypothesis is that the field's habitual practice of reporting whichever of these a given instrument passes, under the undifferentiated banner of "the fairness evidence," is not merely imprecise but structurally misleading — and that a defensible standard would require pre-declaring the target property and reporting the ones necessarily sacrificed. *To interrogate: is there a defensible priority ordering among the four for a low-stakes enrichment screener versus a rationed placement, or does the ordering have to be argued case by case? Does any existing professional standard already require this and go unenforced?*

- **Seed B — The binding constraint on equitable identification may sit almost entirely outside the instrument.** Card & Giuliano (6.6) show that changing nothing about the eligibility standard and only universalising administration raised Plan B identification odds by 174%; Grissom & Redding show teacher race predicts referral after conditioning on test scores; Peters et al. show norm reference (building versus national) moves representation far more than state-versus-national does; and Lohman et al. show the nonverbal instruments themselves carry substantial residual group differences and norming errors. The starter hypothesis is that instrument choice is a second-order lever relative to *who gets tested* and *whom they are compared against*, and that effort spent proving a new instrument is less biased may buy less equity than effort spent on universal administration and local norms. *To interrogate: is this an artifact of one Florida district's unusually strict IQ thresholds and unusually low pre-program identification rate, which Card & Giuliano themselves flag as a generalisability limit? Are there settings where screening is already universal and the instrument becomes the binding constraint?*

- **Seed C — A cut score is simultaneously the fairness liability and the only free causal design available, and this is not a coincidence.** Chouldechova's identity (6.5) shows the threshold is exactly where calibration and error-rate balance are forced apart under unequal base rates. Thistlethwaite & Campbell (6.8) show the same threshold generates a quasi-experiment. The starter hypothesis is that any organisation that has committed to a numeric cut score has thereby committed to *both* an unavoidable fairness trade-off *and* an obligation it usually ignores: it already has the data to estimate whether the program on the far side of the bar does anything, and choosing not to run that regression discontinuity is a choice. *To interrogate: what sample size and what density near the cutoff are actually needed for adequately powered RD in a district-scale setting — given the Type M exaggeration problem Gelman & Carlin describe, is a single district ever big enough, and does that push toward multi-site pooling or toward not making the claim at all?*

- **Seed D — Regression to the mean, not bias, may be the most common explanation for a screener that "works," and the standard evaluation design cannot distinguish them.** Barnett et al. (6.8) state that regression to the mean intensifies with measurement error and with following up only on a baseline-selected sub-sample. Lohman, Korb & Lakin (6.7) document that the standard error of measurement on one widely used nonverbal screener is twice that of its competitors. The starter hypothesis is that instruments with larger measurement error will produce *larger* apparent before/after gains in the selected group, purely mechanically — meaning that under the naive evaluation design, a *worse* screener can look like a *better* program. *To interrogate: can this be quantified for realistic reliability values and cut-score positions? If it can, is there a defensible reporting requirement — reliability-adjusted expected regression alongside any observed gain — and does anyone currently do this?*

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **Page-level quotations from the *Standards* were verified through secondary sources.** The ISBN, the chapter structure, and the chapter-opening page (Ch. 3, p. 49) were verified in the freely distributed Standards PDF; the specific quotations attributed to pp. 63, 64, and 216 were verified via a technical report quoting them. Someone relying on these should confirm against the print edition.
- **Chen's (2007) cutoff table was verified via two independent secondary sources quoting p. 501**, not from the paywalled original article. The direction and structure of the criteria are consistent across sources; the exact numerals should be confirmed at the source before being used as a pre-registered threshold.
- **Two chapter-length primary sources have no DOI and incomplete pagination verification:** Holland & Thayer (1988) in *Test Validity* (pp. 129–145 confirmed via the ETS catalogue record) and Thissen, Steinberg & Wainer (1993) in *Differential Item Functioning* (chapter confirmed via reference lists; page range not confirmed).
- **Raju (1990) volume and issue confirmed; page range not independently confirmed.**
- **No systematic review or meta-analysis of DIF findings on gifted-identification instruments specifically was located in this pass.** The DIF methodology sources here are method papers and the gifted sources are individual studies; the intersection — a synthesis of what DIF analyses actually find on the widely used ability screeners — is a genuine gap and may not exist.
- **No meta-analysis of universal screening effects across districts was located.** The evidence base for the screening effect rests substantially on one district's natural experiment, whose authors explicitly flag single-district generalisability as a limitation. Whether the effect replicates at the scale and in the settings the policy discourse assumes is unresolved by the sources here.
- **Device, input-modality, and accommodation effects were not covered with primary evidence.** The *Standards* establish the general obligation to minimise construct-irrelevant variance from "physical, or other characteristics," but no verified primary study of device or input-mode effects on a nonverbal reasoning screener was located in this pass. This is a real hole given how much screening now happens on tablets.
- **Python tooling coverage for DIF and measurement invariance could not be verified to parity with R.** `fairlearn` and `semopy` and the Python `rdrobust` were verified. No Python package with method coverage comparable to `difR` or `lordif` was located and verified; treating "there is a Python equivalent" as true would be an unsupported assumption.
- **COI screening was applied where affiliation was verifiable** (Naglieri as NNAT author; Mattern & Patterson as College Board researchers; Fairlearn maintainers' employers; Peters et al.'s think-tank commentary; semopy's self-benchmark). Affiliations for the remaining sources were not systematically audited, so absence of a COI tag should not be read as a verified absence of conflict.
- **Contested questions left contested by design:** whether nonverbal instruments reduce disparities (6.7), whether the canonical differential-prediction finding survives artifact correction (6.4), and whether gifted programming produces achievement effects (6.8). In each case both positions are cited at their strongest and neither is adjudicated here.
