### Category 2: What "validity" formally means, and what a validation argument requires

*General idea: In contemporary measurement theory, validity is not a property a test possesses but a judgement about how well evidence and theory support a specific interpretation of scores for a specific use — which means a new instrument cannot be "validated" in the abstract, only validated for a stated purpose by assembling and defending a chain of inferences. Within that broad agreement there are real, unsettled disputes: whether social consequences belong inside validity at all, whether the unified conception is coherent, and how far evidence that scores predict an outcome licenses using those scores to sort people into a program.*

---

- **Subcategory 2.1: The unified conception — validity as a judgement about inferences and actions, not about a test**
  - **Source:** Messick (1989); Messick (1995); Lane & Marion (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Messick defined validity as "an integrated evaluative judgment of the degree to which empirical evidence and theoretical rationales support the adequacy and appropriateness of inferences and actions based on test scores or other modes of assessment." *(Messick, 1989, "Validity," in R. L. Linn (Ed.), *Educational Measurement*, 3rd ed., pp. 13–103, American Council on Education/Macmillan; quoted at p. 13. Chapter pagination confirmed via the ETS publication record and multiple independent reference lists; the quotation itself is reproduced verbatim in Lane & Marion, 2025, p. 195, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004.)* `T2`
      - Messick argued the traditional split of validity into content, criterion, and construct "types" is "fragmented and incomplete," specifically because it omits both the value implications of score meaning as a basis for action and the social consequences of score use. *(Messick, 1995, *American Psychologist* 50(9):741–749, abstract, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.50.9.741.)* `T2`
      - Messick named six "distinguishable aspects of construct validity" that "function as general validity criteria or standards for all educational and psychological measurement": **content**, **substantive**, **structural**, **generalizability**, **external**, and **consequential**. *(Messick, 1995, *American Psychologist* 50(9):741–749, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.50.9.741.)* `T2`
      - The six aspects as Messick characterised them: **content** — evidence of content relevance, representativeness, and technical quality; **substantive** — theoretical rationales for observed response consistencies plus empirical evidence that respondents actually engage those processes; **structural** — the fidelity of the scoring structure to the structure of the construct domain; **generalizability** — the extent to which score properties and interpretations generalize to and across population groups, settings, and tasks, including validity generalization of test–criterion relationships; **external** — convergent and discriminant evidence from multitrait–multimethod comparisons plus evidence of criterion relevance and applied utility; **consequential** — appraisal of the value implications of score interpretation as a basis for action and of the actual and potential consequences of test use, especially regarding bias, fairness, and distributive justice. *(Messick, 1995, *American Psychologist* 50(9):741–749; identical wording appears in the ERIC full-text versions of the same address, ERIC ED380496 and ED380504.)* `T2`
      - Messick's six aspects are aspects **of construct validity**, not six separate validities; he presented construct validity as the overarching framework binding score interpretation to test use. *(Messick, 1995, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.50.9.741; characterisation corroborated in Lane & Marion, 2025, pp. 194–196, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Messick relocated validity from the instrument to the *claim*. On his account you never validate a test; you validate a specific inference drawn from scores and a specific action taken on that inference, and you do so by assembling evidence across six aspects that are facets of one underlying construct-validity judgement rather than a menu to choose from. Two of the six — substantive (are test-takers actually doing the cognitive work you think they are?) and consequential (what happens to people because of this use?) — are the ones most often skipped in practice, and they are precisely the ones Messick added to the older scheme.
    - **Link to source:** [Messick 1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.50.9.741)

  - **Source:** Borsboom, Mellenbergh & van Heerden (2004) — *dissent from the unified conception*
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Against the unified/consequential tradition, these authors proposed that "a test is valid for measuring an attribute if (a) the attribute exists and (b) variations in the attribute causally produce variation in the measurement outcomes." *(Borsboom, Mellenbergh & van Heerden, 2004, *Psychological Review* 111(4):1061–1071, abstract, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.111.4.1061.)* `T2`
      - They characterised their position as diverging from prevailing validity theory by emphasising "ontology, reference, and causality," whereas prevailing theory "focuses on epistemology, meaning, and correlation." *(Borsboom et al., 2004, *Psychological Review* 111(4):1061–1071, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.111.4.1061.)* `T2`
      - Their practical implication is that validation research "must not be directed at the relation between the measured attribute and other attributes but at the processes that convey the effect of the measured attribute on the test scores." *(Borsboom et al., 2004, *Psychological Review* 111(4):1061–1071, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.111.4.1061.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** A prominent minority position holds that the unified conception is bloated and that correlational evidence is largely beside the point. On this realist view, the question is whether the attribute exists and causally drives the score — which redirects validation effort toward response-process and mechanism evidence and away from the correlation-with-other-tests studies that dominate most published validation work. This is a live disagreement, not a settled footnote.
    - **Link to source:** [Borsboom et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.111.4.1061)

- **Subcategory 2.2: The professional standard — the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* and its five sources of validity evidence**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014); APA/NCME revision announcements (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The most recent published edition of the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* is the 2014 edition (the seventh in the lineage), jointly sponsored by the American Educational Research Association, the American Psychological Association, and the National Council on Measurement in Education; predecessor editions appeared in 1966, 1974, 1985, and 1999, preceded by the 1954 and 1955 technical recommendations. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Washington, DC: AERA, front matter; open-access text at testingstandards.net.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* define validity as "the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests," and call validity "the most fundamental consideration in developing tests and evaluating tests." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 11.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* state explicitly: "It is the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses that are evaluated, not the test itself," and "It is incorrect to use the unqualified phrase 'the validity of the test.'" *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 11.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* identify five sources of validity evidence: (a) test content, (b) response processes, (c) internal structure, (d) relations to other variables, and (e) consequences of testing. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 13.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* treat validity as a unitary concept and deliberately refuse the older nomenclature: "this edition refers to types of validity evidence, rather than distinct types of validity. To emphasize this distinction, the treatment that follows does not follow historical nomenclature (i.e., the use of the terms content validity or predictive validity)." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 14.)* `T1`
      - The five sources are not a checklist: "each type of evidence presented below is not required in all settings. Rather, support is needed for each proposition that underlies a proposed test interpretation for a specified use." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 14.)* `T1`
      - Standard 1.1 requires that "The test developer should set forth clearly how test scores are intended to be interpreted and consequently used. The population(s) for which a test is intended should be delimited clearly, and the construct or constructs that the test is intended to assess should be described clearly." Its comment adds: "No test permits interpretations that are valid for all purposes or in all situations. Each recommended interpretation for a given use requires validation." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 1.1.)* `T1`
      - Standard 1.2 requires that "A rationale should be presented for each intended interpretation of test scores for a given use, together with a summary of the evidence and theory bearing on the intended interpretation." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Standard 1.2.)* `T1`
      - A revision of the 2014 *Standards* is underway but not published: the sponsoring organisations announced a Standards Management Committee (Michael Rodriguez for AERA, Fred Oswald for APA, Kristen Huff for NCME) and, in February 2024, selected Andres De Los Reyes and Ye Tong as co-chairs of the Joint Committee charged with revising the 2014 edition. *(APA Science Directorate, "The Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing," apa.org announcement; corroborated by NCME and by an Educating All Learners summary of the Joint Committee appointment. Projected publication date not established by any source located.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The 2014 *Standards* is the authoritative professional framing and is freely available. It ratifies the unified view, replaces "types of validity" with "sources of validity evidence," and — importantly for anyone planning a study — explicitly says you do not need all five sources. What you need is evidence for each *proposition* your intended interpretation rests on. It also forbids the phrase "the validity of the test" outright. A revision is in progress with co-chairs appointed in 2024, so the 2014 edition remains the citable standard for now.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards 2014 (open access)](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)

- **Subcategory 2.3: Argument-based validation (Kane) — the structure that makes a validation study plannable**
  - **Source:** Kane (1992); Kane (2006); Kane (2013); Cook, Brydges, Ginsburg & Hatala (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Kane's argument-based approach was set out in *Psychological Bulletin* in 1992 and elaborated as the validity chapter of the fourth edition of *Educational Measurement*. *(Kane, 1992, "An argument-based approach to validity," *Psychological Bulletin* 112(3):527–535, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.112.3.527; Kane, 2006, "Validation," in R. L. Brennan (Ed.), *Educational Measurement*, 4th ed., pp. 17–64, Westport, CT: American Council on Education/Praeger. Chapter authorship and volume contents confirmed via ERIC record ED493398 and a library catalogue record; the 17–64 page range is corroborated by multiple reference lists, though at least one citation gives 18–64.)* `T2`
      - In the 2013 formulation, validation "suggests that the claims based on the test scores be outlined as an argument that specifies the inferences and supporting assumptions needed to get from test responses to score-based interpretations and uses," and validation is "an evaluation of the coherence and completeness of this interpretation/use argument and of the plausibility of its inferences and assumptions." *(Kane, 2013, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 50(1):1–73, abstract, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12000.)* `T2`
      - Kane's 2013 paper makes eight numbered general points. Among them: (1) "it is the proposed score interpretations and uses that are validated and not the test or the test scores"; (3) "more-ambitious claims require more support than less-ambitious claims"; (4) more-ambitious claims "tend to be more useful ... but they are also harder to validate"; (6) "the evaluation of score uses requires an evaluation of the consequences of the proposed uses; negative consequences can render a score use unacceptable"; (7) "the rejection of a score use does not necessarily invalidate a prior, underlying score interpretation"; and (8) "the validation of the score interpretation on which a score use is based does not validate the score use." *(Kane, 2013, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 50(1):1–73, abstract, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12000.)* `T2`
      - Kane renamed the object of validation from the "interpretive argument" (IA) to the "interpretation/use argument" (IUA) in order, in his words, "to give interpretations and uses equal billing." *(Kane, 2013, p. 2, as quoted and discussed in a *Language Testing* review of validity theory, DOI 10.1177/0265532215593312. Page attribution taken from that secondary source; not independently checked against the printed page.)* `T2`
      - A widely cited practical guide renders Kane's framework as four inferences: **Scoring** (translating an observation into one or more scores); **Generalisation** (using the score as a reflection of performance in a test setting); **Extrapolation** (using the score as a reflection of real-world performance); and **Implications** (applying the score to inform a decision or action). It concludes that "the Implications and associated decisions are the most important inferences in the validity argument," and advises that evidence "should focus on the most questionable assumptions in the chain of inference." *(Cook, Brydges, Ginsburg & Hatala, 2015, *Medical Education* 49(6):560–575, DOI 10.1111/medu.12678.)* `T2`
      - In the argument-based framing, each inference in the chain carries a **warrant** (the general licence for making that step) and a set of **assumptions**, and the validity argument supplies **backing** for or **counterevidence** against each. *(Lane & Marion, 2025, *Educational Measurement*, 5th ed., pp. 191–275, esp. the worked IUA examples, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Kane's contribution is procedural rather than definitional, and it is the reason this framework is the most useful one for planning. You write down the full chain from "someone responded to a task" to "therefore we did this to them," you name the warrant licensing each link and the assumptions each warrant depends on, and then you go collect evidence — starting with whichever assumption is weakest, not whichever study is easiest. Two of his eight points are unusually sharp: validating an *interpretation* does not validate a *use* built on it, and rejecting a use does not retroactively invalidate the interpretation. Those two are separable claims requiring separable evidence.
    - **Link to source:** [Kane 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12000) · [Cook et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.12678)

- **Subcategory 2.4: The older tripartite scheme, and why the field abandoned it as a set of separable "types"**
  - **Source:** Cronbach & Meehl (1955); Guion (1980); AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The scheme originates with the APA Committee on Psychological Tests, which between 1950 and 1954 "found it necessary to distinguish four types of validity, established by different types of research and requiring different interpretation"; the committee's "chief innovation" was the term *construct validity*. *(Cronbach & Meehl, 1955, *Psychological Bulletin* 52(4):281–302, p. 281, DOI 10.1037/h0040957.)* `T2`
      - The original four were predictive, concurrent, content, and construct validity; Cronbach and Meehl distinguished them by "a different emphasis on the criterion," noting that in predictive or concurrent validity "the criterion behavior is of concern to the tester, and he may have no concern whatsoever with the type of behavior exhibited in the test." *(Cronbach & Meehl, 1955, *Psychological Bulletin* 52(4):281–302, DOI 10.1037/h0040957.)* `T2`
      - Cronbach and Meehl introduced the **nomological network**: "the interlocking system of laws which constitute a theory," holding that "a necessary condition for a construct to be scientifically admissible is that it occur in a nomological net, at least some of whose laws involve observables," and that construct validation cannot be claimed "unless the network makes contact with observations, and exhibits explicit, public steps of inference." *(Cronbach & Meehl, 1955, *Psychological Bulletin* 52(4):281–302, DOI 10.1037/h0040957.)* `T2`
      - Guion attacked the tripartite scheme directly: "There has been an almost mystical, trinitarian concept of validity in mental testing over the last quarter century," and the professional guidelines "seem to treat them as something of a holy trinity representing three different roads to psychometric salvation." *(Guion, 1980, "On Trinitarian doctrines of validity," *Professional Psychology* 11(3):385–398, quotation at p. 386, DOI 10.1037/0735-7028.11.3.385. Quotation transcribed from an OCR copy of the article and independently corroborated with page attribution by a University of Cambridge review of validity's evolution, DOI 10.17863/cam.100522.)* `T2`
      - Guion's own resolution was not to discard the three terms but to subordinate them: "In psychometric theology, we can speak of one validity, evidenced in three ways" — while adding that "the essential unity of validity is much more closely related to the notion of construct validity than to the other two 'persons,'" and that "there is reasonable doubt whether the other two consistently serve as evidence of validity." *(Guion, 1980, *Professional Psychology* 11(3):385–398, DOI 10.1037/0735-7028.11.3.385.)* `T2`
      - The current *Standards* formally close the question of nomenclature: the five sources "do not represent distinct types of validity. Validity is a unitary concept." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 14.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The three-part vocabulary that practitioners still use daily was a committee artefact from the early 1950s, and it began collapsing almost immediately. Guion's objection — voiced a full decade before Messick's chapter — was that the three were being treated as interchangeable routes to the same destination when in fact construct validity subsumes the other two and the other two do not reliably supply validity evidence on their own. The practical consequence is that "we established criterion validity" is not a defensible stopping point; it names one kind of evidence for one proposition, and says nothing about whether the scores mean what they are claimed to mean.
    - **Link to source:** [Cronbach & Meehl 1955](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040957) · [Guion 1980](https://doi.org/10.1037/0735-7028.11.3.385)

- **Subcategory 2.5: Consequential validity — a genuine and unresolved dispute**
  - **Source:** Messick (1989/1995); Linn (1997); Shepard (1997); Popham (1997); Mehrens (1997); Messick (1998); Kane (2013); Lane & Marion (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The dispute was staged as an explicit exchange in a single 1997 issue of *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*, volume 16, issue 2: Shepard, "The Centrality of Test Use and Consequences for Test Validity," pp. 5–24; Popham, "Consequential validity: Right Concern — Wrong Concept," pp. 9–13; Mehrens, "The Consequences of Consequential Validity," pp. 16–18. *(All three DOIs verified: 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00585.x, 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00586.x, 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00588.x.)* `T2`
      - Popham's framing questions were: "Why is the 1985 view of validity in the *Standards* clear and useful? Does the issue of social consequences confuse the meaning of validity? Can we address test use consequences without making them a facet of validity?" *(Popham, 1997, *EM:IP* 16(2):9–13, abstract, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00586.x.)* `T2`
      - Arguing the opposite side, **Linn** held that "the evaluation of consequences rightly belongs in the domain of validity," and thereby called for appraisal of both intended positive and potential unintended negative consequences as fundamental to validation efforts. *(Linn, 1997, "Evaluating the Validity of Assessments: The Consequences of Use," *EM:IP* 16(2):14–16, quoted at p. 16; attribution and quotation read directly from Lane & Marion, 2025, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004; Linn DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00587.x.)* `T2` — *Attribution corrected on review: this quotation was initially recorded here as Shepard's. The source sentence reads "Linn (1997) argued that … (p. 16) … Popham (1997) and Mehrens (1997) argued in opposition," so the quoted words and the p. 16 locator are Linn's, not Shepard's. Note also that the 2025 chapter's own reference list misprints this article's volume as 21(1); the registry record gives 16(2):14–16.*
      - Shepard's separate and stronger position in the same debate is that appraising the soundness of score-based decisions is "a logical part of the evaluation of test use, which has been an accepted focus of validity for several decades." *(Shepard, 1997, "The Centrality of Test Use and Consequences for Test Validity," *EM:IP* 16(2):5–24, quoted at p. 5 in Lane & Marion, 2025, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004; Shepard DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00585.x.)* `T2`
      - Messick replied to Mehrens, Popham and others by conceding the core of the objection while rejecting its force: opponents "find it easy to argue that evidence supporting the accuracy of score inferences about a person's current status on a construct is separable and orthogonal to the consequences of test misuse ... The argument is easy because it is basically true but immaterial ... validity refers not just to the accuracy of score inferences but also to evaluation of the appropriateness, meaningfulness, and usefulness of score inferences." *(Messick, 1998, "Test Validity: A Matter of Consequence," *Social Indicators Research* 45(1–3):35–44, DOI 10.1023/A:1006964925094. Quotation reproduced from a secondary source attributing it to p. 41; the wording was **not** independently verified against the published article — treat the exact phrasing as unconfirmed.)* `T2`
      - At least one critic has argued that Messick's defence is circular, because the sole basis offered for rejecting the objection is an appeal to Messick's own definition of validity. *(Secondary commentary reproducing and analysing the Messick 1998 passage, ebrary.net summary of "Conceptual Problems with Consequential Validity." Author and original publication not identified from the page retrieved — unverified provenance.)* `T3`
      - Kane's position sits between the camps: consequences enter validity via the *use*, not via the interpretation — "the evaluation of test score uses requires an evaluation of the consequences of the proposed uses, and negative consequences can render a score use unacceptable," while "the rejection of a score use does not necessarily invalidate a prior, underlying score interpretation." *(Kane, 2013, *JEM* 50(1):1–73, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12000; the first clause quoted at p. 46 via the *Language Testing* review, DOI 10.1177/0265532215593312.)* `T2`
      - The 2014 *Standards* sidestepped the nomenclature by treating consequences as a *source of evidence* rather than adjudicating whether consequences are constitutive of validity — and the wording of the heading itself encodes the hedge. Four of the five sources are named in strict parallel ("Evidence Based on Test Content," "…Response Processes," "…Internal Structure," "…Relations to Other Variables"); the fifth breaks the pattern and is titled "**Evidence for Validity *and* Consequences of Testing**," which conjoins the two rather than declaring consequences to be validity evidence in the same sense as the others. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, §§"Sources of Validity Evidence" (p. 13) through p. 21; all five headings located in the full text and the asymmetry confirmed on review.)* `T1` — *Quotation corrected on review: this fact previously rendered the fifth source as "evidence based on consequences of testing" in quotation marks. That exact string occurs **zero** times in the 2014 *Standards*; it was a paraphrase presented as a quotation. The corrected heading strengthens rather than weakens the DOK 2 reading below.*
      - The 2025 handbook chapter records the dispute as historical fact rather than as settled: after describing Shepard's position, it states plainly, "Popham (1997) and Mehrens (1997) argued in opposition." *(Lane & Marion, 2025, *Educational Measurement*, 5th ed., pp. 191–275, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is not a resolved question dressed up as one. Everyone agrees consequences matter and should be studied; the fight is over whether studying them is *validation* or is a separate evaluative activity. Popham and Mehrens hold that folding consequences into validity blurs a psychometric distinction that practitioners need — a test can measure accurately and still be used badly, and calling the bad use "invalid" hides where the fault lies. Messick conceded the separability point was "basically true" but held it immaterial, on the grounds that his definition of validity was never limited to accuracy. Kane offers the most workable compromise: split the object in two, so that consequences bear on the *use* argument while leaving the *interpretation* argument intact. The 2014 *Standards* sidestep the metaphysics by listing consequences as a source of evidence, which is a diplomatic non-answer.
    - **Link to source:** [Linn 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00587.x) · [Popham 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00586.x) · [Shepard 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00585.x) · [Mehrens 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1997.tb00588.x) · [Messick 1998](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1006964925094)

- **Subcategory 2.6: Validity is indexed to a purpose — describing an attribute is not the same as deciding who gets selected**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Lane & Marion (2025); Peters, Matthews, McBee & McCoach (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The *Standards* state that when scores are interpreted in more than one way — the example given is "both to describe a test taker's current level of the attribute being measured and to make a prediction about a future outcome" — "each intended interpretation must be validated." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 11.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* give a direct worked statement of purpose-boundedness: "Evidence supporting the interpretation of scores on a mathematics achievement test for placing students in subsequent courses (i.e., evidence that the test interpretation is valid for its intended purpose) does not permit inferring validity for other purposes (e.g., promotion or teacher evaluation)." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 14.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* note that different uses of the *same* achievement test — placement, diploma endorsement, college admission — each imply "a somewhat different interpretation": respectively, "that a student will benefit from a particular instructional intervention, that a student has mastered a specified curriculum, or that a student is likely to be successful with college-level work." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, pp. 11–12.)* `T1`
      - The 2025 handbook chapter makes the same point with a concrete case: "validity evidence to support or refute the use of a college-readiness test as a high school state assessment should be commensurate with this proposed purpose and not rely solely on evidence supporting the test for admissions decisions." *(Lane & Marion, 2025, *Educational Measurement*, 5th ed., pp. 191–275, at p. 191, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004.)* `T2`
      - The *Standards* address gifted-and-talented placement specifically: "Test scores alone should never be used as the sole basis for including any student in special education programming, or excluding any student from such programming. Test scores should be interpreted in the context of the student's history, functioning, and needs." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 12, "Educational Testing and Assessment.")* `T1`
      - The *Standards* give a gifted-identification example of the multiple-measures principle in a comment on decisions with major impact on a student: "it may not be advisable or lawful to automatically accept students into a gifted program if their IQ is measured to be above 130 without considering additional relevant information about their performance," and conversely some students below that threshold "may be accepted based on other measures or data sources, such as a test of creativity, a portfolio of student work, or teacher recommendations." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 12, comment accompanying the standard on decisions of major impact in educational settings.)* `T1`
      - A book chapter titled "Identification for What?" argues the identification question cannot be settled independently of the service being offered. *(Peters, Matthews, McBee & McCoach, 2021, "Identification for What?", in *Beyond Gifted Education*, pp. 21–38, Routledge, DOI 10.4324/9781003233299-2. **Only the bibliographic record was verified; the chapter's argument was not read and this characterisation rests on the title alone — treat as unverified.**)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The purpose-boundedness rule is stated more bluntly in the professional standard than almost anywhere else: evidence that scores validly support one decision "does not permit inferring validity for other purposes," full stop. Describing where someone currently sits on an attribute and deciding to place them somewhere are different interpretations that require separately validated arguments — and the placement interpretation smuggles in an additional claim, namely that the student *will benefit* from the placement. The *Standards* also speak directly to gifted identification, and their guidance is against mechanical single-score cutoffs in both directions.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards 2014, chap. 1 and chap. 12](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf) · [Lane & Marion 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004)

- **Subcategory 2.7: What a validation plan actually looks like, and in what order evidence is gathered**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Benson (1998); Shepard (1993); Kane (2006/2013) via Lane & Marion (2025); Cook et al. (2015)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The *Standards* specify the starting point: "Validation logically begins with an explicit statement of the proposed interpretation of test scores, along with a rationale for the relevance of the interpretation to the proposed use," and "the proposed interpretation includes specifying the construct the test is intended to measure." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, p. 11.)* `T1`
      - The *Standards* add that the conceptual framework "is partially shaped by the ways in which test scores will be used," and that "in general, adequate support for proposed interpretations for specific uses will require multiple sources of evidence." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, pp. 11–14.)* `T1`
      - Benson set out a three-stage "strong program of construct validation": a **substantive stage** (defining the theoretical and empirical domains of the construct and operationalising it into observed variables), a **structural stage** (determining how the observed variables relate to one another and to the construct), and an **external stage** (determining whether measures of the construct relate in expected ways to measures of other constructs). She explicitly recast these as *stages* "to convey that construct validation is indeed a process." *(Benson, 1998, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 17(1):10–17, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1998.tb00616.x. Crossref records pp. 10–17; some reference lists give 10–22, likely reflecting a jump page.)* `T2`
      - Benson attributes the substantive/structural/external tripartition to Loevinger (1957). *(Benson, 1998, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1998.tb00616.x; the same attribution appears independently in Shepard, 1993, *Review of Research in Education* 19:405–450, DOI 10.2307/1167347. The Loevinger original was not consulted directly.)* `T2`
      - Shepard proposed organising validity studies around three prioritising questions: "What does the testing practice claim to do? What are the arguments for and against the intended aims of the test? and What does the test do in the system other than what it claims, for good or bad?" *(Shepard, 1993, *Review of Research in Education* 19:405–450, at p. 429, as quoted in Lane & Marion, 2025, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197654965.003.0004; article DOI 10.2307/1167347. Page attribution taken from the quoting source.)* `T2`
      - Kane advocates constructing the interpretation/use argument **during design**, not after: he "pushed for the development of the IUA in the assessment design phase. Step one is a written statement of the intended interpretation, the intended use, and the construct — Kane would have this drafted at design time, not retrofitted. Step two is the chain of inferences and the assumptions each one rests on. Step three is triage: identify the weakest assumption and attack it first, because the argument fails at its weakest link, not at its average link. Benson's substantive → structural → external ordering supplies a default progression for the score-meaning half of the argument (define the construct, then check the internal structure, then check external relations), and Shepard's three questions supply the prioritisation heuristic — including the deliberately awkward third one about what the test does in the system beyond what it claims. Notably, no source in this set endorses "run a correlation with an established instrument and declare validity."

- **Subcategory 2.8: Prediction is not benefit — the distinction that most often collapses in practice**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Kane (2013); Cronbach & Snow (1977); Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The *Standards* state the requirement directly for placement uses: "A fundamental purpose of validation is to indicate whether these specific benefits are likely to be realized. Thus, in the case of a test used in placement decisions, the validation would be informed by evidence that alternative placements, in fact, are differentially beneficial to the persons and the institution." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, chap. 1, "Validity," §"Evidence for Validity and Consequences of Testing," run-in subhead "Interpretation and uses of test scores intended by test developers," **p. 19**. Quotation transcribed verbatim from the full 2014 text and confirmed on review; page located by mapping the volume's own table of contents — "Sources of Validity Evidence" p. 13, "Integrating the Validity Evidence" p. 21 — against the running-head page breaks between them.)* `T1` — *Location corrected on review: originally recorded as falling between pp. 11 and 13, i.e. in the chapter's opening discussion. It is in fact on p. 19, inside the **consequences-of-testing** source of evidence. This placement matters: the placement-benefit requirement is not a general preamble about intended uses, it is an instance of the consequences evidence source, which links this subcategory directly to Subcategory 2.5.*
      - Kane's eighth general point is that "the validation of the score interpretation on which a score use is based does not validate the score use." *(Kane, 2013, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 50(1):1–73, abstract, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12000.)* `T2`
      - Cronbach and Snow's handbook on aptitude–treatment interactions established the logic that assigning different people to different instructional treatments is justified by an *interaction* between learner aptitude and treatment, not by a main effect showing one treatment is better on average. *(Cronbach & Snow, 1977, *Aptitudes and Instructional Methods: A Handbook for Research on Interactions*, New York: Irvington/Halsted Press, xviii + 574 pp., ISBN 0470150661, LCCN 76-005510. Bibliographic record verified via the Internet Archive catalogue entry. The ATI concept and its attribution to this volume are corroborated by later methodological literature, e.g. Preacher & Sterba on aptitude-by-treatment interactions in educational intervention research; the specific argument was not read in the primary text.)* `T2`
      - An earlier Cronbach and Snow report frames one ATI goal as the position that aptitude and personality dimensions "should be a criterion for placement rather than for rejection or selection in a program." *(Cronbach & Snow, 1969, "Individual Differences in Learning Ability as a Function of Instructional Variables," Final Report, ERIC ED029001, abstract.)* `T3`
      - Empirically, an identification threshold that successfully sorts students can coexist with no detectable program benefit at that threshold: using a regression discontinuity design at a gifted-and-talented eligibility cutoff plus a randomised magnet lottery, one study found "achievement for marginal students neither improves nor worsens from GT services in the short run," despite those students being "exposed to higher achieving peers and, in the RD sample, a more advanced curriculum." Lottery winners improved only in science. *(Bui, Craig & Imberman, 2014, *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy* 6(3):30–62, DOI 10.1257/pol.6.3.30. Verified on review against the full published article: all three quoted strings ("achievement for marginal students neither improves nor worsens from GT services in the short run," "exposed to higher achieving peers," "more advanced curriculum") occur verbatim, and the science finding reads "We also find that lottery winners only perform better in science." Volume/issue/pages confirmed from the article masthead.)* `T2`
      - The same study reports it "cannot rule out zero" under a bounding analysis, and finds no significant negative effects. *(Bui, Craig & Imberman, 2014, *AEJ: Economic Policy* 6(3):30–62, DOI 10.1257/pol.6.3.30. "Cannot rule out zero" confirmed verbatim in the full published article on review.)* `T2`
      - Identification-system design choices can dominate instrument quality: a simulation study of two-stage gifted identification found that "in nearly all conditions, identification systems that require a nomination before testing result in a large proportion of gifted students being missed," and that "under commonly implemented conditions, the nomination stage can cause the false negative rate to easily exceed 60%." *(McBee, Peters & Miller, 2016, "The Impact of the Nomination Stage on Gifted Program Identification," *Gifted Child Quarterly* 60(4):258–278, abstract, DOI 10.1177/0016986216656256.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the sharpest available line, and the professional standard draws it explicitly: for a placement use, validation is informed by evidence that the *alternative placements are differentially beneficial* — not merely that scores correlate with later outcomes. Predicting who will do well is a statement about the world as it already is; claiming a placement helps is a counterfactual claim about what happens to a person because of the placement. Cronbach and Snow supply the underlying logic (differential assignment needs an aptitude-by-treatment interaction, not a main effect), Kane supplies the argument structure (interpretation validated ≠ use validated), and the regression-discontinuity evidence supplies the existence proof: a cutoff can be doing exactly what it was designed to do while the program behind it produces no measurable short-run achievement gain for the students it just admitted. Separately, the McBee et al. result is a reminder that a screening *system* has validity properties distinct from those of any instrument inside it — a gate placed in front of a good test can dominate the system's error rate.
    - **Link to source:** [Bui, Craig & Imberman 2014](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.6.3.30) · [McBee et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216656256) · [Kane 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12000)

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Are consequences part of validity, or a separate evaluation?** Messick and Shepard say yes, consequences are constitutive. Popham and Mehrens say no — the concern is legitimate but the concept is wrong, and folding consequences into validity blurs a distinction practitioners need. Messick's published reply concedes the separability argument is "basically true" while calling it immaterial, which at least one critic reads as circular. Kane splits the difference by attaching consequences to the *use* argument rather than the *interpretation* argument. The 2014 *Standards* list consequences as a source of evidence without adjudicating the underlying question. This is unresolved, and the 2025 handbook chapter still reports it as an opposition rather than a settled matter.

2. **Is the unified conception even coherent?** Borsboom, Mellenbergh and van Heerden argue the whole post-Messick apparatus focuses on the wrong things — epistemology, meaning and correlation instead of ontology, reference and causality. If they are right, the standard menu of correlational validation studies is largely beside the point and effort should shift to response-process and mechanism evidence. This sits awkwardly beside the *Standards*, which treat "relations to other variables" as a full-fledged source of evidence.

3. **The *Standards* both loosen and tighten.** They loosen by saying not every source of evidence is required in every setting — which sounds permissive. They tighten by requiring support for *each proposition* underlying the intended interpretation, and by insisting each purpose be separately validated. Read carelessly, the first sentence licenses collecting whichever evidence is cheapest; read correctly, it forbids exactly that.

4. **Guion's residual doubt has never been retired.** Guion wrote in 1980 that there is "reasonable doubt whether the other two consistently serve as evidence of validity" — that is, whether content and criterion evidence are validity evidence at all rather than merely useful information. The field's response was to relabel them as "sources of evidence" under a unitary concept. Relabelling is not the same as answering the doubt.

5. **Kane's framework tells you to prioritise, but not how to know you are done.** "Focus on the most questionable assumptions" is sound triage and offers no stopping rule. Combined with Kane's own point that more ambitious claims require more support, this means the strength of a validation programme is judged against the ambition of the claim — a standard with no fixed threshold, which is honest but hard to operationalise or to defend to a sceptic.

6. **Consequences evidence is the hardest to get and the first to be dropped.** Every framework in this category treats intended benefit as central to a selection or placement use, and the *Standards* explicitly require evidence that alternative placements are differentially beneficial. That evidence requires a counterfactual design. Instrument-level validation work is comparatively cheap. The frameworks demand the expensive evidence and the field routinely publishes the cheap evidence.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own** — a connection across sources that looks promising but has not been argued through, tested against counterexamples, or defended. None is a finished insight.*

- **Seed A — "Concurrent validation against an established instrument may be the weakest link in the chain, not the strongest evidence."** *Starter hypothesis.* The default way to argue a new screener works is to show it correlates with an incumbent instrument. But Kane's structure says the argument fails at its weakest link, and the incumbent-correlation study speaks only to the extrapolation/external portion of the chain — while leaving scoring, response-process, and decision inferences untouched. Borsboom's position sharpens this further: a correlation with another test is not evidence that the attribute causally produces the score. If both are right, the study everyone runs first may be the one that carries the least argumentative weight. *Things that would falsify or complicate it:* the *Standards* explicitly permit supporting a prediction proposition without content evidence, so a *narrow* claim ("this score ranks students similarly to the incumbent") may be fully supportable by exactly that study. The seed may be attacking an over-claim rather than the method.

- **Seed B — "The unit that needs validating may be the selection system, not the instrument."** *Starter hypothesis.* Kane validates interpretations and uses; the *Standards* validate interpretations for specified uses; both are framed around a test. But McBee et al. show that a nomination gate placed in front of a perfectly adequate test can drive false-negative rates above 60%, meaning the system's error properties are largely determined by structure rather than instrument quality. This suggests the IUA for an identification programme should be written over the whole pipeline — gate, instrument, cutoff, decision rule — with the instrument as one inference among several. *Things that would falsify or complicate it:* Kane's framework arguably already accommodates this, since the decision inference sits at the end of the chain and can encode a multi-stage rule; the seed may be a clarification of existing theory rather than an extension of it. Worth checking whether anyone has written a published IUA at the system level.

- **Seed C — "Consequential validity's critics may be more useful to a study designer than its defenders."** *Starter hypothesis.* Popham's objection — that consequences are a real concern but the wrong concept — has an underappreciated practical payoff: it forces you to specify *which* claim a bad outcome falsifies. If the scores are accurate and the placement produces no benefit, the fault is in the theory of the program, not in the measurement, and the remedy is different. Kane's separation of interpretation from use is effectively Popham's distinction rehabilitated inside validity theory. If so, the practically strongest move is to write two arguments that can fail independently, rather than one integrated judgement in which a null program effect contaminates the measurement claim. *Things that would falsify or complicate it:* Messick's reply is that accuracy was never the whole of validity, and Shepard's counter is that separating them lets test developers disclaim responsibility for foreseeable misuse. The seed needs an answer to who owns the failure when the two arguments are separated.

- **Seed D — "The regression-discontinuity finding and the *Standards*' 'differentially beneficial' clause are the same requirement stated in two vocabularies."** *Starter hypothesis.* The measurement literature says validation of a placement use should be informed by evidence that alternative placements are differentially beneficial. The economics literature operationalises exactly that with a discontinuity at the eligibility cutoff and finds, in at least one large setting, no short-run achievement effect for marginal students. Read together, they suggest a specific claim: the professional standard for validating a selection use already implies a causal design, and the causal-inference toolkit is the natural way to satisfy it — but the two literatures rarely cite each other, so the requirement and the method have not been joined up. *Things that would falsify or complicate it:* a null at the margin does not imply a null in the interior of the eligible range, and the *Standards*' clause says validation would be "informed by" such evidence, not that it requires an experiment. The strength of the equivalence claim is the thing to test.
