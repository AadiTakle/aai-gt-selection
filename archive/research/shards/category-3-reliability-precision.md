### Category 3: Reliability and precision — methods, assumptions, and tools

*General idea: Reliability is not one number and not one method — it is a family of designs (single occasion, repeated occasions, alternate forms, multiple raters) each of which estimates a different quantity under different assumptions, and the single most-reported statistic in the field (Cronbach's alpha) is the one whose assumptions are most routinely violated and least often checked. For a screener whose job is to make a keep/refer classification at a threshold, the correct precision statistics are conditional on score level and are expressed as decision consistency and decision accuracy, not as an average reliability coefficient.*

- **Subcategory 3.1: Internal consistency — coefficient alpha, its assumptions, and its documented misuse**
  - **Source:** Cronbach (1951)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Coefficient alpha is defined as `α = [k/(k−1)] · [1 − (Σσ²ᵢ / σ²ₓ)]`, where `k` is the number of items, `σ²ᵢ` is the variance of item `i`, and `σ²ₓ` is the variance of total test scores. *(Cronbach, 1951, *Psychometrika* 16(3):297–334, DOI 10.1007/BF02310555.)* `T2`
      - Cronbach showed that alpha equals the mean of all possible split-half coefficients obtainable from different splittings of a test, and is therefore "an estimate of the correlation between two random samples of items from a universe of items like those in the test." *(Cronbach, 1951, *Psychometrika* 16(3):297–334, DOI 10.1007/BF02310555.)* `T2`
      - Cronbach himself stated that alpha is an appropriate index of equivalence and of first-factor concentration "except for very short tests," and that "tests divisible into distinct subtests should be so divided before using the formula." *(Cronbach, 1951, *Psychometrika* 16(3):297–334, DOI 10.1007/BF02310555.)* `T2`
      - Cronbach explicitly noted that alpha is not independent of test length: "α increases as the test is lengthened," and he proposed the derived mean inter-item correlation `r̄ᵢⱼ` (obtained by applying the Spearman–Brown formula in reverse) as the length-independent index of inter-item homogeneity. *(Cronbach, 1951, *Psychometrika* 16(3):297–334, DOI 10.1007/BF02310555.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Alpha originated as a computational convenience — one number replacing the arbitrary choice among split halves in an era before computers — and its author already flagged two of the limitations later treated as discoveries: it inflates with test length, and it should not be applied across a test containing distinct subtests. The often-repeated claim that alpha measures "internal consistency" traces to this paper, but Cronbach tied that language to first-factor concentration and to the separate index `r̄ᵢⱼ`, not to alpha itself.
    - **Link to source:** [Cronbach 1951](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02310555)
  - **Source:** Sijtsma (2009); Sijtsma & Pfadt (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Sijtsma argues alpha "always has a value, which cannot be equal to the test score's reliability given the interitem covariance matrix and the usual assumptions about measurement error" — i.e. alpha is a lower bound to reliability, not reliability. *(Sijtsma, 2009, *Psychometrika* 74(1):107–120, DOI 10.1007/s11336-008-9101-0.)* `T2`
      - Sijtsma's stated conclusion 4: "Alpha is not a measure of internal consistency. Neither is it a measure of the degree of unidimensionality," and he notes both very low and very high alpha values can co-occur with unidimensionality or multidimensionality. *(Sijtsma, 2009, *Psychometrika* 74(1):107–120, DOI 10.1007/s11336-008-9101-0.)* `T2`
      - Sijtsma's stated conclusion 5: statistics based on a single test administration "convey little if any information about individuals' measurement accuracy reflected by their propensity distributions." *(Sijtsma, 2009, *Psychometrika* 74(1):107–120, DOI 10.1007/s11336-008-9101-0.)* `T2`
      - Sijtsma's take-away is narrower than it is often quoted as being: "Use α as a lower bound for test-score reliability or use greater lower bounds, but do not use α for anything else." *(Sijtsma & Pfadt, 2021, *Psychometrika* 86(4):843–860, DOI 10.1007/s11336-021-09789-8.)* `T2`
      - In the 2021 follow-up, the original critique's authors state that criticism of alpha has run to "a degree that does not do justice" to its limited usefulness, and they defend a continuing role for alpha as a lower bound. *(Sijtsma & Pfadt, 2021, *Psychometrika* 86(4):843–860, DOI 10.1007/s11336-021-09789-8.)* `T2`
      - Sijtsma & Pfadt report the citation counts that make the misuse consequential: Cronbach (1951) had 51,327 Google Scholar citations and Sijtsma (2009) had 2,412, as of 17 May 2021. *(Sijtsma & Pfadt, 2021, *Psychometrika* 86(4):843–860, DOI 10.1007/s11336-021-09789-8.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The canonical critique makes two separable claims that are frequently merged. The technical claim is that alpha is a lower bound and that better lower bounds exist. The interpretive claim is that alpha carries no information about dimensionality or about any individual's measurement accuracy. Notably the same author later pushed back on the anti-alpha literature his paper launched, defending alpha's use specifically as a lower bound — so "alpha is bad" is not the field's settled position; "alpha answers a narrower question than people ask of it" is.
    - **Link to source:** [Sijtsma 2009](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-008-9101-0) · [Sijtsma & Pfadt 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-021-09789-8)
  - **Source:** McNeish (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - McNeish states that violating alpha's assumptions "yields estimates of reliability that are too small, making measures look less reliable than they actually are" in many circumstances. *(McNeish, 2018, *Psychological Methods* 23(3):412–433, DOI 10.1037/met0000144.)* `T2`
      - McNeish provides evidence that "recommendations against Cronbach's alpha have not appreciably changed how empirical studies report reliability," and documents that studies cite the methodological critiques while continuing to use alpha — sometimes citing them to rationalise an unfavourable alpha value. *(McNeish, 2018, *Psychological Methods* 23(3):412–433, DOI 10.1037/met0000144.)* `T2`
      - The alternatives McNeish evaluates are omega total, Revelle's omega total, the greatest lower bound, and Coefficient H; the paper includes a software appendix for implementing each. *(McNeish, 2018, *Psychological Methods* 23(3):412–433, DOI 10.1037/met0000144.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The gap here is sociological, not mathematical. The methodological literature has recommended replacements for decades and the recommendation has not propagated into practice; citing the critique has itself become a way of continuing the practice. The direction of alpha's bias matters for how a critique lands: under mere violation of tau-equivalence alpha *understates* reliability, so an author reporting alpha is usually being conservative — which is precisely why the practice survives.
    - **Link to source:** [McNeish 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000144)
  - **Assumptions and failure modes of alpha (stated explicitly):**
    - Alpha equals the population reliability only under **essential tau-equivalence** — item true scores differ by at most an additive constant, equivalently equal factor loadings on a single common factor — *and* **uncorrelated errors**. `T2` (Sijtsma 2009; McNeish 2018; Dunn et al. 2014, all cited in this subcategory.)
    - If tau-equivalence fails but errors remain uncorrelated, alpha is a **lower bound** and understates reliability.
    - If errors are **positively correlated** (shared method, item bundles, common stimulus passages, adjacent items on a screen), alpha can **overstate** reliability — this is the dangerous direction and is the specific case Sijtsma & Pfadt (2021) devote their follow-up to.
    - Alpha rises with test length by construction (Spearman–Brown, `ρ_kk = kρ / [1 + (k−1)ρ]`), so a high alpha on a long test is uninformative and a low alpha on a very short test is not evidence of a bad instrument.
    - Alpha is **not** a dimensionality index in either direction. Dimensionality must be established separately by factor-analytic or IRT means.
    - Alpha is sample-dependent through `σ²ₓ`: a sample with restricted true-score variance (e.g. an already-selected high-scoring group) yields a lower alpha for the same instrument. This is the same restriction-of-range mechanism that attenuates validity coefficients.

- **Subcategory 3.2: What the field now recommends instead — the omega family and the greatest lower bound**
  - **Source:** Revelle & Zinbarg (2009); McDonald (1999)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Revelle & Zinbarg's central technical claim: "contrary to the name, the glb is not the greatest lower bound of reliability but rather is systematically less than `ω_t`" (McDonald's omega total). *(Revelle & Zinbarg, 2009, *Psychometrika* 74(1):145–154, DOI 10.1007/s11336-008-9102-z.)* `T2`
      - They agree with Sijtsma that alpha is inappropriate "when considering how well a test measures one concept," but recommend `ω_t` rather than the glb. *(Revelle & Zinbarg, 2009, *Psychometrika* 74(1):145–154, DOI 10.1007/s11336-008-9102-z.)* `T2`
      - Their third argument is practical rather than statistical: "the end user needs procedures that are readily available in open source software," and they note that "techniques for estimating the glb [are] not readily available for the end user." *(Revelle & Zinbarg, 2009, *Psychometrika* 74(1):145–154, DOI 10.1007/s11336-008-9102-z.)* `T2`
      - The omega notation is genuinely ambiguous in the literature: Revelle & Zinbarg use `ω_t` for the coefficient of McDonald (1999, eq. 6.20) and note that Zinbarg, Revelle & Yovel (2005) relabelled the coefficient of McDonald (1978, eq. 9) as `ω_hierarchical` (`ω_h`). *(Revelle & Zinbarg, 2009, *Psychometrika* 74(1):145–154, DOI 10.1007/s11336-008-9102-z; Zinbarg, Revelle, Yovel & Li, 2005, *Psychometrika* 70(1):123–133, DOI 10.1007/s11336-003-0974-7.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** "Omega" is not one coefficient. `ω_h` estimates the proportion of total score variance attributable to a single **general** factor (what you want if you intend to interpret a total score as measuring one thing); `ω_t` estimates the proportion attributable to **all** common factors (what you want if you intend to correct a correlation for attenuation). Reporting "omega" without specifying which one is as uninformative as reporting alpha. A useful discipline: state the coefficient, the factor model it came from, and the estimator.
    - **Link to source:** [Revelle & Zinbarg 2009](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-008-9102-z)
  - **Source:** Ten Berge & Sočan (2004); Jackson & Agunwamba (1977)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Ten Berge & Sočan argue that reliability measures designed for congeneric tests "rely on a unidimensionality hypothesis, which can neither be confirmed nor rejected when there are only three test parts, and will invariably be rejected when there are more than three test parts," and propose Jackson & Agunwamba's greatest lower bound instead. *(Ten Berge & Sočan, 2004, *Psychometrika* 69(4):613–625, DOI 10.1007/BF02289858.)* `T2`
      - They acknowledge the glb's known defect directly: "this bound has a reputation for overestimating the population value when the sample size is small," but argue the sampling-bias problem "does not play a role when the number of test parts is small" and that unidimensionality-based reliability "is equally affected" when it does. *(Ten Berge & Sočan, 2004, *Psychometrika* 69(4):613–625, DOI 10.1007/BF02289858.)* `T2`
      - They report the counter-intuitive result that unidimensionality and reliability "act as antagonistic concepts" from at least two perspectives. *(Ten Berge & Sočan, 2004, *Psychometrika* 69(4):613–625, DOI 10.1007/BF02289858.)* `T2`
      - The glb originates with Jackson & Agunwamba's algebraic lower bounds for tests of non-homogeneous items. *(Jackson & Agunwamba, 1977, *Psychometrika* 42(4):567–578, DOI 10.1007/BF02295979.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The glb is the maximum reliability value consistent with the observed covariance matrix under any admissible split into true and error covariance — so it makes the fewest structural assumptions of any of these coefficients. Its price is an upward small-sample bias, which means the glb and omega trade one failure mode for another: omega assumes a factor model that may be wrong; the glb assumes almost nothing but is optimistic when `n` is small. Neither is universally correct, and choosing between them is a substantive decision that must be stated.
    - **Link to source:** [Ten Berge & Sočan 2004](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02289858)
  - **Source:** Dunn, Baguley & Brunsden (2014); Revelle & Condon (2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Dunn et al. characterise the field's state as a "mismatch between theory and practice" arising partly because scale users are unfamiliar with the psychometric literature on alpha "and partly because alternatives to alpha are not widely known." *(Dunn, Baguley & Brunsden, 2014, *British Journal of Psychology* 105(3):399–412, DOI 10.1111/bjop.12046.)* `T2`
      - Their contribution is explicitly implementational: a guide to computing "point and interval estimates of omega using a free, open source software environment." *(Dunn, Baguley & Brunsden, 2014, *British Journal of Psychology* 105(3):399–412, DOI 10.1111/bjop.12046.)* `T2`
      - Revelle & Condon rank single-administration split-half coefficients from worst to best as `β` (worst split half) → `α`, `λ₃` (average split half) → `λ₄` (greatest split half), and argue that "model-based estimates (`ω_h`, `ω_t`) should be reported." *(Revelle & Condon, 2019, *Psychological Assessment*, DOI 10.1037/pas0000754 — volume/pages not verified.)* `T2`
      - Revelle & Condon state that `ω_t` "is a model based estimate of the Greatest Lower Bound of the total reliability of a test as is the best split half reliability (`λ₄`)." *(Revelle & Condon, 2019, online supplement, DOI 10.1037/pas0000754.)* `T3`
      - Revelle & Condon report that for very low general-factor saturation, "the EFA based `ω_h` is positively biased and that a CFA based estimate (`ω_g`) is more accurate" — i.e. the recommended replacement has its own estimator-dependent bias. *(Revelle & Condon, 2019, online supplement, DOI 10.1037/pas0000754.)* `T3`
      - Coefficient alpha equals Guttman's `λ₃`. *(Revelle & Condon, 2019, *Psychological Assessment*, DOI 10.1037/pas0000754 — volume/pages not verified.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The current recommendation is not "use omega" but "report a model-based coefficient together with the model it assumes, plus a confidence interval." Two implementation details are load-bearing and usually omitted: which omega, and whether it was estimated by exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis — the EFA route is positively biased exactly in the low-general-factor case where the coefficient matters most.
    - **Link to source:** [Dunn et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjop.12046) · [Revelle & Condon 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000754)
  - **Formula, stated precisely:** For a unidimensional congeneric factor model with standardised general-factor loadings `λᵢ` and unique variances `θᵢᵢ`, McDonald's omega is `ω = (Σλᵢ)² / [ (Σλᵢ)² + Σθᵢᵢ ]`. Unlike alpha, this does not require `λᵢ` to be equal — that single relaxation is the entire substantive difference between alpha and omega. `T2`

- **Subcategory 3.3: Test–retest reliability and stability, and how retest/practice effects contaminate it**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The professional standard defines the test–retest logic and its embedded assumptions explicitly: the test is re-administered "after a brief period during which the examinee's standing on the variable being measured would not be expected to change," and "it is assumed that the first administration has no influence on the second administration." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, ch. 2, p. 33, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - Standard 2.19 requires that "if the test–retest or alternate-form approach is used, the interval between administrations should be indicated," and states that reporting a bare coefficient is unacceptable: "it is unacceptable to say simply, 'The reliability/precision of scores on test X is .90.'" *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.19, ch. 2 Cluster 8, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - Standard 2.12 warns that "a reliability or generalizability coefficient based on a sample of examinees spanning several grades or a broad range of ages in which average scores are steadily increasing will generally give a spuriously inflated impression of reliability/precision." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.12, ch. 2 Cluster 4, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The standard notes reliability is population-dependent: "if variability in the construct of interest in the population for which scores are being generated is substantially different from what it is in the population for which reliability/precision was evaluated," the reported figure does not transfer. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The standard's own framing exposes the design's central contradiction: the interval must be long enough that the first administration does not influence the second, and short enough that the trait has not changed. For cognitive measures these two requirements are in direct conflict, and the "no influence" assumption is empirically false (see below). The age-heterogeneity warning is a specific and commonly violated trap: pooling ages inflates the coefficient because between-age true-score variance enters the numerator.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards 2014 (full text PDF)](http://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)
  - **Source:** Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Meta-analysis of 174 samples from 122 studies, 786 test outcomes, total N = 153,185, examining retest effects on cognitive ability tests across up to four administrations. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Standardised mean change from the first to the second administration was **0.327**, 95% CI [0.278, 0.375], p < 0.001. *(Scharfen et al., 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, Table 2, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Cumulative change from the first to the third administration was **0.495**, 95% CI [0.406, 0.585]; first to fourth was **0.516**, 95% CI [0.401, 0.631]. *(Scharfen et al., 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, Table 2, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - The incremental gain from the second to the third administration was **0.169**, smaller than the first-to-second gain; the increment from the third to the fourth administration was **0.021**, 95% CI [−0.020, 0.062], p = 0.318 — not statistically significant. *(Scharfen et al., 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, Table 2, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - The authors conclude there are "significant retest effects and no further score gains after the third test administration." *(Scharfen et al., 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Significant moderators of retest-effect size were cognitive ability operation, test content, **equivalence of test forms**, retest interval, and participant age. *(Scharfen et al., 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Heterogeneity was high: the reported standard deviation of the true effect (`τ`) for the first-to-second comparison was 0.317, so the 95% prediction interval around a mean effect of 0.33 spans well below zero and well above 0.9. *(Scharfen et al., 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, Table 2, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Subgroup results by content for the first-to-second administration: figural **0.321** [0.254, 0.388], numerical **0.175** [0.101, 0.249], verbal **0.331** [0.253, 0.410], and "S" content **0.406** [0.329, 0.483]. *(Scharfen et al., 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, Table 9, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Repeated cognitive testing produces a score gain of roughly a third of a standard deviation on the first retest, about half a standard deviation cumulatively by the third administration, and then it flattens. Two consequences follow. First, any before/after comparison on a cognitive measure that lacks a control condition is confounded by an effect of this documented magnitude. Second, the heterogeneity is so large that the pooled mean is a poor prediction for any specific instrument — the moderators (content type, form equivalence, interval) matter more than the average.
    - **Link to source:** [Scharfen et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003)
  - **Source:** Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007); Van Iddekinge & Arnold (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Meta-analysis of 50 studies, 107 samples, 134,436 participants on practice effects for cognitive ability tests in selection settings; sample-weighted mean `d` = **0.24**, and **0.26** after correction for sampling error. *(Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard, 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(2):373–385, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373.)* `T1`
      - Effects were larger when practice was accompanied by test coaching and when identical forms were used across administrations. *(Hausknecht et al., 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(2):373–385, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373.)* `T1`
      - The authors report that "as many as 25% to 50% of applicants in organizational and educational settings are retested with measures of cognitive ability." *(Hausknecht et al., 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(2):373–385, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373.)* `T1`
      - Summarising the same meta-analysis, a later systematic review gives the moderator breakdown: corrected `d` = **0.70** with coaching versus **0.24** without; **0.46** for identical forms versus **0.24** for alternate forms; and score improvements larger at shorter intervals, with a median inter-test span of only 20 days. *(Van Iddekinge & Arnold, 2017, *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 4:445–471, DOI 10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349.)* `T1`
      - The same review notes that scores improving across *non-identical* forms "suggests that improvements are not a simple function of remembering test content." *(Van Iddekinge & Arnold, 2017, *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 4:445–471, DOI 10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349.)* `T1`
      - The review reports that retest effects on knowledge-based tests are substantially larger than on cognitive ability tests, citing individual studies with `d` from 0.27 to 0.93. *(Van Iddekinge & Arnold, 2017, *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 4:445–471, DOI 10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Alternate forms roughly halve the retest gain relative to identical forms but do not eliminate it — so a rotating item pool mitigates rather than solves the problem, and the residual gain (`d` ≈ 0.24) is still large enough to move examinees across a threshold. The fact that gains survive form changes rules out pure item memorisation and points at transferable test-taking familiarity, which is precisely the kind of skill that differs systematically by prior testing exposure.
    - **Link to source:** [Hausknecht et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373) · [Van Iddekinge & Arnold 2017](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349)
  - **Source:** te Nijenhuis, van Vianen & van der Flier (2007)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Meta-analysis of 64 test–retest studies using IQ batteries, total N = 26,990, reporting a correlation between item/subtest `g` loadings and score gains of **−1.00**, which the authors interpret as "there is no `g` saturation in score gains." *(te Nijenhuis, van Vianen & van der Flier, 2007, *Intelligence* 35(3):283–300, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2006.07.006.)* `T1`
      - In an accompanying learning-potential study, the correlation between score gains and `g` loadedness of item scores was **−0.39**; the `g` loadedness of item scores decreased after a mediated intervention training; and low-`g` participants increased their scores more than high-`g` participants. *(te Nijenhuis et al., 2007, *Intelligence* 35(3):283–300, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2006.07.006.)* `T2`
      - The authors conclude that because gains are not related to `g`, "the generalizable `g` component decreases," explaining why gains "did not generalize to scores on other cognitive tests and to `g`-loaded external criteria." *(te Nijenhuis et al., 2007, *Intelligence* 35(3):283–300, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2006.07.006.)* `T2`
      - **Discrepancy flagged:** a later systematic review reports this correlation as **−.95** rather than −1.00. The primary paper's abstract states −1.00. *(Van Iddekinge & Arnold, 2017, *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 4:445–471, DOI 10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349, versus te Nijenhuis et al., 2007, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2006.07.006.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the strongest available statement that retest gains are not gains in the construct. The retested score is a different mixture — more test-specific skill, less general ability — which means retest gains simultaneously raise scores and degrade the score's predictive content. A correlation of exactly −1.00 in a meta-analysis of 64 studies should be treated with caution (it implies zero residual variance, which is implausible and suggests heavy correction/artefact adjustment), and the review's −.95 may reflect a differently corrected figure; the qualitative conclusion is robust across both.
    - **Link to source:** [te Nijenhuis et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2006.07.006)
  - **Source:** Calamia, Markon & Tranel (2012)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Meta-analyses of "nearly 1600 individual effect sizes" representing mean-level performance change on tests used to assess core neuropsychological domains. *(Calamia, Markon & Tranel, 2012, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist* 26(4):543–570, DOI 10.1080/13854046.2012.680913.)* `T1`
      - Practice effects are defined as "score increases due to factors such as memory for specific test items, learned strategies, or test sophistication." *(Calamia et al., 2012, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist* 26(4):543–570, DOI 10.1080/13854046.2012.680913.)* `T1`
      - Moderators associated with the magnitude of change in many cases: use of alternate forms, participant age, clinical diagnosis, and length of the test–retest interval. *(Calamia et al., 2012, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist* 26(4):543–570, DOI 10.1080/13854046.2012.680913.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Independently of the selection-testing literature, the clinical literature reaches the same conclusion from a different evidence base and with the same moderators. Note that domain-specific magnitudes are in the article body, which is paywalled — the abstract-level facts above are what could be verified without access, and any domain-specific number should be read from the article itself before use. `[COI: this literature draws heavily on commercially published test batteries; magnitudes reported in publisher manuals are not independent of the publisher.]`
    - **Link to source:** [Calamia et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/13854046.2012.680913)
  - **Assumptions and failure modes of test–retest estimates:**
    - Requires: no true change over the interval, and independence of the two administrations. **Both are violated for cognitive measures** — the second is violated by an effect of `d` ≈ 0.24–0.33.
    - A test–retest correlation is not decomposable into "stability" and "precision" from two occasions alone. Revelle & Condon distinguish short-delay retest (**dependability**, largely precision) from long-delay retest (**stability**, precision plus true change) and note that three or more occasions with contrasting delays are needed to separate specific, state, and trait variance. `T2`
    - A retest correlation can be high while the retest **mean** shifts substantially: rank order can be preserved while every score inflates. A screener evaluated by correlation alone can therefore look stable while systematically misclassifying repeat testers at a fixed threshold.
    - Interval length is a free parameter that materially changes the answer, which is why Standard 2.19 requires it to be reported.

- **Subcategory 3.4: Alternate and parallel forms — and why a rotating item pool changes the question**
  - **Source:** Cronbach (1951); AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Kolen & Brennan (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Before 1951 the field distinguished **coefficients of stability** (same test, two time points), **coefficients of equivalence** (different item sets intended to measure the same attribute), **coefficients of equivalence and stability** (different item sets separated by a time interval), and a **coefficient of precision** (same test twice in one session — characterised as hypothetical and not realisable in practice). Cronbach's 1951 article concerns coefficients of equivalence. *(Retrospective commentary on Cronbach 1951 hosted at cda.psych.uiuc.edu; authorship of the commentary not verified — treat the taxonomy as standard psychometric history rather than as a citable primary claim.)* `T3`
      - The professional standard states that "for most testing programs, scores are expected to generalize over alternate forms of the test, occasions (within some period), testing contexts, and raters (if judgment is required in scoring)," and that overall reliability given all these sources "can be estimated through a test–retest study involving different forms administered on different occasions." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - Test equating exists specifically so that "scores from multiple test forms can be used interchangeably"; the standard reference treats equating, scaling, and linking as three distinct operations, and devotes a dedicated chapter to standard errors of equating. *(Kolen & Brennan, 2014, *Test Equating, Scaling, and Linking: Methods and Practices*, 3rd ed., Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7; ch. 7 "Standard Errors of Equating", DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7_7.)* `T2`
      - A desirable property of an equating relationship is that "test forms are built to the same content and statistical specifications, so that they measure the same underlying trait or construct." *(Kolen & Brennan, cited in He, *Maintaining standards in on-demand testing using item response theory*, Ofqual report.)* `T3`
      - Alternate-form use was a significant moderator of retest effects in both major meta-analyses: gains were roughly halved but not removed (corrected `d` 0.46 identical versus 0.24 alternate). *(Hausknecht et al., 2007, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373, as summarised in Van Iddekinge & Arnold, 2017, DOI 10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113349; Scharfen et al., 2018, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Any instrument that draws items from a rotating pool is implicitly claiming alternate-form equivalence, and that claim is an empirical obligation, not a design assumption. Two distinct things must be shown: that the forms measure the same construct (same content and statistical specifications), and that scores are on a common scale (equating, with its own standard error that propagates into every threshold decision). Crucially, an alternate-forms coefficient is the *right* reliability coefficient for a rotating-pool instrument — a single-administration internal-consistency coefficient does not capture form-to-form variation at all, and will therefore overstate the precision of a score that could have come from any of many forms.
    - **Link to source:** [Kolen & Brennan 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7)
  - **Assumptions and failure modes:**
    - **Classically parallel forms** require equal means, equal variances, and equal correlations with any external variable. **Randomly parallel forms** (the G-theory framing) require only that forms be sampled from a common universe — and under this weaker and more realistic assumption, forms differ in difficulty, so form-to-form mean variation becomes a genuine component of error. `T2` (Brennan, 2001; see Subcategory 3.5.)
    - Equating error is an additional, usually unreported, source of uncertainty at a cut score. A "same" score on two forms is a linked estimate with its own standard error.
    - Item-pool rotation interacts with exposure: items seen by more examinees drift, and drift breaks the equating that the rotation was meant to enable.
    - An alternate-forms coefficient confounds form equivalence with occasion effects unless the two forms are administered in the same session — and same-session administration reintroduces fatigue and carry-over.

- **Subcategory 3.5: Generalizability theory — variance components and D-studies answer design questions a single coefficient cannot**
  - **Source:** Cronbach, Gleser, Nanda & Rajaratnam (1972); Brennan (2001)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Generalizability theory was defined and systematised in a 1972 monograph that "incorporated, systematized, and extended" the authors' prior research and "liberalizes classical test theory, in part through the application of analysis of variance procedures that focus on variance components." *(Cronbach, Gleser, Nanda & Rajaratnam, 1972, *The Dependability of Behavioral Measurements: Theory of Generalizability for Scores and Profiles*, Wiley, ISBN 0-471-18850-6; description from Brennan, 2001, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4757-3456-0.)* `T2`
      - The theory's precursor is a 1963 article: Cronbach, Rajaratnam & Gleser, "Theory of generalizability: a liberalization of reliability theory," *British Journal of Statistical Psychology* 16:137–163, DOI 10.1111/j.2044-8317.1963.tb00206.x. *(As cited in secondary sources; DOI not fetched.)* `T2`
      - The 1972 authors themselves described their book as "complexly organized and by no means simple to follow" (p. 3). *(Quoted in Brennan, 2001, *Generalizability Theory*, Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4757-3456-0.)* `T2`
      - Brennan (2001) is the standard modern extended treatment, published in Springer's *Statistics for Social and Behavioral Sciences* series, ISBN 978-0-387-95282-6. *(Brennan, 2001, *Generalizability Theory*, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4757-3456-0.)* `T2`
      - A **G study** "involves the collection of a sample of data from a universe of admissible observations that consists of facets defined by an investigator," and its principal result is "a set of estimated random effects variance components." *(Brennan, *Coefficients and Indices in G Theory*, CASMA Research Report No. 1, University of Iowa.)* `T3`
      - A **D study** "provides estimates of universe score variance, error variances, certain indexes that are like reliability coefficients, and other statistics for a measurement procedure associated with an investigator-specified universe of generalization." *(Brennan, *Coefficients and Indices in G Theory*, CASMA Research Report No. 1, University of Iowa.)* `T3`
      - In G theory, **absolute** error variance for a person × item design is `σ²(Δ) = σ²(I) + σ²(pI) = σ²(i)/nᵢ + σ²(pi)/nᵢ`, and **relative** error variance is `σ²(δ) = σ²(pI) = σ²(pi)/nᵢ`. Hence `σ²(Δ) = σ²(δ) + σ²(I)`, so absolute error is always at least as large as relative error unless `σ²(I)` is zero. *(Brennan, *Coefficients and Indices in G Theory*, CASMA Research Report No. 1, Equations 20 and 23, University of Iowa.)* `T3`
      - The **generalizability coefficient** is `Eρ² = σ²(τ) / [σ²(τ) + σ²(δ)]`, "the analogue of a reliability coefficient in classical test theory," appropriate for norm-referenced interpretations. *(Brennan, CASMA Research Report No. 1, Equation 1.)* `T3`
      - The **index of dependability** is `Φ = σ²(p) / [σ²(p) + σ²(Δ)]`, defined by Brennan & Kane (1977a,b), appropriate for domain-referenced interpretations. *(Brennan, CASMA Research Report No. 1, Equation 26.)* `T3`
      - For a **cut score** `λ`, the corresponding index is `Φ(λ) = [σ²(p) + (μ − λ)²] / [σ²(p) + (μ − λ)² + σ²(Δ)]`, derived from the domain-referenced agreement function `d(S_pI, S_qJ) = (S_pI − λ)(S_qJ − λ)`. *(Brennan, CASMA Research Report No. 1, Equations 32–33; notation of Brennan & Kane 1977a,b.)* `T3`
      - Under a threshold loss function, the agreement coefficient `θ` "is simply the proportion of consistent decisions," and the chance-corrected version `θ_c` "is Cohen's (1960) coefficient kappa" — connecting G theory directly to the decision-consistency indices of Subcategory 3.7. *(Brennan, CASMA Research Report No. 1, Equations 30–31; Cohen, 1960, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 20(1):37–46, DOI 10.1177/001316446002000104.)* `T3`
      - When `λ = X̄`, `Φ(λ)` equals KR-21, "indicative of the fact that KR–21 involves absolute error variance, not the relative error variance of classical theory." *(Brennan, CASMA Research Report No. 1, footnote; Kuder & Richardson, 1937.)* `T3`
      - Fixing a facet narrows the universe of generalization and mechanically increases both `Eρ²` and `Φ`; Brennan's worked illustration gives `Eρ̂² = .88` and `Φ = .86` after fixing a facet. *(Brennan, CASMA Research Report No. 1.)* `T3`
      - Brennan's own caution: these coefficients "can be very useful if they are interpreted correctly, but almost always they need to be buttressed with additional information — especially information about error variances." *(Brennan, CASMA Research Report No. 1.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** G theory replaces the single undifferentiated error term of classical theory with separately estimated variance components for each facet of the measurement design (items, occasions, raters, and their interactions). The practical payoff is the D study: once the components are estimated, one can compute what precision would result from any hypothetical design — how many items, how many occasions, how many raters — and so *plan* an instrument to a precision target rather than measure one after the fact and hope. Two distinctions carry most of the weight. First, relative versus absolute error: if a decision compares a person to a fixed threshold rather than to other people, the correct error term is absolute error `σ²(Δ)`, which includes item-difficulty variance and is always larger. Second, `Φ(λ)`: because the numerator contains `(μ − λ)²`, dependability is highest when the cut score sits far from the mean and lowest when the cut sits near the mean — the mathematically explicit statement that a threshold placed in the densest part of the distribution is the hardest place to make a reliable decision.
    - **Link to source:** [Brennan 2001](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4757-3456-0) · [Brennan, CASMA Report No. 1](https://education.uiowa.edu/sites/education.uiowa.edu/files/2026-04/casma-research-report-1-archived.pdf)
  - **Assumptions and failure modes:**
    - Variance components are estimated by ANOVA-type or ML methods and can come out **negative** in small samples; how negative estimates are handled (set to zero, or retained) changes downstream coefficients and must be reported.
    - Univariate G theory typically uses **random-effects** models; treating a facet as fixed narrows the universe of generalization and inflates the coefficient. Whether a facet is fixed or random is a substantive claim about what the score should generalize to, not a modelling convenience.
    - D-study projections assume the estimated components transfer to the projected design — i.e. that adding items adds items *like* the ones sampled. Adding easier or more homogeneous items violates this.
    - Unbalanced designs require different estimation machinery than balanced ones (see the software note in Subcategory 3.9).
    - G theory quantifies generalization across facets; it says nothing about whether the universe of admissible observations is the right universe. That is a validity question, not a reliability question.

- **Subcategory 3.6: Conditional standard error of measurement and IRT information — why average reliability is the wrong statistic at a cut score**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Standard 2.14**, verbatim: "When possible and appropriate, conditional standard errors of measurement should be reported at several score levels unless there is evidence that the standard error is constant across score levels. Where cut scores are specified for selection or classification, the standard errors of measurement should be reported in the vicinity of each cut score." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.14, ch. 2 Cluster 5, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The comment to Standard 2.14 adds two requirements: "If it is assumed that the standard error is constant over a broad range of score levels, the rationale for this assumption should be presented," and "the model on which the computation of the conditional standard errors is based should be specified." It also states that estimating conditional standard errors "is usually feasible with the sample sizes that are used for analyses of reliability/precision." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.14 comment, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - **Standard 2.13** requires the SEM, "both overall and conditional (if reported)," to be "provided in units of each reported score." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.13, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - **Standard 2.15** requires investigation when "conditional standard errors of measurement or test information functions will differ substantially for various subgroups." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.15, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The standard states the rationale directly: "Conditional standard errors of measurement can be much more informative than a single average standard error for a population. If decisions are based on test scores and these decisions are concentrated in one area or a few areas of the score scale, then the conditional errors in those areas are of special interest." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, p. 39, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The standard also warns that conditional SEMs are scale-dependent: "for the raw-score scale, the conditional standard error may appear to be high at one score level and low at another, but when the conditional standard errors are restated in units of scale scores, quite different trends in comparative precision may emerge." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, p. 39, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The standard notes that conditional standard errors "are difficult to estimate directly, but can be estimated indirectly. For example, the test information functions based on IRT models can be used to estimate standard errors for different values of a latent ability parameter and/or for different observed scores," with the caveat that "it is important that the model assumptions be consistent with the data." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, p. 36, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The overarching Standard 2.0 lists the acceptable forms of precision evidence as "reliability or generalizability coefficient, information function, conditional standard error, index of decision consistency," and states that "a higher degree of reliability/precision is required for score uses that have more significant consequences for test takers." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.0, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The professional standard does not merely permit conditional reporting — it requires the standard error in the vicinity of each cut score whenever a cut score exists, and requires an explicit rationale if constancy is assumed instead. It also anticipates the standard evasions: reporting in the wrong score metric, and citing a model-based estimate without checking the model. For a screener with a threshold, "reliability = .90" does not satisfy this standard; the SEM at the threshold does.
    - **Link to source:** [Standards 2014 (full text PDF)](http://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)
  - **Source:** Feldt, Steffen & Gupta (1985); Lord (1955); Keats (1957)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Comparing five methods of estimating conditional standard errors, all five "yielded a maximum value close to the middle of the score scale, with a sharp decline occurring near the extremes of the scale," and the authors judge that these trends "probably characterize the raw score standard error of most standardized achievement and ability tests." *(Feldt, Steffen & Gupta, 1985, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 9(4):351–361, DOI 10.1177/014662168500900402.)* `T2`
      - The authors explicitly caution that this shape is not universal: "Other types of tests, constructed using alternative principles, might well exhibit different trends." *(Feldt, Steffen & Gupta, 1985, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 9(4):351–361, DOI 10.1177/014662168500900402.)* `T2`
      - Two methods were recommended: polynomial smoothing of point estimates (attributed to Thorndike, 1951) and Keats's (1957) modification of the error variance derived under Lord's (1955) binomial error model. *(Feldt, Steffen & Gupta, 1985, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 9(4):351–361, DOI 10.1177/014662168500900402.)* `T2`
      - Under Lord's binomial error model, "a universe of acceptable test items from which a randomly selected set of size `k` constitutes a test form"; the resulting conditional error standard deviation for number-correct score `x` on `n` items is `σ_e(x) = √[ x(n − x) / (n − 1) ]`. *(Feldt, Steffen & Gupta, 1985, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 9(4):351–361, DOI 10.1177/014662168500900402; original model in Lord, 1955, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 15:325–336; modification in Keats, 1957, *Psychometrika* 22:29–41.)* `T2`
      - The authors note there are "at least six ways of approximating SE at specific levels," and that many test authors and users are familiar only with the single overall SEM formula. *(Feldt, Steffen & Gupta, 1985, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 9(4):351–361, DOI 10.1177/014662168500900402.)* `T2`
      - A later comparison using test–retest and parallel-forms criteria found that "for test–retest conditions, the estimation techniques are interchangeable," but "for parallel forms conditions, the procedures resulted in estimates that were meaningfully different." *("A Comparison of Score Level Estimates of the Standard Error of Measurement," 1992, *Journal of Educational Measurement*, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1992.tb00374.x — authorship not verified; cited by title and DOI only.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Conditional precision is not a refinement; it is a qualitatively different picture. For a typical number-correct test, measurement error is *largest in the middle of the score distribution* and smallest at the extremes — which is exactly the inverse of what one would want if the decision threshold is placed near the middle. The binomial formula makes the mechanism transparent: `x(n−x)` is maximised at `x = n/2`. Note also that the choice among CSEM estimators is consequential specifically under parallel forms, which is the design a rotating item pool implies.
    - **Link to source:** [Feldt, Steffen & Gupta 1985](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662168500900402)
  - **Source:** Lord (1980); IRT information machinery
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The test information function for a unidimensional IRT model is the sum of the item information functions over all items in the test, and "the standard error (standard deviation) of a person ability measure is inversely proportional to the test information." *(He, *Maintaining standards in on-demand testing using item response theory*, Ofqual report, citing Embretson & Reise 2000 and Lord 1980.)* `T3`
      - The foundational treatment is Lord (1980), *Applications of Item Response Theory to Practical Testing Problems*, Erlbaum, Hillsdale NJ; reissued with DOI 10.4324/9780203056615. *(Lord, 1980; DOI from the Kolen & Brennan 2014 reference list, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7_1.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** IRT reframes precision as a function of the latent trait level rather than a single scalar. Because information is additive over items, a test can be deliberately assembled to concentrate information at a chosen point on the scale — which is the natural formal expression of "make this screener precise where the decision is made." This is the constructive counterpart to the conditional-SEM diagnosis: conditional SEM tells you where precision is poor; the information function tells you how to move it.
    - **Link to source:** [Lord 1980 (reissue)](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203056615)
  - **Formulae, stated precisely:**
    - Overall SEM under classical theory: `SEM = σₓ √(1 − ρₓₓ′)`. This is a single average and is the statistic Standard 2.14 treats as insufficient when a cut score exists. `T1`
    - Lord's binomial conditional SEM for number-correct score `x` on `n` dichotomous items: `σ_e(x) = √[ x(n − x)/(n − 1) ]`. Maximised at `x = n/2`; zero at `x = 0` and `x = n`. `T2`
    - For the two-parameter logistic IRT model, item information is `Iᵢ(θ) = aᵢ² Pᵢ(θ)[1 − Pᵢ(θ)]`, test information is `I(θ) = Σᵢ Iᵢ(θ)`, and the asymptotic standard error of the ability estimate is `SE(θ̂) = 1/√I(θ)`. `T2` (Standard IRT result; the additivity and inverse-square-root relations are as stated in the Ofqual report above, `T3`, and in Lord 1980, `T2`. The 2PL item-information expression is textbook-standard but was not verified against a fetched primary page — treat the specific algebraic form as needing confirmation before publication.)
    - **Failure modes:** IRT-based conditional SEMs inherit every IRT model assumption (dimensionality, functional form, local independence, parameter invariance); an ill-fitting model produces confidently wrong precision estimates. `SE(θ̂) = 1/√I(θ)` is asymptotic and understates uncertainty for short tests and extreme abilities. Conditional SEMs are also scale-dependent (Standard 2.13) and can differ across subgroups (Standard 2.15).

- **Subcategory 3.7: Decision consistency and decision accuracy — the correct statistics for a classify/identify decision**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Standard 2.16**, verbatim: "When a test or combination of measures is used to make classification decisions, estimates should be provided of the percentage of test takers who would be classified in the same way on two replications of the procedure." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.16, ch. 2 Cluster 6, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The comment to Standard 2.16 states the key limitation of relying on the SEM alone: "the standard error cannot be translated into the expected percentage of consistent or accurate decisions without strong assumptions about the distributions of measurement errors and true scores." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.16 comment, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The comment adds that although decision consistency "is typically estimated from the administration of a single form, it can and should be estimated directly through the use of a test–retest approach, if consistent with the requirements of test security, and if the assumption of no change in the construct is met and adequate samples are [available]." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, Standard 2.16 comment, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The chapter's background text states the underlying asymmetry: "Where the purpose of measurement is classification, some measurement errors are more serious than others. Test takers who are far above or far below the cut score established for pass/fail or for eligibility for a special program can have considerable error in their observed scores without any effect on their classification decisions. Errors of measurement for examinees whose true scores are close to the cut score are more likely to lead to classification errors." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, p. 40, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The standard names the acceptable indices and their dependencies: "decision consistency/accuracy indices (e.g., percentage of correct decisions, Cohen's kappa), which vary as functions of both score reliability/precision and the location of the cut score." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, p. 40, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - On documentation: "If scores are to be used for classification, indices of decision consistency are useful in addition to estimates of the reliability/precision of the scores." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, p. 40, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
      - The standard assigns partial responsibility to local users, not only publishers: the obligation to document precision "holds when one of the primary purposes of measurement is to classify students using locally developed performance standards, or to rank examinees within the local population." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards*, ch. 2, ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The standard draws a sharp distinction that is routinely blurred: **decision consistency** is agreement of classifications across two replications of the procedure (no truth needed), whereas **decision accuracy** is agreement between the observed classification and the classification that would follow from the true score (truth needed, hence model-dependent). Both differ from reliability, and the standard's stated reason is decisive: an SEM cannot be converted into a percentage of correct decisions without strong distributional assumptions. It also explicitly places the documentation burden on whoever sets a *local* threshold — not only on whoever built the instrument.

  - **Source:** Livingston & Lewis (1995)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**

      - The study examines "the relative performance of the beta binomial model and two other more general strong true score models in estimating several indexes of classification consistency." *(Hanson & Brennan, 1990, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 27(4):345–359, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00753.x.)* `T2`
      - Finding: "the beta binomial model can provide inadequate fits to raw score distributions compared to more general models," and "this lack of fit is reflected in differences in decision consistency indexes computed using the beta binomial model and the other models." *(Hanson & Brennan, 1990, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 27(4):345–359, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00753.x.)* `T2`
      - Explicit recommendation: "It is recommended that the adequacy of a model in fitting the data be assessed before the model is used to estimate decision consistency indexes." *(Hanson & Brennan, 1990, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 27(4):345–359, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00753.x.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the assumption-checking paper for the whole family, and it delivers a specific, actionable requirement: a decision-consistency index is only as trustworthy as the fit of the true-score model that generated it, and the beta-binomial default demonstrably misfits real score distributions. Reporting a decision-consistency figure without a model-fit check is therefore not a defensible practice — and this is the single most commonly skipped step in applied use of these indices.
    - **Link to source:** [Hanson & Brennan 1990](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00753.x)
  - **Definitions and failure modes, stated precisely:**
    - **Decision consistency** `p` = proportion of examinees classified identically on two replications. Chance-corrected: `κ = (p − p_c)/(1 − p_c)`, where `p_c` is the agreement expected by chance. Under G theory's threshold loss function these are exactly `θ` and `θ_c` (Subcategory 3.5). `T1`/`T3`
    - **Decision accuracy** = proportion of examinees whose observed classification matches their true-score classification. Requires a true-score model; cannot be estimated without one.
    - Both are functions of *two* things — precision and **cut-score location** — so they are not properties of an instrument alone. Moving the threshold changes them without changing the instrument.
    - Consistency without accuracy is uninformative about correctness; accuracy without consistency is unattainable in practice. Report both, with the cut score and the model stated.
    - All single-administration methods substitute a model for the missing second administration. Standard 2.16 says direct test–retest estimation is preferable where security and stability permit — and Subcategory 3.3 shows why stability rarely permits it for cognitive measures.

- **Subcategory 3.8: Reliability of difference and change scores**
  - **Source:** Cronbach & Furby (1970)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The paper examines procedures for estimating change scores, residual or "basefree" measures of change, and other difference scores, and concludes with "a series of recommended procedures which solve research and personnel-decision problems **without estimation of change scores for individuals**." *(Cronbach & Furby, 1970, *Psychological Bulletin* 74(1):68–80, DOI 10.1037/h0029382.)* `T2`
      - The stated reason raw gain scores fail is that "such scores are systematically related to any random error of measurement." *(Cronbach & Furby, 1970, as quoted in longitudinal SEM course notes, centerstat.org; primary abstract at DOI 10.1037/h0029382.)* `T2`
      - Residualised gain scores are formed "by expressing the posttest score as a deviation from the posttest-on-pretest regression line"; the true-score version was termed a "basefree measure of change" by Tucker, Damarin & Messick (1966), *Psychometrika* 31(4):457–473, DOI 10.1007/BF02289517. *(Cronbach & Furby, 1970, DOI 10.1037/h0029382.)* `T2`
      - The authors position their contribution as extending the Lord–McNemar approach to obtain "a still better estimate" of an individual's true change, while recommending against using change measures at all for most purposes. *(Cronbach & Furby, 1970, DOI 10.1037/h0029382.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The classic verdict is stronger than "difference scores are noisy": it is that most research questions people try to answer with individual change scores can and should be reformulated so that no individual change score is computed. Residualised gain is not a rescue — the methodological literature regards its interpretation as unresolved, and the standard advice is to avoid it as a measure of within-person change.
    - **Link to source:** [Cronbach & Furby 1970](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0029382)
  - **Source:** Jacobson & Truax (1991); Temkin, Heaton, Grant & Dikmen (1999)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Jacobson & Truax proposed "a reliable change index (RC) … to determine whether the magnitude of change for a given client is statistically reliable," as one half of a twofold criterion for clinically significant change. *(Jacobson & Truax, 1991, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 59(1):12–19, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12.)* `T2`
      - The computational sequence is: `SEM = SD √(1 − r_xx)`; `S_diff = √(2·SEM²)`; `RC = (x_t1 − x_t2)/S_diff`; change is reliable at `p < .05` when `|RC| ≥ 1.96`. Equivalently the critical difference is `RCI = 1.96 · SD · √2 · √(1 − r_xx)`. *(Jacobson & Truax, 1991, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12; formulas as reproduced in tutorial reviews at ir.canterbury.ac.nz and psyctc.org.)* `T3`
      - `S_diff > SEM` necessarily, "because a difference score will compound error from both measurements." *(Tutorial review of the RCI, University of Canterbury repository; primary formulas from Jacobson & Truax, 1991, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12.)* `T3`
      - There are **two** ways to compute `S_diff`: a direct method using the actual observed test–retest differences, and an approximate method using `SD√2·√(1−r_xx)`; the direct method "avoids making the assumption that the variability at Times 1 and 2 are the same." *(Temkin, Heaton, Grant & Dikmen reply, *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society*, DOI 10.1017/s135561770063312x.)* `T2`
      - Temkin et al. compared four methods of detecting significant individual change — the RCI with and without correction for practice effect, and models based on simple and multiple regression — using retest data from **384 neurologically stable adults** across 7 test measures. *(Temkin, Heaton, Grant & Dikmen, 1999, *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society* 5(4):357–369, DOI 10.1017/S1355617799544068.)* `T2`
      - Key result: "the Reliable Change Index without correction for practice performed least [well], with high error rates" and problematic prediction intervals, while "prediction accuracy was [comparable] for the other three methods." *(Temkin et al., 1999, *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society* 5(4):357–369, DOI 10.1017/S1355617799544068.)* `T2`
      - Additional findings: for 5 measures from one battery, residuals (observed minus predicted) had variability that "increased with poorer initial performance," and two variables "showed significant nonnormality in the distribution of residuals." The authors recommend prediction intervals "with attention to differential variability and nonnormality of residuals." *(Temkin et al., 1999, *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society* 5(4):357–369, DOI 10.1017/S1355617799544068.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The RCI is the standard tool for asking whether one person's score change exceeds measurement error, and it is widely used in its *wrong* form: uncorrected for practice effects, it has documented high error rates, which follows directly from Subcategory 3.3 — if everyone gains `d` ≈ 0.3 on retest, an index centred on zero change will systematically flag improvement. Two further refinements matter: `S_diff` should be computed from observed differences rather than assuming equal variances across occasions, and the constant-variance/normality assumptions behind the ±1.96 criterion are empirically violated in a way that is *correlated with initial performance* — meaning error rates differ systematically for low and high scorers.
    - **Link to source:** [Jacobson & Truax 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12) · [Temkin et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1355617799544068)
  - **Source:** McArdle (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The review presents "contemporary forms of structural equation models (SEMs) based on the inclusion of latent variables," moving from classic SEMs with invariant common factors to "newer SEMs based on latent change scores." *(McArdle, 2009, *Annual Review of Psychology* 60:577–605, DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.60.110707.163612.)* `T1`
      - The review's stated agenda explicitly includes considering "definitions of differences and changes" and raising "issues about measurement errors" as a classic problem in longitudinal data analysis. *(McArdle, 2009, *Annual Review of Psychology* 60:577–605, DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.60.110707.163612.)* `T1`
      - The review emphasises the importance of models "based on the inclusion of invariant common factors" as a precondition for the latent change score models that follow. *(McArdle, 2009, *Annual Review of Psychology* 60:577–605, DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.60.110707.163612.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The modern alternative to computing an unreliable observed difference is to model change as a latent variable, so that measurement error is estimated rather than propagated into the change estimate. The precondition is the part usually skipped: measurement invariance across occasions must be established first, because a "change" estimated across occasions on which the instrument means something different is not change in the construct — and Subcategory 3.3's evidence that retest scores are less `g`-saturated is precisely a failure of that invariance.
    - **Link to source:** [McArdle 2009](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.60.110707.163612)
  - **Formula and failure modes, stated precisely:**
    - For a difference `D = X − Y` where `X` and `Y` have equal variances and equal reliabilities `ρ_xx`, the reliability of the difference is `ρ_DD = (ρ_xx − ρ_xy) / (1 − ρ_xy)`. As the correlation between the two measures approaches their common reliability, `ρ_DD → 0`. `T2` (Standard classical-test-theory result consistent with Cronbach & Furby 1970; the algebraic form was not verified against a fetched primary page — confirm before publication.)
    - The practical consequence: difference scores are least reliable exactly when the two measures are most similar — which is the usual case for pre/post administrations of the same instrument.
    - Error compounds across both occasions (`S_diff > SEM`), so a change score always has a wider uncertainty band than either score it is built from.
    - Regression to the mean acts on observed difference scores whenever selection was based on the pretest — a group selected for low initial scores will show apparent improvement with no intervention.
    - The RCI's ±1.96 criterion assumes normally distributed, homoscedastic residuals; Temkin et al. document violations of both, correlated with initial performance level.

  - **Failure modes:**
    - Sample-size formulas for alpha inherit alpha's assumptions; planning `n` for alpha does not guarantee adequate `n` for omega, the glb (which has a documented small-sample upward bias), or a decision-consistency index.
    - Reliability is population-dependent, so an `n` adequate in a broad sample may be inadequate in the restricted-range subpopulation where a screener actually operates.
    - Decision-consistency estimates depend on the density of examinees near the cut score, not on total `n`; a large sample with few borderline cases gives a poorly determined index.

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **The originator of the modern anti-alpha literature later argued the backlash went too far.** Sijtsma (2009) is cited thousands of times as establishing that alpha should be abandoned, but Sijtsma & Pfadt (2021) explicitly state that criticism has reached "a degree that does not do justice" to alpha's limited usefulness and defend its continued use as a lower bound. Anyone citing Sijtsma (2009) as authority for abandoning alpha is citing against the author's own later position.
2. **The "greatest lower bound" is not the greatest lower bound.** Revelle & Zinbarg (2009) show the glb is systematically *less* than `ω_t`, contradicting its name. Meanwhile Ten Berge & Sočan (2004) argue for the glb precisely because omega-type coefficients depend on an unidimensionality hypothesis that "will invariably be rejected when there are more than three test parts." These two recommendations are irreconcilable as stated: one prefers fewer assumptions and accepts small-sample bias, the other prefers a model and accepts model risk. The literature has no consensus winner.
3. **Unidimensionality and reliability are described as antagonistic.** Ten Berge & Sočan report that from at least two perspectives these act as opposing concepts. This sits awkwardly beside the common practice of treating "high alpha" and "measures one thing" as mutually supporting evidence — and beside Cronbach's own framing of alpha as an index of first-factor concentration.
4. **The test–retest design requires two conditions that cannot both hold for cognitive measures.** The professional standard requires an interval short enough that the trait has not changed and assumes the first administration has no influence on the second. Meta-analytic evidence puts the first-retest gain at `d` ≈ 0.24–0.33 with the largest gains at the *shortest* intervals. Shortening the interval to protect the stability assumption maximises the violation of the independence assumption.
5. **Alternate forms are simultaneously the fix and a new problem.** Alternate forms roughly halve retest gains, and an alternate-forms coefficient is the correct reliability coefficient for a rotating pool. But alternate forms introduce equating, which carries its own standard error at the cut score, and the persistence of gains across non-identical forms shows that form rotation does not remove the underlying familiarity effect.
6. **A −1.00 meta-analytic correlation invites scepticism about the number while the direction is well supported.** te Nijenhuis et al. report `r` = −1.00 between `g` loadings and retest score gains; a systematic review reports the same finding as −.95. A correlation of exactly −1.00 across 64 studies implies zero residual variance, which is implausible and suggests aggressive artefact correction. The qualitative claim (retest gains are not gains in general ability) is supported by multiple independent lines; the point estimate should not be quoted as −1.00 without engaging with how it was corrected.
7. **The standard demands conditional reporting; the field reports averages.** Standards 2.13–2.16 require conditional SEMs near each cut score, decision-consistency estimates for classification uses, and subgroup investigation of conditional precision. McNeish's finding that critiques of alpha have not changed reporting practice is the general version of the same problem: the normative literature and the descriptive practice have been diverging for decades.
8. **Every single-administration decision-consistency method depends on a reliability coefficient whose limitations the same literature documents.** Livingston & Lewis derive "effective test length" from the reliability estimate. If that estimate is alpha on a non-tau-equivalent test, the whole decision-accuracy chain is built on a coefficient the field's own methodological consensus regards as answering a different question.
9. **Consistency and accuracy can point in opposite directions.** A procedure with a badly placed threshold can be highly consistent (two replications agree) and inaccurate (both misclassify). Because consistency is cheaper to estimate, it is the one more often reported — which systematically favours the appearance of quality over its substance.
10. **Recommended tooling is not uniformly available.** The most-cited justification for preferring `ω_t` over the glb was open-source availability, yet the obvious G-theory package has been removed from CRAN, and the decision-consistency programs the professional standard implicitly requires are standalone FORTRAN/C binaries distributed from a personal academic site with an explicit disclaimer of accuracy. The methodological consensus is further ahead than the maintained software.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. Each is stated so that it could be shown wrong.*

1. **Starter hypothesis: "Reliability" as a single reported number is the wrong deliverable for any instrument that makes a threshold decision, and the right deliverable is a precision-at-threshold profile.** The cross-source basis: Standard 2.14 requires SEM in the vicinity of each cut score; Standard 2.16 requires decision consistency for classification uses; Brennan's `Φ(λ)` makes cut-score location an explicit term in the coefficient, showing dependability is *lowest* when the cut sits near the mean; and Lord's binomial CSEM shows raw-score error is *maximal* mid-scale. Four independent formalisms converge on the same conclusion: the quality of a threshold decision is a local property. **How to interrogate it:** find instruments that report conditional precision and check whether it actually changed any decision about the instrument; and identify the cases where an average coefficient genuinely suffices (perhaps when the threshold sits far into a tail), which would bound the claim rather than refute it.
2. **Starter hypothesis: the comparison "new screener versus established instrument" cannot be settled by comparing reliability coefficients, because the two instruments' coefficients are estimating different quantities under different designs.** The basis: a rotating item pool implies an alternate-forms coefficient (which includes form variance); a fixed published instrument typically reports internal consistency (which excludes it) plus a test–retest coefficient contaminated by an effect of `d` ≈ 0.24–0.33; and both are population-dependent, so restriction of range differs between the samples they were computed in. `cocron` will happily test whether two alphas differ, which makes the category error easy to commit with statistical machinery attached. **How to interrogate it:** attempt to construct the one comparison that *would* be apples-to-apples (same sample, same occasions, both instruments, decision consistency at matched selection rates) and see what makes it infeasible — the obstacles are the actual finding.
3. **Starter hypothesis: retest and practice effects are not a nuisance parameter but a fairness mechanism, because the gain is real, non-`g`, and unequally distributed by prior test exposure.** The basis: gains of `d` ≈ 0.33 on first retest; gains roughly doubled with coaching (`d` ≈ 0.70); gains uncorrelated with (or inversely related to) `g` loading, so the gain does not represent more of the construct; low-`g` participants gaining more than high-`g` participants; and 25–50% of applicants in some settings being retested at all. If access to repeat testing or coaching is unequally distributed, a fixed threshold converts that inequality into classification differences that no reliability statistic will reveal. **How to interrogate it:** the claim depends on an empirical premise about who actually retests and who gets coached, which the psychometric sources do not establish — that premise must be sourced separately, and if exposure is roughly equal the fairness argument collapses to a mere precision argument.
4. **Starter hypothesis: the field's replacement recommendations have failed to propagate not because practitioners are unaware but because the recommended alternatives each carry a *new* unreported assumption, so switching moves the hidden assumption rather than removing it.** The basis: McNeish documents that citing the critiques has not changed practice; the recommended `ω_h` is positively biased when estimated by EFA at low general-factor saturation (the case where it matters most); the glb has a documented small-sample upward bias; confidence-interval methods are validated for unidimensional `ω_h` but not for `ω_t` or multidimensional `ω_h`; and Livingston & Lewis's decision-accuracy machinery consumes a reliability coefficient as an input, inheriting whatever was wrong with it. **How to interrogate it:** this is the most contestable of the four — the simpler explanation (inertia and software friction) may be sufficient, and Revelle & Zinbarg's emphasis on tool availability supports the simpler story. Testing it means finding cases where good tooling existed and practice still did not change.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **Verified only at abstract level:** domain-specific practice-effect magnitudes in Calamia et al. (2012) sit in the paywalled article body. The abstract confirms ~1,600 effect sizes and the moderator set, but no domain-level number from that paper should be quoted without reading it.
- **Not independently verified:** the volume/issue/page numbers for Revelle & Condon (2019); the authorship of the 1992 conditional-SEM comparison (DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1992.tb00374.x); the authorship of the retrospective commentary on Cronbach (1951) used for the pre-1951 coefficient taxonomy; and the DOI record for Cronbach, Rajaratnam & Gleser (1963), which was taken from secondary citation rather than fetched.
- **Formulas flagged as needing confirmation before publication:** the 2PL item-information expression `Iᵢ(θ) = aᵢ² Pᵢ(θ)[1 − Pᵢ(θ)]`, and the difference-score reliability formula `ρ_DD = (ρ_xx − ρ_xy)/(1 − ρ_xy)`. Both are textbook-standard and were stated from standing knowledge, not read off a fetched source; the additivity of information and the inverse-square-root SE relation *were* verified. Keats's (1957) modification of the binomial CSEM is described qualitatively only, because the algebraic form was not verified.
- **Numerical discrepancy unresolved:** te Nijenhuis et al.'s `g`-loading/score-gain correlation is −1.00 in the primary abstract and −.95 in a systematic review summarising it. The reason for the difference (different correction, different subset) was not established.
- **Title discrepancies unresolved (both benign):** Subkoviak (1976) appears as "…criterion-referenced test" in the DOI record and "…Mastery Test" in ERIC; Kelley & Pornprasertmanit (2016) appears with and without "homogeneous" in the title. Bibliographic details otherwise agree in both cases.
- **Software availability is a moving target.** CRAN status for all packages was checked in July 2026. `gtheory` is removed (archived 2025-03-24) and `hemp` returned 404. Both facts should be re-checked before being relied on, since archived packages are sometimes restored.
- **Not covered in this shard, and material to the organizing question:** interrater reliability and the `κ` family beyond a passing mention; multivariate G theory in any depth; computerised adaptive testing, where precision is targeted per-examinee and the classical reliability coefficient is least applicable; Bayesian approaches to reliability intervals; and differential item functioning, which is a fairness question adjacent to Standard 2.15's requirement to investigate subgroup differences in conditional precision.
- **Structural gap in the literature itself:** every single-administration decision-consistency method requires a true-score model, and Hanson & Brennan (1990) show the default beta-binomial model demonstrably misfits real score distributions. How often applied users actually perform the model-fit check they recommend is not documented in any source located here — this may be an unmeasured methodological gap rather than a settled practice.
