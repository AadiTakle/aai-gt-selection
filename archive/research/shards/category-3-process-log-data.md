### Category 3: Process data, log files, and interactive-task telemetry

*General idea: Interactive assessment platforms emit a dense stream of behavioural exhaust — every click, pause, revision, navigation, and tool invocation — and a large literature argues that this stream reveals how a person arrived at an answer, not merely whether they got it right. The evidence, examined closely, supports a much narrower claim than the enthusiasm suggests: a small number of theoretically pre-specified, well-behaved indicators (most robustly, response-time-based disengagement detection) demonstrably improve score validity, while the large majority of "found" process features are task-specific, weakly reliable, rarely replicated, and — in the handful of studies that actually run the test — do not survive a controlled incremental-validity comparison against outcome scores plus an existing ability measure.*

- **Subcategory 3.1: What process data is, and the frameworks needed to make it analysable at all**
  - **Source:** Bergner & von Davier (2019); Kroehne & Goldhammer (2018); Provasnik (2021); Maddox (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A review of process-data use in a long-running national assessment programme proposes an ordered five-level structure for how process data can be used, ranging from ignoring process entirely (counting only outcomes), to incorporating process data as auxiliary or essential *in addition to* the outcome, to modelling the process *as* the outcome — either holistically in a rubric score or in a measurement model accounting for sequential dependencies. *(Bergner & von Davier, 2019, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 44(6):706–732, DOI 10.3102/1076998618784700.)* `T2` `[COI: second author was affiliated with a commercial assessment organisation; the assessment programme reviewed is delivered under contract by commercial testing vendors]`
      - The same review documents that historically, Level 1 (outcome only) and Level 4 uses dominated; with the advent of computer-based testing, Level 2 uses (treating computer skills as relevant to performance) became more common; and only in recent years have Level 3 and Level 5 uses emerged — for example, using response times to filter for motivation. *(Bergner & von Davier, 2019, DOI 10.3102/1076998618784700.)* `T2`
      - Log data from educational assessments is formally classified as a species of *paradata* — data generated as a by-product of computer-assisted data collection, a category originating in survey research. *(Kroehne & Goldhammer, 2018, *Behaviormetrika* 45(2):527–563, DOI 10.1007/s41237-018-0063-y.)* `T2`
      - The same paper proposes finite state machines as a generic analytic framework whose specific benefit is *separating platform-specific log events from the definition of indicators by states*; states represent log data filtered through a theoretical process model, so the log file is encoded selectively rather than exhaustively. The authors explicitly frame this in terms of the definition of "complete" log data, the verification of log data, and the reproducibility of log-data analyses. *(Kroehne & Goldhammer, 2018, DOI 10.1007/s41237-018-0063-y.)* `T2`
      - A keynote published in a peer-reviewed venue argues that process data "have no 'natural,' normative, or prescribed format" and "do not exist 'out there'" but are constructs that must be defined a priori or a posteriori relative to a theory or research question; the author cautions against reifying process data. *(Provasnik, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9, Article 1, DOI 10.1186/s40536-020-00092-z.)* `T2` `[COI: paper reports a symposium convened by a commercial assessment organisation; author affiliated with a national statistics/education agency]`
      - The same paper records that a 2018 international symposium of process-data researchers and large-scale assessment developers reached general consensus that the field still needed to (1) develop a systematic approach to log files — i.e. answer what log files should capture — and (2) develop a theory for process data — i.e. answer how to use it. It characterises prior work as "undertaken largely independently by individual scholars, research teams, or organizations" and as "somewhat haphazard." *(Provasnik, 2021, DOI 10.1186/s40536-020-00092-z.)* `T2`
      - A commissioned policy working paper states that although process data are often described as "collected" as a by-product of computer-based testing, they "are nevertheless constructed phenomena, that cannot escape from the methodological assumptions, treatments and the application of theoretical models," and that the ubiquity, granularity, or technical availability of such data "does not negate the requirement for interpretation and use arguments, and appropriate validation." *(Maddox, 2023, OECD Education Working Papers No. 286, DOI 10.1787/5d9009ff-en.)* `T3` `[COI: author affiliated with a commercial assessment analytics company as well as a university; paper commissioned by an intergovernmental body that operates large-scale assessments]`
      - The same paper notes that log data is "especially vulnerable to threats to validity because of its partiality, as it only captures log events from respondent interactions with the keyboard and mouse," and specifically does not capture idle time away from keyboard, reading behaviour between clicks, or off-screen activity such as use of pen and paper. *(Maddox, 2023, DOI 10.1787/5d9009ff-en.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** "Process data" is not a thing you collect; it is a thing you *construct* from a raw event stream by imposing a theory about which events matter and how they aggregate. The event stream itself is also systematically incomplete — it sees keyboard and mouse, and is blind to reading, thinking, scratch paper, and everything happening away from the input device. The most useful taxonomy in the literature is not about *what* is logged but about *how strongly the process is allowed to enter the inference*: from ignored, to auxiliary, to essential alongside the outcome, to being the outcome. Almost all of the interesting validity questions live at the boundary between "auxiliary" and "essential," and the field's own senior figures characterise its accumulated work as theory-poor and uncoordinated.
    - **Link to source:** [Process data in NAEP](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998618784700); [Log data framework](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41237-018-0063-y); [Quixotic quest](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-020-00092-z); [OECD process data](https://doi.org/10.1787/5d9009ff-en)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The strongest positive evidence — response-time indicators of disengagement, which demonstrably improve score validity**
  - **Source:** Wise & Kong (2005); Wise & DeMars (2006); Wise (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A response-time-based index (the proportion of test items on which an examinee exhibited "solution behaviour" rather than a rapid guess) computed on an 80-item university assessment showed very high internal consistency (α = .97). *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - The same index showed convergent validity via significant correlations with self-reported effort (r = .25) and with a person-fit statistic (r = −.42), and discriminant validity via near-zero correlations with a college-admissions test score. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - In the same study, filtering out examinees flagged as low-effort by the response-time index successively increased the convergent-validity correlation between test scores and an external criterion, while the criterion scores themselves remained virtually unchanged — the pattern expected if filtering removes noise rather than shifting the sample. Filtering by the response-time index produced modestly *more* favourable results than filtering by self-reported effort while deleting fewer examinees. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - An IRT model that incorporates item response time into both proficiency estimation and item-parameter estimation ("effort-moderated" model) outperformed a standard three-parameter logistic model in a real-data study and a simulation study on four criteria: better model fit, more accurate item-parameter estimates, more accurate test-information estimates, and proficiency estimates with higher convergent validity against external variables. *(Wise & DeMars, 2006, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 43(1):19–38, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x.)* `T2`
      - A later review article treats rapid-guessing behaviour as an identifiable, interpretable, and consequential phenomenon in its own right, addressing its identification, interpretation, and implications for score use. *(Wise, 2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 36(4):52–61, DOI 10.1111/emip.12165.)* `T2`
      - Subsequent simulation work on effort-moderated IRT reports that rapid-guessing produces *differential* bias in ability estimates — greater bias for lower-scoring examinees than higher-scoring ones — and frames this differential bias explicitly as a fairness concern, not merely an accuracy concern. *(Analysis of two-step and multidimensional effort-moderated IRT variants, 2024, open-access methodological article; specific journal/volume not independently verified here.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The single clearest incremental-validity win in this entire literature is also the least glamorous: use response times to detect that a person was not really trying, and either down-weight or exclude those responses. This clears the bar the way the rest of the field does not — it improves model fit, improves parameter recovery, and raises the correlation between the score and an *external* criterion, which is the actual test of added validity rather than added correlation. It is worth noticing exactly why it works. The indicator is theoretically pre-specified (a threshold below which a response cannot reflect solution behaviour), it targets a *threat* to the outcome score rather than trying to measure a new construct, and its claim is negative and modest: "this response is uninformative." Almost every failed process-data programme differs from this one by attempting something far more ambitious.
    - **Link to source:** [Response time effort](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2); [Effort-moderated IRT](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x); [Rapid guessing](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12165)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Process indicators in interactive problem-solving tasks — strong correlations, and what happens when incremental validity is actually tested**
  - **Source:** Greiff, Wüstenberg & Avvisati (2015); Goldhammer et al. (2014); Stadler, Hofer & Greiff (2020); Teig, Scherer & Kjærnsli (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Log files from a single interactive problem-solving item in a large international assessment (N = 16,219 students, 44 countries/economies) were analysed for use of an optimal exploration strategy (vary-one-thing-at-a-time, VOTAT). The authors report that VOTAT application was strongly related both to performance on that same item and to overall problem-solving proficiency, at the individual and country level, and that distinct levels of non-mastery were identifiable — including students who applied VOTAT yet still failed the item. *(Greiff, Wüstenberg & Avvisati, 2015, *Computers & Education* 91:92–105, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2015.10.018.)* `T2` — *the specific coefficient magnitudes reported in the paper's tables could not be verified from the accessible abstract/landing pages; the relationship is therefore stated qualitatively here.*
      - In a national field trial of an adult skills assessment (N = 1,020, ages 16–65), linear mixed models showed that the time-on-task effect was *positive* in problem solving (which required controlled processing) and increased with task difficulty, but *negative* in reading (which required more routine processing) and more negative the easier the task. In problem solving the positive time-on-task effect *decreased* with increasing skill; in reading the negative time-on-task effect *increased* with increasing skill. The authors conclude time on task "has no uniform interpretation but is a function of task difficulty and individual skill." *(Goldhammer, Naumann, Stelter, Tóth, Rölke & Klieme, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(3):608–626, DOI 10.1037/a0034716.)* `T2`
      - In a sample of students who all achieved the *maximum* score on five complex problem-solving tasks (i.e. zero variance in the outcome score), significant variance remained in time-on-task (s² = 260.09, p = .005) and in number of interactions (s² = 0.381, p < .001). *(Stadler, Hofer & Greiff, 2020, *Computers in Human Behavior* 111:106442, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442.)* `T2`
      - In the same study, both process indicators significantly predicted GPA in a structural equation model — time-on-task β = 0.17, p = .015; number of interactions β = −0.17, p < .001. When an intelligence measure was added as a predictor of GPA (β = 0.55, p < .001), both process effects were reduced to negligible, non-significant levels: time-on-task β = −0.09, p = .053; number of interactions β = 0.09, p = .100. *(Stadler, Hofer & Greiff, 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442.)* `T2`
      - Note on the same result: the two process coefficients did not merely shrink toward zero when intelligence was added — both *reversed sign* (+0.17 → −0.09; −0.17 → +0.09). *(Derived directly from the coefficients reported in Stadler, Hofer & Greiff, 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442.)* `T2`
      - Log-file data from simulated scientific inquiry tasks in a large international assessment has been used to identify distinct latent profiles of inquiry performance that go beyond response correctness. *(Teig, Scherer & Kjærnsli, 2020, *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* 57(9):1400–1429, DOI 10.1002/tea.21657.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This subcategory contains the field's flagship demonstrations, and they establish two things cleanly and one thing not at all. Cleanly established: (a) behaviour during an interactive task is not redundant with the score — people with identical scores demonstrably behave differently, and those behavioural differences are not random; (b) the *meaning* of even the simplest process feature is not fixed — time on task flips sign depending on whether the task demands controlled or routine processing, and its slope depends on the examinee's own skill, so "spent longer" is not interpretable without knowing the task and the person. Not established: that these indicators add validity. The one study here that ran the decisive test — regressing an external criterion on process indicators with and without an established ability measure in the model — found the process effects collapsed to non-significance once ability entered. The sign reversal is a further warning: coefficients that flip when a correlated predictor enters are behaving like suppressors or unstable estimates, not like a stable independent signal. The authors nonetheless summarise their result as supporting process data "over and above test scores," which is defensible only against the *bare score*, not against the score plus an ability measure. That gap between what was shown and what was claimed is the characteristic move of this literature.
    - **Link to source:** [PISA log-file showcase](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2015.10.018); [Time on task](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034716); [First among equals](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442); [Inquiry log profiles](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.21657)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Evidence-Centered Design — why the claim must be built before the data is mined**
  - **Source:** Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond (2003); Mislevy & Haertel (2006); Goldhammer, Hahnel, Kroehne & Zehner (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The foundational statement of evidence-centered design frames assessment as an evidentiary argument: "we observe what students say, do, or make in a few particular circumstances and attempt to infer what they know, can do, or have accomplished more generally," connected by "a web of inference." The framework makes explicit the interrelations among substantive arguments, assessment designs, and operational processes, and is organised around linked student, evidence, and task models. *(Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond, 2003, *Measurement: Interdisciplinary Research and Perspectives* 1(1):3–62, DOI 10.1207/s15366359mea0101_02.)* `T2` `[COI: two of three authors were employed by a commercial assessment organisation that funded the design programme]`
      - The stated motivation for the framework was the need to develop assessments incorporating "purposes, technologies, and psychological perspectives that are not well served by familiar forms of assessments"; a companion introduction from the same programme states the framework spans "a broad range of assessment types, from familiar standardized tests and classroom quizzes, to coached practice systems and simulation-based assessments, to portfolios and student-tutor interaction." *(Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond, 2003, DOI 10.1207/s15366359mea0101_02; Mislevy, Almond & Lukas, 2003, *ETS Research Report Series* 2003(1), DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.2003.tb01908.x.)* `T3` `[COI: as above]`
      - A subsequent treatment aimed at practitioners sets out implications of evidence-centered design for educational testing generally. *(Mislevy & Haertel, 2006, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 25(4):6–20, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00075.x.)* `T2`
      - A validity-focused paper argues process indicators should move "from byproduct to design factor," and that the *activity spaces within items need to be designed* so that observed behaviours constitute a representative sample of the universe of possible observations — otherwise the generalisation inference cannot be justified. *(Goldhammer, Hahnel, Kroehne & Zehner, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9(1), Article 20, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5.)* `T2`
      - The same paper states that "there is a lack of theory or process models relating behavioral low-level features to attributes of the work process through evidence identification and accumulation," and that such comprehensive theories "may not be available for complex ability/competence domains encompassing various cognitive processes." It therefore concludes that both exploratory theory-building and data-driven evidence identification are needed, and that a *necessary condition* for cumulative progress is that indicator construction be transparent — that how derived actions and states link to log events be made explicit — and reproducible across studies. *(Goldhammer et al., 2021, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Evidence-centered design is the answer to a question most process-data work never asks: what claim am I entitled to make, and what would count as evidence for it? Its discipline is that you specify the target competency, the observable features that constitute evidence about it, and the tasks engineered to elicit those observables — *in that order, before data exists*. This matters more for process data than for anything else, because a log file supports an effectively unbounded number of post-hoc features, so a post-hoc "finding" carries almost no evidential weight. Note also the honest self-assessment from within the design-oriented camp: the theories needed to link low-level behavioural features to work-process attributes largely do not exist for complex competencies. That admission cuts both ways — it justifies exploratory work, but it also means that a design-first framework is currently being applied without the substantive theory it presupposes, and its stated minimum condition (transparent, reproducible indicator construction) is a *precondition* for evidence, not evidence itself.
    - **Link to source:** [Structure of educational assessments](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15366359mea0101_02); [ECD implications](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00075.x); [Byproduct to design factor](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5)

- **Subcategory 3.5: The measurement critique — task-specificity, non-replication, overfitting, and explicit null incremental-validity results**
  - **Source:** Stadler, Brandl & Greiff (2023); Goldhammer et al. (2021); Zhang & Deane (2015); Provasnik (2021); AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Reviewing two decades of interactive tasks in large-scale assessments, the authors state: "the lack of generalizability of findings and a missing relation between data and theory strongly limit the replicability of research on sequence data from interactive tasks in educational large-scale assessments, and thus, its scientific value." *(Stadler, Brandl & Greiff, 2023, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 39(6):1852–1859, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12847.)* `T2`
      - The same paper distinguishes low-level features (time-on-task, number of interactions), which "can be more idiosyncratic and may not convey the same meaning across different tasks," from higher-level, theoretically coded features, which can support conceptual replication across differing items. *(Stadler, Brandl & Greiff, 2023, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12847.)* `T2`
      - The same paper characterises the existing corpus as "scarce, often restricted to single or a few selected items with little common theoretical underpinning and minimal attempts at replicating findings." *(Stadler, Brandl & Greiff, 2023, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12847.)* `T2`
      - A study of keystroke-logged writing-process features tested their incremental value over automatically extracted product (final-text) features for predicting human ratings. Adding a writing-fluency process factor produced a statistically significant but very small improvement in adjusted R², from .709 to .714, F(1, 500) = 7.84, p < .001. **None** of the other process factors had statistically significant incremental value, and **on cross-validation in a second sample, none of the factors added incrementally to the product features at a statistically significant level.** *(Zhang & Deane, 2015, ETS Research Report RR-15-27, *ETS Research Report Series* 2015(2):1–12, DOI 10.1002/ets2.12075.)* `T3` `[COI: both authors employed by a commercial assessment organisation; report published in that organisation's own series]`
      - The authors of that study attribute the null to a structural reason rather than a technical one: human raters "have no knowledge of the writing process leading to the final text," and the product features already measure the text quality specified in the scoring rubric. *(Zhang & Deane, 2015, DOI 10.1002/ets2.12075.)* `T3`
      - The 2014 professional testing standards list five sources of validity evidence, one of which is evidence based on response processes, defined as "evidence concerning the fit between the construct and the detailed nature of the performance or response actually engaged in by test takers," and note that "documentation of other aspects of performance, like eye movements or response times, may also be relevant to some constructs." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Chapter 1, Washington, DC: AERA.)* `T1`
      - The same standards state directly: "there are many other cases where claims about response processes are not part of the validity argument. In some cases, multiple response processes are available for solving the problems of interest, and the construct of interest is only concerned with whether the problem was solved correctly." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Chapter 1.)* `T1`
      - The same standards also state that "each type of evidence … is not required in all settings. Rather, support is needed for each proposition that underlies a proposed test interpretation for a specified use" (p. 14). *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Chapter 1.)* `T1`
      - A commissioned policy paper notes that because response processes are multi-dimensional, "any single method used for the collection of process data is vulnerable to threats to validity associated with methodological bias and partiality," and recommends triangulation with eye tracking, think-aloud, and video studies to validate interpretive use of log data. *(Maddox, 2023, OECD Education Working Papers No. 286, DOI 10.1787/5d9009ff-en.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The critique has four distinct legs and they compound. **Task-specificity:** low-level features do not mean the same thing on a different item, so a coefficient estimated on one task is not a property of the person. **Non-replication:** the literature is built on single items, small samples, and almost no replication attempts, by the assessment of researchers who work in it. **Overfitting:** a log file supports unbounded feature construction, so anything can be found; the writing-process study is the cleanest demonstration — a feature that reached significance in one sample failed to replicate incrementally in a second from the same design. **The standards do not require it:** the governing professional standards explicitly contemplate that when multiple response processes can produce a correct answer and the construct is about correctness, response-process evidence is simply not part of the validity argument. That last point is the one most often skipped. The burden of proof runs the other way from how it is usually assumed: process data is not presumptively informative and awaiting refutation; it is presumptively irrelevant to the score's validity argument unless a specific proposition in that argument requires it.
    - **Link to source:** [20 years of interactive tasks](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12847); [Writing process incremental value](https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12075); [Byproduct to design factor](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5); [OECD process data](https://doi.org/10.1787/5d9009ff-en)

- **Subcategory 3.6: Game-based versus gamified, and what gameplay telemetry actually measures**
  - **Source:** Landers & Sanchez (2022); Landers, Armstrong, Collmus, Mujcic & Blaik (2022); Auer, Mersy, Marin, Blaik & Landers (2022); Bipp, Wee, Walczok & Hansal (2024); Gomez, Ruipérez-Valiente & García Clemente (2023); Ramos-Villagrasa, Fernández-del-Río & Castro (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A definitional review distinguishes three things routinely conflated: *game-based assessment* is an assessment **method** in which the person participates in a core gameplay loop while trait information is inferred; *gameful design* is a **design strategy** in which assessment professionals use game mechanics to guide decisions when building a new assessment; *gamification* is a **redesign strategy** in which game mechanics are added to an assessment that already exists. Only the first is an assessment method; the other two are (re)design strategies. *(Landers & Sanchez, 2022, *International Journal of Selection and Assessment* 30(1):1–13, DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12376.)* `T2` `[COI: first author has commercial and advisory relationships with game-based assessment vendors, disclosed in related work]`
      - A theory-driven game-based assessment of general cognitive ability showed strong convergence with a latent general-intelligence factor (β = .97) in an academic sample (N = 633), and predicted GPA (r = .16) and, in a small organisational sample (N = 49), supervisory ratings of job performance (r = .29). *(Landers, Armstrong, Collmus, Mujcic & Blaik, 2022, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 107(10):1655–1677, DOI 10.1037/apl0000954.)* `T2` `[COI: study funded by a commercial game-based assessment vendor; the lead author became a compensated affiliate of that vendor; two co-authors were employed by it]`
      - **Directional null in the same study:** unit-weighted composites of the *traditional* test battery showed incremental prediction of GPA beyond the game-based battery, **but not the reverse**. *(Landers et al., 2022, DOI 10.1037/apl0000954.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - The same study found similar adverse impact for the traditional battery and the game-based assessment, and no differential prediction of criteria; reactions were more positive across all measures for the game-based assessment. *(Landers et al., 2022, DOI 10.1037/apl0000954.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - A secondary analysis engineered 65 features from the raw trace data of that same game-based assessment (N = 621) — counts of game objects interacted with, time spent doing so, and mouse-movement data across intervals — and applied a series of machine-learning models. The authors report "promising evidence for trace data modeling of cognitive ability, including incremental contribution to the prediction of a criterion grade point average (GPA), but less promising evidence for trace data modeling of conscientiousness." *(Auer, Mersy, Marin, Blaik & Landers, 2022, *International Journal of Selection and Assessment* 30(1):82–102, DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12363.)* `T2` `[COI: one co-author employed by the commercial vendor that produced the assessment]`
      - The same author team later characterised that result more soberly in a methodological paper: the trace-data models "were unable to predict general cognitive ability at a sufficiently high level to support measurement equivalence (R² = .434)," though they predicted the criterion slightly better than the cognitive-ability measure did — which the authors note could indicate either improved measurement *or* the reflection of additional constructs beyond cognitive ability. *(Landers, Auer, Mersy, Marin & Blaik, 2022, *International Journal of Testing* 22(3–4):243–263, DOI 10.1080/15305058.2022.2134394.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - The same methodological paper states that "trace data generally only provide weak signals in true score variance of target constructs from a vast set of behaviors," and lists error sources that must be isolated: working-memory load from screen size, lag from unreliable internet, erratic mouse hardware, low clicking precision from inexperience with computers and mice, and browser incompatibility. *(Landers et al., 2022, DOI 10.1080/15305058.2022.2134394.)* `T2`
      - A three-level meta-analysis of 52 samples from 44 papers (> 6,100 adult participants) found an overall observed correlation of r = 0.30, 95% CI [0.26, 0.34] between game-related assessment indicators and traditional cognitive-ability measures (corrected r = 0.45), with substantial heterogeneity, Q_E(806) = 3871.42, p < .001. *(Bipp, Wee, Walczok & Hansal, 2024, *Journal of Intelligence* 12(12):129, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - **The most damaging finding in the same meta-analysis:** game-related assessments *intended* to assess cognitive ability did **not** show stronger relationships with traditional cognitive-ability measures than game-related assessments **not** designed to measure cognitive ability. *(Bipp et al., 2024, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - The same meta-analysis reports that its reliability corrections produced correlations above 1.0 in seven cases — because the underlying studies reported reliabilities as low as 0.16–0.20 — which the authors describe as "raising further questions about how to adequately assess reliability in GRAs." *(Bipp et al., 2024, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - The same meta-analysis states plainly: "it remains unclear whether GRAs provide incremental predictive value for academic and work outcomes beyond traditional selection instruments," and notes that only a few studies have examined predictive validity for grades or work performance at all. *(Bipp et al., 2024, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - The first systematic literature review of empirical game-based assessment studies (65 papers) concludes that the field's current limitations indicate future research "would benefit from the use of bigger data samples and more specialized algorithms," and identifies replication and validation problems as open challenges. *(Gomez, Ruipérez-Valiente & García Clemente, 2023, *IEEE Transactions on Learning Technologies* 16(4):500–515, DOI 10.1109/TLT.2022.3226661.)* `T1`
      - A PRISMA systematic review of 34 articles on game-related assessment in personnel selection concludes that such assessments "can be used" but that "the supposed advantages of GRA over conventional tests are fewer than imagined," and that claims of comparable accuracy with better reactions and reduced adverse impact and faking "still lack scientific support." *(Ramos-Villagrasa, Fernández-del-Río & Castro, 2022, *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:952002, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Take the strongest single case in this literature — a game-based assessment built theory-first, published in a top journal, converging at β = .97 with latent g. Even there, when the decisive comparison is run, the *traditional* battery adds incrementally over the game, and the game does not add over the traditional battery. Then widen to the meta-analytic view and the picture gets worse: the average observed correlation with conventional cognitive measures is r ≈ .30, heterogeneity is enormous, some underlying reliabilities are so low (0.16–0.20) that correction formulas break, and — the finding that should stop the conversation — games *designed* to measure cognitive ability correlate with cognitive tests no better than games that were never designed to measure anything. That last result is very hard to reconcile with a claim that gameplay telemetry is measuring the intended trait; it is much more consistent with a broad, weakly construct-specific "does well at digital tasks" factor. Note too the sequencing of the trace-data claim: presented as "promising evidence … including incremental contribution" in the applied journal, and as "unable to predict … at a sufficiently high level to support measurement equivalence" by the same authors in the methods journal. Both are true. Only the second is a validity statement.
    - **Link to source:** [Game-based vs gamified definitions](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12376); [Theory-driven GBA of g](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000954); [Trace data ML](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12363); [You are what you click](https://doi.org/10.1080/15305058.2022.2134394); [GRA meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence12120129); [GBA systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1109/TLT.2022.3226661); [GRA selection review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002)

- **Subcategory 3.7: Stealth assessment — the claim and the actual evidence base, characterised honestly**
  - **Source:** Shute, Wang, Greiff, Zhao & Moore (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The canonical validation study of stealth assessment for problem solving embedded assessment in a lightly modified commercial video game, built a four-facet problem-solving competency model (analysing givens and constraints; planning a solution pathway; using tools effectively; monitoring and evaluating progress), identified in-game indicators for each facet, and implemented the evidence model as Bayesian networks — a unique network per game level, 42 levels in total. The approach is explicitly grounded in evidence-centered design. *(Shute, Wang, Greiff, Zhao & Moore, 2016, *Computers in Human Behavior* 63:106–117, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2` `[COI: authors acknowledge support from a game-based learning organisation and worked with a commercially published entertainment game]`
      - Fifty-five 7th-grade students at one suburban school played the game on a tablet for about three hours over three consecutive days; after exclusions for missing data, **complete data were available from 47 students** (20 male, 27 female). *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`
      - The in-game overall problem-solving estimate correlated significantly with both external measures: r = 0.40, p < .01 with a matrix-reasoning test, and r = 0.41, p < .01 with an external complex-problem-solving measure. *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`
      - At the facet level the pattern was uneven: of the four in-game facets, "tool usage" and "evaluating progress" did **not** correlate significantly with the matrix-reasoning test (r = 0.25 and r = 0.04 respectively), nor with the external complex-problem-solving overall score (r = 0.25 and r = 0.22, both non-significant). *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`
      - Internal consistency of the four-facet stealth assessment was Cronbach's α = 0.76; the external complex-problem-solving measure's rule-application subscale reached only α = 0.43 in this sample, which the authors describe as "less than ideal." *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`
      - The correlation between the overall in-game problem-solving estimate and one of its own constituent facets ("planning a solution") was r = 0.98 — described by the authors as "almost indicating identity." *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`
      - The authors state their own principal methodological limitations: the sample was small (47) and the Bayesian-network parameters were adjusted based on that limited sample, so "the Bayes nets may suffer from an overfitting problem" and "may not fit another sample as well as they fit the data collected from our small sample." *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`
      - The stated next step in the paper was "running a larger validation study." *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Stealth assessment is a genuinely serious methodological proposal — it is the one strand of gameplay telemetry that starts from evidence-centered design rather than post-hoc mining, and its competency-model-to-indicator chain is the right shape. But the evidence base beneath the widely repeated claim is thinner than the claim: a single-site convergent-validity study, n = 47, correlations of r ≈ .40 with external measures, two of four facets not significantly related to either external measure, an overall score essentially collinear (r = .98) with one of its own facets, and an author-acknowledged overfitting risk from tuning networks on that same small sample. Crucially, what was demonstrated is **convergent correlation with existing measures**, not incremental validity — the study does not test whether the gameplay-derived estimate adds anything beyond what the conventional instruments already provide. Every one of these caveats is stated by the authors themselves; the distortion happens downstream, in how the study is cited.

- **Subcategory 3.8: Fairness and construct-irrelevant variance in telemetry — device, input modality, and prior familiarity**
  - **Source:** AERA, APA & NCME (2014); Ohlms, Hohner & Melchers (2025); Davis, Kong, McBride & Morrison (2016); Davis, Morrison, Kong & McBride (2017); Ling (2016); Leutner, Codreanu, Brink & Bitsakis (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The professional testing standards specify that evidence of response processes "can contribute to answering questions about differences in meaning or interpretation of test scores across relevant subgroups," and that process studies involving different subgroups "can assist in determining the extent to which capabilities irrelevant or ancillary to the construct may be differentially influencing test takers' test performance." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Chapter 1.)* `T1`
      - The same standards identify construct-irrelevant variance as a source of potential unfair advantage or disadvantage to subgroups, giving as an example vocabulary more complex than the job requires functioning as construct-irrelevant variance for English-language learners. *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Chapter 1.)* `T1`
      - A study of a cognitive game-based assessment found a positive relationship between test-takers' video-game usage and game-based assessment performance, alongside a *negative* association between video-game usage and school performance. The authors state this "provide[s] empirical support for" the concern that experienced players hold an unfair advantage, and warn that ignoring the bias "could inadvertently disadvantage certain applicants and also impair criterion-related validity by introducing criterion-irrelevant variance." *(Ohlms, Hohner & Melchers, 2025, *Applied Psychology* 74(5), DOI 10.1111/apps.70038.)* `T2`
      - A separate meta-analysis of game-related assessment notes that prior research has already shown age, gender, and prior gaming experience can influence performance on such assessments, and that users "must ensure that GRAs produce psychometrically unbiased outcomes for different applicant groups across different cultural contexts." *(Bipp, Wee, Walczok & Hansal, 2024, *Journal of Intelligence* 12(12):129, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - On device comparability of *scores*: a study of high-school students comparing tablets and computers found no significant differences for math and science; for reading, a small device effect favouring tablets appeared in the middle-to-lower part of the score distribution, driven by increases for male students; no interactions of device with ethnicity were observed. *(Davis, Morrison, Kong & McBride, 2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 36(3):35–45, DOI 10.1111/emip.12158.)* `T2` `[COI: this research team's related device-comparability work was published through a commercial assessment vendor's research programme]`
      - A companion study reports broadly comparable performance across tablets and computers for assessment purposes, and frames the key variable as device "form factor" — the way the person accesses and manipulates content — noting that desktops and laptops share a form factor while touch-screen tablets differ in screen size (typically 7–10 inches) and in input method (finger selection, on-screen or external keyboard). *(Davis, Kong, McBride & Morrison, 2016, *Applied Measurement in Education* 30(1):16–26, DOI 10.1080/08957347.2016.1243538.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - A randomised study assigning 403 8th-graders to desktop, tablet alone, or tablet with external keyboard found no significant difference in reading, math, or writing section scores **or in section response time** across the three conditions; all students had used the devices for six months or longer. *(Ling, 2016, *International Journal of Testing* 16(4):352–377, DOI 10.1080/15305058.2016.1160097.)* `T2` `[COI: author affiliated with a commercial assessment organisation]`
      - A methodological paper on trace data lists screen size (via working-memory load), internet lag, erratic mouse hardware, low clicking precision due to inexperience with computers and mice, and browser incompatibility as identified error sources that must be isolated and mitigated in spatiotemporal telemetry. *(Landers, Auer, Mersy, Marin & Blaik, 2022, *International Journal of Testing* 22(3–4):243–263, DOI 10.1080/15305058.2022.2134394.)* `T2` `[COI: co-authors affiliated with a commercial game-based assessment vendor]`
      - An applied multi-study paper on game-based cognitive-ability assessment in recruitment reports evaluating adverse impact against practical and statistical thresholds and applying an algorithmic "bias penalisation" method during model scoring, while stating that "additional samples are [needed] to validate the generalisability of the Bias Penalisation" approach in other recruitment contexts. *(Leutner, Codreanu, Brink & Bitsakis, 2023, *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:942662, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.942662.)* `T3` `[COI: three of four authors were employed by the commercial assessment vendor whose product is evaluated, per the paper's own conflict-of-interest statement]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There are two separable fairness questions here and the literature answers only one of them. The answered question is whether *scores* are comparable across devices; on this the evidence is reassuring — tablets and computers produce broadly comparable scores for experienced users, with small, localised exceptions. The unanswered question is whether *process indicators* are comparable, and that is a different and harder question, because timing and interaction-count features are functions of input latency, pointer precision, screen real estate (hence scrolling), and hardware reliability in a way that a correct/incorrect outcome is not. One randomised study found no device difference in section-level response time, which is real evidence, but section-level timing is a far coarser quantity than the fine-grained mouse-trajectory and inter-action-interval features that telemetry models actually consume; the vendors' own methodologists enumerate screen size, lag, mouse hardware, and clicking inexperience as error sources they must isolate. Layer on top of this the gameplay-familiarity result — prior video-game usage predicts *better* game-based assessment performance while predicting *worse* school performance — and you have a construct-irrelevant variable that inflates the predictor and deflates the criterion simultaneously, which is the worst possible configuration for both fairness and criterion-related validity. Prior familiarity with the interaction medium is also directly correlated with socioeconomic access to that medium, which the current evidence base does not adequately address.
    - **Link to source:** [Video game usage and GBA bias](https://doi.org/10.1111/apps.70038); [Disaggregated device effects](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12158); [Device comparability](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957347.2016.1243538); [iPad vs desktop](https://doi.org/10.1080/15305058.2016.1160097); [Trace data methodology](https://doi.org/10.1080/15305058.2022.2134394); [GBA in recruitment](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.942662)

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Authors' conclusions frequently outrun their own numbers.** The clearest instance: a study finds process indicators predict an external criterion, then finds those effects drop to non-significance (and reverse sign) once an established ability measure enters the model, and concludes the study "supports the assumption of test-taking behavior actually representing differences in CPS ability — over and above test scores." That conclusion holds against a bare score and fails against a score-plus-ability baseline. The same pattern appears in trace-data work, where the same author team wrote "promising evidence … including incremental contribution" in an applied journal and "unable to predict … at a sufficiently high level to support measurement equivalence" in a methods journal.

2. **The design-first camp and the empirical record contradict each other about what is currently possible.** Evidence-centered design says the claim, the observables, and the tasks must be specified in advance. But the same design-oriented authors state that the theories linking low-level behavioural features to work-process attributes largely do not exist for complex competencies. So the methodology that would make process data interpretable presupposes theory that the field acknowledges it lacks — which means most current work is either doing post-hoc mining under a design-first banner, or is honestly exploratory and should not be cited as validity evidence.

3. **Enthusiasm scales with distance from the incremental-validity test.** Where the test is not run, the literature reports "significant relations," "promising evidence," and "great potential." In the small set of studies where it *is* run — writing process features against product features, game-based batteries against traditional batteries, process indicators against ability measures — the result is null, marginal, or directionally against the process data. This is not a coincidence of which studies happened to be run; it is what you would expect if process features are largely reflections of the same underlying ability that the outcome score already captures.

4. **The professional standards are more permissive of correctness-only measurement than the field's discourse admits.** The governing standards explicitly contemplate that when multiple response processes can produce a correct answer and the construct concerns correctness, response-process evidence is *not part of the validity argument*. Much process-data advocacy proceeds as if collecting process evidence were a validity obligation. It is a conditional obligation, triggered only by specific propositions in a specific interpretive argument.

5. **"Game-based" and "gamified" are formally distinguished in the definitional literature and routinely conflated in the empirical literature.** The meta-analytic corpus pools purpose-built cognitive game-based assessments with commercial entertainment games used opportunistically — and then finds no difference between them in relation to cognitive tests. Either the distinction does not matter empirically (which would undermine the design-theory case) or the pooling is masking real heterogeneity (which would undermine the meta-analytic estimate). Both readings are damaging.

6. **Reliability is treated as a solved problem in advocacy and as an open one in the methods.** A meta-analysis had to discard seven reliability-corrected estimates because they exceeded 1.0, driven by underlying reliabilities as low as .16–.20. A widely cited stealth-assessment validation reports α = .76 for a four-facet model in which the overall score correlates .98 with one facet. There is no settled answer to what "reliability" even means for a score built from performance across heterogeneous mini-games or game levels.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of these is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. They are cross-source connections that the sources do not themselves state.*

- **Seed A — "The successful process indicators are the ones making negative claims."** *Starter hypothesis.* The one process feature with a clean, replicated incremental-validity record (response-time-based disengagement detection) differs from the failures on a specific dimension: its claim is that a response is *uninformative*, not that a behaviour *measures something*. Detecting "this observation should not count" requires only a threshold and a theory of what a non-attempt looks like; measuring "this behaviour indicates planning ability" requires a validated construct-to-behaviour mapping that the field concedes does not exist. If this holds, the actionable rule is that process data earns its place first as a **filter on the evidence** and only much later as **evidence itself** — and a programme that skips the first use to reach for the second is inverting the difficulty ordering. The author should test this against counter-examples: are there any well-replicated *positive* process indicators, and if so what do they share?

- **Seed B — "Process features are mostly a noisy re-measurement of the same construct, and 'incremental' claims usually mean 'incremental over an artificially impoverished baseline.'"** *Starter hypothesis.* Three independent results point the same way: process effects on an external criterion vanish once ability enters the model; the traditional battery adds over the game-based battery but not vice versa; and games designed to measure cognitive ability correlate with cognitive tests no better than games never designed to measure anything. A single explanation covers all three — process features load on the same general factor the outcome score already loads on, plus construct-irrelevant medium-familiarity variance. If so, the diagnostic question for any incremental-validity claim is not "incremental over what?" but "incremental over the *best available conventional alternative*, in a held-out sample." The author should look for a documented case where a process feature survives that specific test, and treat its absence as informative.

- **Seed C — "Construct-irrelevant variance in telemetry is worse than construct-irrelevant variance in scores, because it corrupts predictor and criterion in opposite directions."** *Starter hypothesis.* Device-comparability research is reassuring about *scores* and largely silent about *process indicators*, which are far more sensitive to input latency, pointer precision, and screen real estate than correctness is. Meanwhile, medium familiarity has been found to raise game-based assessment performance while *lowering* the academic criterion. Combined, this suggests telemetry-based measurement may carry a fairness liability that score-based measurement does not, and that the standard device-comparability evidence base does not license extending "scores are comparable across devices" to "process features are comparable across devices." The author should check whether any published study has directly tested measurement invariance of *process indicators* (not scores) across device or input modality — and treat a null result on that search as a substantive finding.

- **Seed D — "The unit of replication in this field is the item, and that is why nothing replicates."** *Starter hypothesis.* Low-level features are acknowledged not to carry the same meaning across tasks; the corpus is built on single items; and the proposed fix — abstracting to higher-level, theory-coded features — currently lacks the theory it requires. This suggests that the field's replication crisis is not primarily a statistical-power or publication-bias problem but a **construct-definition** problem: there is no task-independent definition of the behaviour, so there is nothing for a second study to replicate. If true, the precondition for any process-data programme is not more data or better algorithms but an explicit, task-independent operational definition of the target behaviour written before any task exists — and any programme that cannot state one should expect its features not to transfer. The author should stress-test this by asking whether the response-time-effort case (Seed A) succeeded precisely because "rapid guess" *is* a task-independent definition.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **No verified numeric effect sizes for the flagship international log-file study.** The relationship between the vary-one-thing-at-a-time strategy indicator and problem-solving proficiency is reported qualitatively above because the paper's coefficient tables could not be reached from accessible abstract or repository pages. There is also a structural concern that could not be checked without the full text: the study relates strategy use on one item to *overall* proficiency, and that item plausibly contributes to the proficiency estimate — a part-whole dependency that would inflate the association. This needs the full text to resolve and should not be cited quantitatively until it is.
- **Very few studies run the decisive comparison at all.** Only a handful of the sources above test process features against an external criterion with a conventional measure already in the model. The literature is therefore not "mixed" on incremental validity — it is mostly *silent*, with a small number of results clustering on the null side. Absence of contrary evidence here reflects absence of testing, not confirmation.
- **No evidence located on measurement invariance of process indicators across device or input modality.** Device-comparability research addresses scores. Whether timing, click-count, and trajectory features are invariant across touch versus mouse, screen sizes, or connection quality appears not to have been directly tested at scale. This is a first-order gap given that these are exactly the features telemetry models consume.
- **No evidence located on socioeconomic gradients in telemetry features specifically.** Prior familiarity with the interaction medium is documented as affecting performance; its correlation with socioeconomic access to technology is a reasonable inference but was not verified against a study in this search.
- **Reliability of process-derived scores is unresolved as a methodological matter.** Internal consistency across heterogeneous mini-games or game levels, test–retest stability under practice effects, and generalisability-theory decompositions of process indicators are all identified as open by the meta-analytic and methodological sources, with no settled standard.
- **The literature on learning *rate* from process data was not located.** Everything above concerns estimating ability or proficiency at a point in time. Whether process indicators improve estimation of *change* or *learning rate* — a distinct and harder inferential target — did not surface in this search and should be treated as an open question rather than assumed to follow.
- **Publication bias is plausible and only partly addressed.** The one meta-analysis here identified just five unpublished studies and received no usable data from twenty contacted authors; it also excluded commercially held validation data on intellectual-property grounds. The direction of that second exclusion is unknown: vendor data could be better (proprietary refinement) or worse (unfavourable results never released).
- **Tier-3 sources are load-bearing in two places.** The strongest single negative incremental-validity result (writing process features failing to cross-validate) comes from a commercial assessment organisation's own report series, and the policy-level framing comes from a commissioned working paper by an author with a commercial affiliation. Both are used above because their claims are specific and self-critical, but neither has been through independent peer review.
