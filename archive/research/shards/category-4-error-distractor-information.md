### Category 4: Information in the wrong answer — distractor-level and polytomous scoring

*General idea: A half-century of psychometric work establishes that which wrong option a person selects is not noise — it carries recoverable information about the latent trait, and models exist to extract it. But the measured size of that gain in ability estimation is small (on the order of two to three points of reliability in reasoning tests), it is concentrated at the low end of the ability range, it depends on distractors that were deliberately built to be diagnostic, and it costs substantially more calibration data. The stronger claim — that error patterns support reliable *profile* or *per-skill* reporting — is where the evidence turns negative, and there is a precise, well-established statistical criterion for deciding when a subscore earns the right to be reported at all.*

---

- **Subcategory 4.1: The founding claim — unordered response categories carry trait information**
  - **Source:** Bock (1972); Thissen (1976); Thissen & Steinberg (1984); Thissen, Steinberg & Fitzpatrick (1989)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A multivariate logistic latent-trait model was proposed for items scored in two or more nominal (unordered) categories; it estimates two item parameters for *each* response alternative of a multiple-choice item and provides "recovery of information from 'wrong' responses when estimating latent ability." *(Bock, 1972, *Psychometrika* 37(1):29–51, DOI 10.1007/BF02291411.)* `T2`
      - In that paper's application to twenty vocabulary items, comparing item and test information curves under multiple-category versus dichotomous scoring, "multiple scoring proves substantially more precise for subjects of less than median ability, and about equally precise for subjects above the median." *(Bock, 1972, *Psychometrika* 37(1):29–51, DOI 10.1007/BF02291411.)* `T2`
      - A 1976 paper titled "Information in wrong responses to the Raven Progressive Matrices" applied this class of reasoning to a figural matrix reasoning test. *(Thissen, 1976, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 13(3):201–214, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1976.tb00011.x; bibliographic record verified via publisher metadata.)* `T2` — *the specific numeric results in this paper were not obtained; only its existence, scope, and citation details are verified here.*
      - An earlier short report titled "Information in wrong responses" appears in the primary-source citation trail of both the 1976 matrix-reasoning paper and later error-analysis work, indicating the idea predates the formal nominal model's popularisation. *(Jacobs, 1970, *Psychological Reports* 26(1):311, DOI 10.2466/pr0.1970.26.1.311; verified only as a citation record.)* `T2`
      - An extended multivariate logistic response model specific to multiple-choice items was introduced, subsuming several earlier proposals as special cases, with marginal maximum likelihood estimation. *(Thissen & Steinberg, 1984, *Psychometrika* 49(4):501–519, DOI 10.1007/BF02302588.)* `T2`
      - A companion paper argued the position directly in its title — "the distractors are also part of the item" — and demonstrated parametric and graphical summaries describing each alternative's relationship to the proficiency being measured, applied to pilot mathematics achievement items, for the purposes of detecting flawed items and guiding item design. *(Thissen, Steinberg & Fitzpatrick, 1989, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 26(2):161–176, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1989.tb00326.x.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The claim that wrong answers carry information is not speculative; it was formalised in 1972 and has a working estimator. The original demonstration already contained the shape of the finding that everything since has replicated: the extra precision arrives almost entirely *below* the median of the ability distribution and is roughly nil above it. This is mechanically sensible — high-ability people mostly answer correctly, so there is no wrong answer to read. The founding literature also framed distractor parameters as a tool for *item quality control* at least as much as for scoring, which is a distinct and less demanding use.
    - **Link to source:** [Bock 1972 nominal response model](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02291411)

- **Subcategory 4.2: Ordered-category (polytomous) models and the information gain from graded scoring**
  - **Source:** Samejima (1969); Masters (1982); Muraki (1992); Rodriguez (2005)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The graded response model was set out for the case where item responses are classified into more than two ordered categories; the monograph's stated purposes included comparing graded-score estimators against conventional dichotomous scoring "in terms of the amounts of information, the standard errors of estimators, and the mean-square errors of estimators." *(Samejima, 1969, *Psychometrika Monograph Supplement No. 17*, published as *Psychometrika* 34(S1):1–97, DOI 10.1007/BF03372160.)* `T2`
      - The Partial Credit model was developed as a unidimensional latent-trait model for responses in two or more ordered categories, extending the Rating Scale model so that the number and structure of response alternatives may vary from item to item while retaining parameter separability. *(Masters, 1982, *Psychometrika* 47(2):149–174, DOI 10.1007/BF02296272.)* `T2`
      - The Generalized Partial Credit model added a varying slope (discrimination) parameter per item; fitted to National Assessment of Educational Progress mathematics data containing both dichotomous and polytomous items, the varying-slope version yielded better fit to the data than the constant-slope Partial Credit model. *(Muraki, 1992, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 16(2):159–176, DOI 10.1177/014662169201600206.)* `T2` `[COI: author affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation, which also published a parallel technical-report version, DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.1992.tb01436.x]`
      - A random-effects meta-analysis synthesising roughly 80 years of research on multiple-choice option counts reported the following average effects on reliability: moving from 5-option to 4-option items reduces reliability by .035; moving from 5- to 3-option items does not affect reliability on average; moving from 4- to 3-option items increases reliability slightly, by .02; moving from 5- or 4-option to 2-option items reduces reliability by .11 and .09 respectively. *(Rodriguez, 2005, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 24(2):3–13, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2005.00006.x.)* `T1`
      - The same meta-analysis reported that *how* distractors are removed matters: when 3-option items are created by eliminating distractors at random, reliability drops .06 on average, "with no change if ineffective distractors are deleted." *(Rodriguez, 2005, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 24(2):3–13, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2005.00006.x.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Ordered-category models are mature, well-specified, and demonstrably fit real polytomous data better than their constrained cousins — but "fits better" is a statement about the model, not about how much more you learn per test-taker. The one genuinely meta-analytic magnitude in this space points the opposite way from naive intuition: the marginal distractor buys you very little reliability. Going from five options to three costs essentially nothing on average, and the *only* substantial reliability penalty appears when you cut all the way down to two options or delete functioning distractors indiscriminately. The operative variable is distractor *quality*, not distractor *count* — which is exactly the condition on which distractor-level scoring depends.
    - **Link to source:** [Rodriguez 2005 meta-analysis of option counts](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2005.00006.x)

- **Subcategory 4.3: Distractor-level scoring in reasoning items — the actual measured magnitudes, and a serious negative result**
  - **Source:** Suh & Bolt (2010); Myszkowski & Storme (2018); Storme, Myszkowski, Baron & Bernard (2019); Bolt, Wollack & Suh (2012); García-Pérez (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Nested logit models were introduced for multiple-choice data, modelling a two-stage process in which a binary logistic model governs whether the item is solved and, conditional on not solving it, a nominal response model governs which distractor is chosen; the models permit collapsibility across distractor categories so distractor information can be included or excluded item-by-item without altering the form of the correct-response curve. *(Suh & Bolt, 2010, *Psychometrika* 75(3):454–473, DOI 10.1007/s11336-010-9163-7.)* `T2`
      - In a sample of 499 undergraduates taking the twelve-item last series of a standard figural matrices test, binary IRT gave good empirical reliability (r<sub>xx′</sub> = 0.843 under a 3PL model); moving to nested logit models that recover distractor information gave a reliability gain of Δr<sub>xx′</sub> = 0.029, bootstrapped 95% CI [0.019, 0.036], z = 6.06, p < .001. *(Myszkowski & Storme, 2018, *Intelligence* 68:109–116, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.03.010.)* `T2`
      - A conceptual replication in a higher-stakes online setting with 2,949 job applicants on a twenty-item logical series test found binary marginal empirical reliabilities of 0.833 (1PL), 0.849 (2PL), 0.868 (3PL) and 0.873 (4PL), against nominal-model reliabilities of 0.857 (nominal response), 0.867 (2PNL), 0.887 (3PNL) and 0.888 (4PNL). *(Storme, Myszkowski, Baron & Bernard, 2019, *Journal of Intelligence* 7(3):17, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence7030017.)* `T2` `[COI: two of four authors were affiliated with a commercial assessment vendor whose product supplied the data]`
      - In that replication the reliability gains from nested logit over the matched binary model were 0.018 (95% CI [0.017, 0.021], z = 17.765) for 2PL→2PNL, 0.019 (95% CI [0.018, 0.023], z = 15.265) for 3PL→3PNL, and 0.015 (95% CI [0.011, 0.020], z = 6.669) for 4PL→4PNL; the authors report that "the gain in reliability was especially obtained at low ability levels." *(Storme et al., 2019, *Journal of Intelligence* 7(3):17, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence7030017.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - A multidimensional extension of the nested logit model was developed specifically because of the concern "that distractor selection reflects a different trait/ability than that underlying the correct response," and was applied to a university testing centre's decision about whether to incorporate distractor selection into scoring. *(Bolt, Wollack & Suh, 2012, *Psychometrika* 77(2):339–357, DOI 10.1007/s11336-012-9257-5.)* `T2`
      - **Negative finding.** An empirical-plus-simulation study comparing polytomous (nominal response and multiple-choice model) against dichotomous (3PL) characterisations of the same test found apparently superior item and test information functions from the polytomous models, then concluded on closer inspection that "these outcomes are artifactual"; two simulation studies confirmed a "structural inadequacy of the nominal response model for multiple-choice items," and found that the multiple-choice model outperformed the 3PL "only when distractor endorsement frequencies vary non-monotonically with ability, although this feature is rarely observed in empirical data sets." *(García-Pérez, 2014, *The Spanish Journal of Psychology* 17:E88, DOI 10.1017/sjp.2014.95.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the best-quantified corner of the category, and the honest headline is *real but small*. Two independent studies on matrix/series reasoning, one low-stakes and one high-stakes, one small and one large, converge on a reliability gain of roughly 0.015 to 0.03 — about two to three points on a scale already sitting in the 0.84–0.87 range. That is a genuine, statistically robust effect and it is not nothing on a short test, but it is far from the transformative gain the phrase "recovering information from wrong answers" suggests. Both studies also agree the gain lives at the low-ability end, which is where the model has wrong answers to read. Against this sit two substantial cautions: a formal argument that apparent information gains from polytomous scoring of multiple-choice items can be an artifact of model misspecification and only become real under a distractor pattern that is empirically rare; and a demonstration that distractor choice may load on a *different* dimension than the correct response, meaning distractor scoring can quietly change what the test measures rather than measuring the same thing better.
    - **Link to source:** [Storme et al. 2019 replication with measured reliability gains](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence7030017)

- **Subcategory 4.4: Diagnostic classification models — attribute-level inference from error patterns, and whether those classifications are reliable enough to act on**
  - **Source:** de la Torre (2009); Templin & Bradshaw (2013); Johnson & Sinharay (2020); Sinharay & Haberman (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A cognitive diagnosis model for multiple-choice options was proposed on the explicit premise that "the dichotomization approach to the analysis of multiple-choice data ignores the potential diagnostic information that can be found in the distractors and is therefore deemed diagnostically suboptimal"; the framework requires that options be *constructed* to be cognitively diagnostic (an extended Q-matrix mapping attributes to each option, not only the key), and a simulation study evaluated the model's viability. *(de la Torre, 2009, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 33(3):163–183, DOI 10.1177/0146621608320523.)* `T2`
      - A reliability measure for the categorical latent variables of diagnostic classification models was defined; using theory- and simulation-based results the authors showed that these models "uniformly provide greater examinee estimate reliability than IRT models for tests of the same length," and attributed this explicitly to "the smaller range of latent variable values examinee estimates can take" in a classification model. *(Templin & Bradshaw, 2013, *Journal of Classification* 30(2):251–275, DOI 10.1007/s00357-013-9129-4.)* `T2`
      - The same paper opens by noting that "despite their use, the reliability of examinee estimates in diagnostic classification model applications has seldom been reported." *(Templin & Bradshaw, 2013, *Journal of Classification* 30(2):251–275, DOI 10.1007/s00357-013-9129-4.)* `T2`
      - Three further measures of the reliability of posterior skill-mastery means were subsequently proposed, on the stated grounds that "as with any assessment, it is important to derive and report estimates of the reliability of the reported scores"; the authors recommended two of the measures for future use. *(Johnson & Sinharay, 2020, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 45(1):5–31, DOI 10.3102/1076998619864550.)* `T2` `[COI: one author affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - **Negative/critical finding.** A commentary in a peer-reviewed measurement journal responding to a major review of diagnostic classification models was titled, and framed around, the question "How much can we reliably know about what examinees know?" *(Sinharay & Haberman, 2009, *Measurement: Interdisciplinary Research and Perspectives* 7(1):46–49, DOI 10.1080/15366360802715486.)* `T2` `[COI: both authors affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - **Methodological caution on the reliability metric itself.** A review of diagnostic classification model practice reports the worked example that if 90% of a population possesses an attribute and the fitted model "provides absolutely no information about the attribute," a classification-accuracy index will nonetheless report accuracy for 90% of the population — i.e. raw classification accuracy is confounded with base rate. The same review notes that a widely used software routine for classification accuracy gave "substantially" different results from published indices and, at time of writing, lacked supporting peer-reviewed publication. *(Cambridge Assessment research report, "Cognitive Diagnostic Models and how they can be useful," summarising Sinharay & Johnson, 2019, pp. 369, 371.)* `T3` `[COI: published by an examination board]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two things are true at once here and they are easy to conflate. First, the models work: given options deliberately engineered so that each distractor implies a specific attribute deficit, a diagnostic classification model can extract attribute-level classifications that a right/wrong model cannot, and it will do so with a *higher* nominal reliability than a continuous-trait model of the same test length. Second, that reliability advantage is partly an artifact of the question being easier — you are asked to sort people into two or a few boxes rather than place them on a continuum, so a coarser answer is naturally more repeatable. That is a real gain in decision consistency, not a gain in information. The field's own reliability metrics are contested; attribute-level classification accuracy is inflated by skewed base rates, and the leading authors concede that these reliabilities were rarely reported at all in the model's first decade of use. Crucially, the diagnostic payoff is contingent on distractors being *designed* against an attribute map beforehand — retrofitting a diagnostic model onto distractors written to be merely plausible is not the same procedure.
    - **Link to source:** [Templin & Bradshaw 2013 on DCM reliability](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00357-013-9129-4)

- **Subcategory 4.5: Subscores and profile reporting — a precise criterion for added value, and how often real tests fail it**
  - **Source:** Haberman (2008); Sinharay (2010); Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman (2011); Sinharay (2010, review); AERA/APA/NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The criterion, stated precisely: define the proportional reduction in mean squared error (PRMSE) in estimating the *true* subscore, relative to a trivial predictor. Let PRMSE<sub>s</sub> be the PRMSE of the observed subscore and PRMSE<sub>x</sub> that of the observed total score. A subscore "provides added value over the total score" **if and only if PRMSE<sub>s</sub> > PRMSE<sub>x</sub>** — that is, if and only if the observed subscore beats the observed total score at estimating the true subscore in mean squared error. PRMSE<sub>s</sub> is exactly equal to the reliability of the subscore. *(Haberman, 2008, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 33(2):204–229, DOI 10.3102/1076998607302636, as restated in Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman, 2011, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 30(3):29–40, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00208.x.)* `T2` / `T1` `[COI: all authors affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - The originating paper states the two joint conditions under which observed subscores have value: "observed subscores are most likely to have value if they have relatively high reliability by themselves and if the true subscore and true total score have only a moderate correlation. Both conditions are important." *(Haberman, 2008, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 33(2):204–229, DOI 10.3102/1076998607302636.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - An augmented subscore — a weighted combination of subscore and total score — has PRMSE at least as large as either alone by construction, and is therefore recommended only when the increase over both is substantial. *(Haberman, 2008, DOI 10.3102/1076998607302636; operationalised as roughly a 10% increase over the better of the two in a companion survey.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - **Negative finding, operational scale.** Across data from 25 operational tests, 16 had *no* subscores with added value by this criterion, "even though several of them report subscores operationally." Among the remaining nine — all of which averaged at least 24 items per subscore — only *some* subscores had added value. *(Sinharay, 2010, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 47(2):150–174, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2010.00106.x, as summarised in Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman, 2011, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00208.x.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - The accompanying simulation study found that to have added value a subscore must (a) be based on a sufficient number of items, roughly 20, and (b) be sufficiently distinct — the disattenuated correlation between subscores must be less than about .85. *(Sinharay, 2010, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2010.00106.x.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - Subscores composed of 10 items were reported to have no added value "even for a realistically extreme (low) disattenuated correlation of 0.70." *(Sinharay, 2010, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2010.00106.x, as quoted in secondary summaries.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - **The incremental-validity bound.** Separately from mean-squared-error added value, there is a hard ceiling on how much a subscore can add to prediction of an external criterion: incremental validity, measured as the difference in coefficient of determination between predicting from total-plus-subscore versus total alone, "is no greater than the reliability of the residual from linear prediction of the subscore by the total score." *(Haberman, 2008, "Subscores and Validity," *ETS Research Report Series*, DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.2008.tb02150.x.)* `T3` `[COI: test-publisher technical report]`
      - **Professional standard.** Standard 1.14: "When interpretation of subscores, score differences, or profiles is suggested, the rationale and relevant evidence in support of such interpretation should be provided." Its comment: "When a test provides more than one score, the distinctiveness and reliability of the separate scores should be demonstrated, and the interrelationship of those scores should be shown to be consistent with the construct(s) being measured" (p. 27). Standard 2.3: "For each total score, subscore, or combination of scores that is to be interpreted, estimates of relevant indices of reliability/precision should be reported" (p. 43). *(American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association & National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Washington, DC: AERA.)* `T1`
      - A critical review of diagnostic score reporting demonstrated "using results from operational and simulated data, that diagnostic scores have to be based on a sufficient number of items and have to be sufficiently distinct from each other to be worth reporting and that several operationally reported subscores are actually not worth reporting." *(Sinharay, 2010, *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 45(3):553–573, DOI 10.1080/00273171.2010.483382.)* `T2` `[COI: author affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - An instructional module on the topic states plainly that "a psychometric model cannot extract something that is not there," and that when factor analysis reveals a single factor, no amount of modelling will produce defensible per-domain scores. *(Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman, 2011, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 30(3):29–40, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00208.x.)* `T1` `[COI: as above]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This subcategory contains the single most useful thing in the whole category, because it converts a vague design intuition into a decidable test. The question "should I report this breakdown?" has a formal answer: report it only if the sub-part predicts its own true value better than the whole test does. That test is cheap to compute and brutally strict. When it was applied to 25 real operational tests, roughly two-thirds had no defensible subscore at all — including tests that were shipping subscores to users. The practical thresholds that emerge are concrete: about 20 items minimum per reported sub-part, and disattenuated correlations among the parts below roughly .85. Ten-item sub-parts fail even under generous assumptions. Note the failure mode is not "the model can't estimate it" — the model estimates it fine. The failure is that the estimate is worse than just using the total score, which means reporting it actively misinforms. And there is a second, independent ceiling: however much a sub-part might *appear* to add to predicting anything external, it cannot exceed the reliability of the part of the sub-part that the total score does not already explain — which for correlated sub-parts is very little. The professional standards make demonstrating distinctness and reliability a *precondition* for reporting, not an afterthought.
    - **Link to source:** [Haberman 2008, "When can subscores have value?"](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998607302636)

- **Subcategory 4.6: Error taxonomies in matrix and figural reasoning — is error type coherent, stable, and predictive?**
  - **Source:** Kunda, Soulières, Rozga & Goel (2016); Vodegel Matzen, van der Molen & Dudink (1994); Gonthier & Roulin (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The published taxonomy of conceptual error types on matrix reasoning tests, synthesised from test manuals, has four categories: **Repetition** (the chosen distractor copies a matrix entry adjacent to the blank space), **Difference** (the distractor is qualitatively distinct from the alternatives, e.g. blank or containing elements found nowhere in the matrix), **Wrong Principle** (the distractor combines matrix elements according to an incorrect rule), and **Incomplete Correlate** (the distractor closely approximates the correct answer but does not fully account for problem details). *(Kunda, Soulières, Rozga & Goel, 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004, synthesising Raven et al., 2003 manuals.)* `T2` `[COI: the underlying error taxonomy originates in commercially published test manuals]`
      - **Coding reliability problem.** An earlier attempt to apply the taxonomy from one form of the test to sets C through E of another form found inter-rater reliability between two coders "to be only around 70%"; the authors concluded classification of those distractors was "problematic," as no explicit methodology for constructing the distractors was apparent in the test or its literature. *(Vodegel Matzen, van der Molen & Dudink, 1994, *Personality and Individual Differences* 16(3):433–445, DOI 10.1016/0191-8869(94)90070-1, as reported in Kunda et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2`
      - The 2016 paper's stated motivating problem was that "there is no established classification of conceptual error types" for the middle form of the test, and that distractors are frequently ambiguous between categories — one worked example is a distractor readable either as Repetition (it duplicates the entry above the blank) or as Incomplete Correlate (it differs from the key by a single missing row). The authors' response was to reorganise the four types into two *orthogonal* classifications rather than one four-way partition. *(Kunda et al., 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2`
      - The authors' review table of prior error-analysis studies records mixed and sometimes contradictory findings across studies, including at least one reporting that "patterns of error types [were] not related to total set score," and another reporting that the proportion of Incomplete Correlate errors correlated with score in lower-scoring groups but not in higher-scoring groups. *(Kunda et al., 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004, review table.)* `T2` — *these are secondhand summaries of primary studies not independently retrieved here.*
      - The same review table reports a study of 818 adults on the advanced form finding that "medium and high-ability participants make more Incomplete Correlate errors than other types" while "low-ability participants do not show an error type preference." *(Babcock, 2002, as summarised in Kunda et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2` — *secondhand; the primary source was not retrieved or verified.*
      - The authors warn of a systematic artifact: "many previous analyses of error types did not correct for the baseline distributions of different error types in the test itself" — i.e. if a test simply contains more Repetition distractors, test-takers will make more Repetition errors regardless of strategy. *(Kunda et al., 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2`
      - **Within-person instability.** A study of intraindividual strategy shifts in matrix reasoning found that individuals change solution strategy *within* a single test session, with the shift depending on working memory capacity and need for cognition. *(Gonthier & Roulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(3):564–579, DOI 10.1037/xge0000660.)* `T2`
      - Independent context, converging: across eye-movement, self-report, and latent-class strategy studies, "constructive matching" (building the correct solution) indicators correlate positively with cognitive ability, while "response elimination" indicators (time on options, back-and-forth gaze between matrix and options) correlate negatively with performance; both strategies can be used on the same item. *(Forthmann, Förster, Schütze, Hebbecker, Flessner, Peters & Souvignier, 2020, *Journal of Intelligence* 8(1):11, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011, reviewing Bethell-Fox et al. 1984; Vigneau et al. 2006; Hayes et al. 2011; Gonthier & Roulin 2019.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Error taxonomies for figural reasoning exist, come from the test manuals themselves, and have obvious face validity — a "nearly right" error is intuitively different from a "copied the box above" error. But the empirical foundation is much weaker than the taxonomy's confident four-category structure implies. Two trained coders agreed only about 70% of the time on which category a distractor belonged to, and the categories are not even logically disjoint: a single distractor can legitimately satisfy two definitions at once. Findings across studies conflict, and a large fraction of published error-pattern results are contaminated by a baseline artifact — nobody was correcting for how many distractors of each type the test happened to contain. Worse for the within-person stability question: people demonstrably switch strategies mid-test, and which strategy they use is itself ability-linked, so error type is not a stable trait-like signature but a moving, ability-dependent quantity. The one pattern with some cross-study consistency is directional rather than diagnostic: as ability rises, errors shift toward "close to correct" and away from "attending to the wrong thing." That is broadly the same information the nested-logit models are extracting numerically — which is reassuring for convergent validity, and also a reason to doubt that a hand-built error taxonomy adds much beyond what a fitted distractor model already captures.
    - **Link to source:** [Kunda et al. 2016 on matrix reasoning error patterns](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004)

- **Subcategory 4.7: Costs — calibration data requirements, parameter instability, and the item-writing burden**
  - **Source:** DeMars (2003); Forthmann et al. (2020); Storme et al. (2019); Gierl, Bulut, Guo & Zhang (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a study manipulating sample size, number of items, and number of categories as separate factors for nominal response model calibration: "Increasing the number of items had little effect on item parameter recovery, but increasing the number of categories increased the error variance of the parameter estimates. Error variance was also greater for more highly discriminating items and for skewed distributions of ability." *(DeMars, 2003, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 27(4):275–288, DOI 10.1177/0146621603027004003.)* `T2`
      - Prior work cited in that study had suggested "the ratio of the sample size to the total number of item parameters was a key factor" — a nominal model estimates two parameters per response alternative, so parameter count scales with options, not items. *(DeMars, 2003, DOI 10.1177/0146621603027004003, citing De Ayala & Sava-Bolesta, 1999.)* `T2`
      - A simulation study aimed at pilot-stage item selection concluded: "it is not generally recommended to use sample sizes of N = 100 for the detection of discriminatory distractors. Based on type-I-error findings, the sample size should be at least N = 200 when items include three distractors. When items include seven distractors, however, sample sizes below N = 500 cannot be recommended without further considerations, due to unacceptable type-I-error rates." *(Forthmann et al., 2020, *Journal of Intelligence* 8(1):11, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011.)* `T2`
      - The same authors state that "direct use of nested logit models with small sample sizes (e.g., N = 200) seems not feasible" given the models' parameter demands — the motivation for their proposing cheaper screening statistics instead. *(Forthmann et al., 2020, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011.)* `T2`
      - Distractors chosen by fewer than 5% of participants are conventionally treated as non-functioning; in that simulation the proportion of distractors falling below the 5% threshold *increased* as true distractor discrimination increased (9.62%, 10.34%, 15.56%, and 21.33% across four increasing levels of nominal-model discrimination), producing missing effect-size estimates. *(Forthmann et al., 2020, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011.)* `T2`
      - **Observed instability in real data.** Even with 2,949 respondents, "the information matrix of the 4PNL model could not be inverted in order to compute the parameter standard errors, which may be a sign that the estimates were unstable"; the authors recommended the more parsimonious two- and three-parameter versions instead. *(Storme et al., 2019, *Journal of Intelligence* 7(3):17, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence7030017.)* `T2` `[COI: as in 4.3]`
      - A comprehensive review of the distractor literature concluded that "despite a vast body of literature on multiple-choice testing, the task of creating distractors has received much less attention," and identified distractor development and quality evaluation as areas where further research is needed. *(Gierl, Bulut, Guo & Zhang, 2017, *Review of Educational Research* 87(6):1082–1116, DOI 10.3102/0034654317726529.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Distractor-level modelling has a cost structure that scales badly in exactly the direction people want to push it. Adding response categories — the whole point of the exercise — directly inflates parameter error variance, and the binding constraint is the ratio of sample size to *parameters*, which grows with options per item rather than with items. Concrete floors from the literature: at least 200 respondents to even *detect* whether a three-option item's distractors are informative, at least 500 for a seven-option item, and considerably more to actually fit the scoring model rather than screen for it. There is a nasty second-order trap: the better a distractor discriminates, the fewer low-ability people choose it, so the most informative distractors are also the ones most likely to fall below the minimum-frequency threshold and drop out of estimation. And instability is not hypothetical — the richest model in a study with nearly three thousand respondents failed to produce standard errors at all. Finally, the whole approach presupposes distractors written to be diagnostic, and the field's own review says that is the least-studied part of item writing.
    - **Link to source:** [DeMars 2003 on sample size and nominal model parameter recovery](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621603027004003)

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **"The model fits better" versus "the estimate is more precise" versus "the estimate is reliable enough to report."** These are three separate milestones and the literature conflates them constantly. Generalized Partial Credit fits polytomous data better than Partial Credit (Muraki, 1992) — that is a model-selection fact and says nothing about how much you learn per person. Nested logit models raise reliability by ~0.02 (Storme et al., 2019) — that is a precision fact. Whether a per-domain or per-error-type score clears Haberman's PRMSE bar is a third question entirely, and it is the one that determines whether you may put a number in front of a person.

2. **Direct contradiction on whether distractor information is real or artifactual.** Bock (1972), Myszkowski & Storme (2018), and Storme et al. (2019) report genuine information gains from distractor scoring. García-Pérez (2014) argues that apparent information gains from polytomous characterisations of multiple-choice items are artifacts of model misspecification, identifies a "structural inadequacy of the nominal response model for multiple-choice items," and finds real gains only under a distractor pattern that is "rarely observed in empirical data sets." Notably, the nested logit family that produced the positive reasoning-test results was *designed* to fix the nominal model's structural problem with multiple-choice items (Suh & Bolt, 2010), and in the large replication the nested models did outperform the plain nominal model — so the two positions may be partially reconcilable, but nobody in this reading has reconciled them explicitly.

3. **Diagnostic models report *higher* reliability while the same research community says diagnostic scores are usually not reportable.** Templin & Bradshaw (2013) show classification models beat continuous models on reliability at equal test length — and attribute this to the coarser answer space. Sinharay and colleagues, across a decade, show that fine-grained diagnostic reporting usually fails an added-value test. These are not actually in conflict, but they read as if they are, and the resolution matters: coarsening the question raises repeatability without raising information.

4. **The meta-analytic evidence on distractor count cuts against distractor-heavy designs.** Rodriguez (2005) finds going from five options to three costs essentially nothing in reliability. Yet distractor-level scoring becomes more attractive with *more* distractors (more categories to read information from) and simultaneously more expensive to calibrate (Forthmann et al., 2020: N ≥ 500 for seven distractors). The count that is optimal for conventional scoring is not obviously the count that is optimal for distractor-level scoring, and no source found here resolves this trade-off directly.

5. **Error type is treated as a stable person characteristic in the taxonomy literature and as a labile within-session variable in the strategy literature.** Gonthier & Roulin (2020) document intraindividual strategy shifts within a single test; the error-taxonomy tradition mostly reports group-level error-type proportions as if they characterised persons. Only about 70% inter-rater agreement on the coding itself (Vodegel Matzen et al., 1994) puts a low ceiling on how stable any person-level error signature could be measured to be.

6. **A conflict of interest pattern worth naming.** A large fraction of the subscore-skepticism literature comes from researchers at one large testing organisation, and a large fraction of the distractor-scoring optimism in applied settings comes from vendors selling short assessments. Both bodies of work are peer-reviewed and internally rigorous, and in this case the incentives arguably run *against* the findings in the skeptical case (a test publisher arguing its own subscores are not reportable). But the asymmetry in who studies what is real.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own — a connection between sources that looks load-bearing but has not been established by any single study cited here.*

- **Seed A — "Distractor information is a low-ability subsidy, not a general precision gain."** Bock (1972) reported the gain was substantial below the median and nil above it. Myszkowski & Storme (2018) and Storme et al. (2019) independently report the gain concentrated at low ability. This is mechanically forced: high scorers produce few wrong answers, so there is nothing to read. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* if the decision being supported is a selection decision at the top of the distribution, distractor-level scoring buys close to nothing, and the ~0.02 average reliability gain is a misleading summary because it averages a real gain at the bottom with zero at the top. The corollary — that distractor scoring is most valuable precisely where you least need precision for a top-end decision, and most valuable where you *do* need it for a placement or support decision — needs to be tested against the conditional reliability curves in the source papers rather than assumed.

- **Seed B — "Distractor quality, not distractor quantity or model sophistication, is the actual limiting reagent."** Rodriguez's (2005) meta-analysis found reliability drops .06 when 3-option items are made by deleting random distractors but is unchanged when ineffective distractors are deleted. de la Torre (2009) requires options *constructed* against an attribute map. Gierl et al. (2017) report distractor creation is the least-studied part of the whole multiple-choice literature. Forthmann et al. (2020) had to invent screening statistics because the models themselves are too data-hungry for pilot samples. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* every reported success in this category rests on distractors that were engineered to be diagnostic, and the modelling machinery is downstream of an item-writing practice that is under-specified in the literature. If that is right, then "should I model distractors?" is the wrong first question and "can I write distractors that map to distinguishable error mechanisms?" is the right one — and the answer to the second determines whether the first is even worth asking.

- **Seed C — "Haberman's criterion is a general firewall against any fine-grained reporting, not just against subscores."** The PRMSE test asks whether a part-score predicts its own true value better than the whole score does, and 16 of 25 operational tests failed it outright (Sinharay, 2010), with roughly 20 items and disattenuated correlations below .85 as necessary conditions. Attribute-level classifications from diagnostic models, per-error-type profiles from an error taxonomy, and per-domain subscores are all structurally the same object: a claim that a sub-part of the evidence carries information the whole does not. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* the added-value criterion — or its diagnostic-model analogues (Johnson & Sinharay, 2020) — should be applied to *any* proposed fine-grained readout, not only to number-correct subscores, and doing so would likely disqualify most error-type profile reporting on short instruments. Testing this requires checking whether PRMSE-style reasoning has actually been formalised for categorical attribute estimates or whether that is an extrapolation.

- **Seed D — "There may be a dimensionality tax hiding inside the reliability gain."** Bolt, Wollack & Suh (2012) built a multidimensional nested logit model specifically because distractor selection may reflect a *different* trait than the correct response. Forthmann et al. (2020) summarise evidence that response-elimination strategy indicators correlate *negatively* with ability while constructive-matching indicators correlate positively — so the behaviour that generates an informative distractor choice is itself strategy-dependent, and strategy shifts within a session (Gonthier & Roulin, 2020). *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* part of the ~0.02 reliability gain from distractor scoring may be reliability of a *second* construct (strategy or test-taking approach) being folded into a single score, which would raise internal consistency while degrading construct validity. Reliability going up is not evidence against this — it is exactly what adding a coherent second dimension would do. Whether any of the cited studies tested for it is the thing to check.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **Assumption, unverified:** that the two reasoning-test replications of the nested-logit reliability gain generalise beyond figural matrices and logical series. Both cited studies used those two item families; both were run by an overlapping author team; one had commercial involvement. No independent third replication was located.
- **Assumption, unverified:** that the ~0.015–0.03 reliability gain translates into any change in a real decision. None of the located studies reported downstream decision consistency, classification accuracy at a cut score, or criterion validity — only reliability. A reliability increase of that size may or may not move a single selection or placement outcome.
- **Gap:** no meta-analysis or systematic review of distractor-level *scoring* gains was located. Rodriguez (2005) meta-analyses option *counts*, and Gierl et al. (2017) comprehensively reviews distractor development and analysis, but neither pools effect sizes for the reliability benefit of scoring distractors. The magnitudes reported above therefore rest on individual primary studies.
- **Gap:** the substantive numeric findings of the 1976 matrix-reasoning wrong-response paper were not obtained. Its title, journal, volume, pages, author, and DOI are verified via publisher metadata; the content is not. Any claim about what it found should be sourced directly before use.
- **Gap:** the specific attribute-classification accuracy figures from the 2009 diagnostic multiple-choice model simulation are widely repeated but were located here only in a conference slide deck and a doctoral dissertation, not in the peer-reviewed article itself. The article's own claim of improved classification accuracy over the dichotomous baseline is verified from its abstract; the percentages are not, and are deliberately omitted above.
- **Gap:** the Babcock (2002) finding on ability-graded error-type preferences, and several other entries in the error-taxonomy review table, are reported here at second hand from a 2016 review. Primary sources were not retrieved.
- **Gap:** no evidence was located on the *test–retest stability of error type within a person* — the direct question of whether someone who makes "nearly right" errors today makes them next month. The strategy-shift literature implies instability but measures strategy, not error type. This is the single most consequential missing piece for any claim that error type is a person-level characteristic.
- **Gap:** no evidence was located on whether error-type composition predicts *learning rate* or *change over time*, as distinct from predicting concurrent ability. Everything located in this category concerns static ability estimation. The organising question's second half — learning rate — is not addressed by this literature at all, and that absence is itself a finding.
- **Assumption, unverified:** that the AERA/APA/NCME 2014 page references (Standard 1.14 at p. 27, Standard 2.3 at p. 43) are correct. These were taken from three independent secondary sources that agree with each other and quote identical wording, but the primary volume's pagination was not directly confirmed.
- **Conflict-of-interest caveat carried throughout:** a substantial share of the subscore and diagnostic-score literature originates with a single large testing organisation, and the applied distractor-scoring replication had commercial vendor co-authorship. Marked inline where applicable.
