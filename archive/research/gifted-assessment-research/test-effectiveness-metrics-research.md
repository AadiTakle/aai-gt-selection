## Findings

### Wave 1 · A1 — IRT / adaptive-testing measurement precision at the gifted tail (2026-07-22)

*Scope: metrics that quantify how precisely a test measures near/above a high cut, and how to certify measurement of the top 1–2%. Labels: [VERIFIED] DOI/URL resolved this pass; [SECONDARY]; [INFERENCE]; [PUB/COI].*

1. **Item Information Function (Fisher info), closed forms.** General (Birnbaum): `I_i(θ)=[P_i'(θ)]²/[P_i(θ)Q_i(θ)]`. **2PL:** `I_i(θ)=a_i²·P_iQ_i`, max `a_i²/4` at θ=b_i. **1PL/Rasch:** `P_iQ_i`, max 0.25, differs only by location. **3PL:** `a_i²·(Q_i/P_i)·[(P_i−c_i)/(1−c_i)]²`, always ≤ the 2PL value (guessing destroys information). *Quantifies conditional precision at each θ — the native tool for "how well do we measure a child at the gifted cut."* Assumes correct model, unidimensionality, local independence, known params. — Baker & Kim (2017), *Basics of IRT Using R*, Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-54205-8 [VERIFIED, ch. 6 read]; Birnbaum (1968) in Lord & Novick [VERIFIED via refs].
2. **Test Information Function (TIF): additivity.** `I(θ)=Σ_j I_j(θ)`. *Adding items raises precision only where those items carry information; mid-difficulty items don't help the tail.* For a **selection/scholarship** decision the ideal TIF is **peaked at the cut**, not flat (Baker). — Baker & Kim (2017) Eq. 6.2, §6.6 [VERIFIED].
3. **Conditional SEM: `SE(θ)=1/√I(θ)`.** IRT error is conditional on θ (unlike CTT's single SEM). *Balloons in the tail where info is small → widens each gifted child's true-score band at the decision line.* Worked: I=2.383→SE=0.648; at θ=±2, I≈1→SE≈1. **Key law: I=1/SE² → halving SE needs 4× information.** — Baker & Kim (2017) Eq. 6.6 [VERIFIED]; Lord (1980) [VERIFIED existence].
4. **Conditional (local) reliability ρ(θ)** = `I(θ)/(I(θ)+1)` (unit-variance θ) = 1−SE(θ)². *Answers "is this test reliable for gifted kids specifically?" A headline .95 can hide ρ(θ=2.33)≈.6.* Targets: ρ=.90 needs I≈9–10; ρ=.95 needs I≈19–20. Report as a **curve**, not a scalar. — integrand form via Andersson & Xin, *Applied Psych. Measurement* (PMC5965626) [VERIFIED]; Baker & Kim (2017) [VERIFIED].
5. **Marginal & empirical reliability:** `ρ = 1 − E[SE²(θ)]/σ_θ²` (marginal); `ρ = Var(θ̂)/(Var(θ̂)+mean SE²)` (empirical). *The headline IRT reliability — but it averages over the whole population, so a mid-precise/tail-weak test still posts a high number.* **Fix: tail-restricted marginal reliability** (integrate SE² only over θ ≥ cut). — Green, Bock, Humphreys, Linn & Reckase (1984), *JEM* 21(4):347–360, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1984.tb01039.x [VERIFIED]. **⚠ Correction candidate:** brainlift §1.3 mis-attributes this 1984 paper to *difference-score* reliability; it is the marginal-reliability/CAT-guidelines paper. Difference-score → Lord & Novick (1968)/Feldt & Brennan. [INFERENCE]
6. **Two IRT reliability definitions differ:** parallel-forms correlation of θ̂ vs. squared correlation of θ,θ̂; because θ̂ is biased, parallel-forms ≥ squared-correlation, and the **gap is largest in the tail**. Report which you use. — Kim (2012), *Psychometrika* 77(1):153–162, DOI 10.1007/s11336-011-9238-0 [VERIFIED]; Lord (1983), DOI 10.1007/BF02294018 [VERIFIED].
7. **Relative Efficiency (RE):** `RE(θ)=I_A(θ)/I_B(θ)`. *The rigorous way to say "test A is X× better for gifted kids." RE(2.33)=2 ⇒ A extracts 2× the info at the top-1% cut (B needs ~2× the items to match).* **This is the honest, unbounded, tail-specific home for a "2× better" claim** (unlike capped reliability/validity). Requires same equated θ metric. — Birnbaum (1968); Lord (1980); van der Linden (1984), *Statistica Neerlandica*, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9574.1984.tb01101.x [VERIFIED].
8. **Targeting information at the cut + 3PL peak shift.** Assemble items so I(θ) peaks at θ_cut. Item max-info location (3PL): `θ_max=b_i+(1/a_i)·ln[(1+√(1+8c_i))/2]` (Lord 1980, p.152); c=0 ⇒ peak at b; c>0 ⇒ peak **above** b. *So MC items for a gifted cut should have b somewhat below the cut; constructed-response (c≈0) peak at b — better for tail.* Target I(θ_cut)≥~10 (ρ≈.90) or ≥~20 (ρ≈.95). — Lord (1980) via *Handbook of Test Development* [VERIFIED formula]; Baker & Kim (2017) §6.6 [VERIFIED]; thresholds [INFERENCE].
9. **CAT SE-based stopping rule** — stop when SE(θ̂)≤δ. δ↔reliability: SE .316→ρ~.90 (I~10); SE .22→ρ~.95 (I~20.7); SE .20→ρ~.96 (I=25). *Promises equiprecise measurement for every examinee — the property a gifted test most needs — if the bank has the items.* Use δ≤0.30 (0.22 near cut) + min/max item bounds. — Weiss & Kingsbury (1984), *JEM* 21(4), DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1984.tb01040.x [VERIFIED]; *J. Computerized Adaptive Testing* + PMC6745632/PMC7518406 [VERIFIED].
10. **Minimum-information & PSER stopping rules** — stop when remaining items give < a floor of info at θ̂, or when expected SE reduction is negligible. *The honest rules for a gifted examinee the bank can't serve: stop rather than fake precision, and signal precision was not achieved.* Floors are ad hoc; tune by simulation. — Choi, Grady & Dodd (2010) [SECONDARY]; *Int. J. Testing* 20(2):146–168 (PMC7518406) [VERIFIED].
11. **Sparse-tail item-bank problem (the crux).** The **bank information function** (TIF if all items administered) collapses where items are scarce; there the SE rule is *unreachable* and RMSE can *rise* with more items. *Banks built for typical students starve the high-ability end exactly like clinical banks starve the high-functioning end — adaptivity cannot manufacture absent information.* **Acceptance test: plot I_bank(θ) and check it clears I≈10–20 at θ=2.05–2.58 before trusting any adaptive gifted screen.** — *Int. J. Testing* 20(2):146–168 (PMC7518406) [VERIFIED]; Morris et al. (2016) [SECONDARY].
12. **Kullback–Leibler "global" information** for item selection when θ̂ is unstable/extreme (Fisher = curvature of KL at θ₀). *More robust than Fisher info for short tests / extreme examinees — the normal situation for a gifted child in the first items; reduces bias & MSE when m<~30.* — Chang & Ying (1996), *Applied Psych. Measurement* 20(3):213–229, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000303 [VERIFIED, full text].
13. **Warm's WLE — the tail is biased OUTWARD.** MLE of θ is biased outward (Lord 1983), worst at extremes; Warm's weighted-likelihood removes first-order bias, stays finite. *Outward bias pushes a high scorer's θ̂ even higher → false positives at the gifted cut & boundary instability — a directional error reliability never reveals. EAP biases inward → can under-identify the extreme gifted. Pick the estimator deliberately and report it.* — Warm (1989), *Psychometrika* 54(3):427–450, DOI 10.1007/BF02294627 [VERIFIED]; Lord (1983), DOI 10.1007/BF02294018 [VERIFIED].
14. **Linking precision to classification: SPRT & CI-around-cut.** CI rule: keep testing while θ_cut ∈ (θ̂ ± γ·SE); stop/classify when the interval excludes the cut. SPRT: test H0:θ≤cut−δ1 vs H1:θ≥cut+δ2 with error rates α,β. *Makes SE-at-cut→decision-error explicit: misclassification ≈ Φ(−|θ−θ_cut|/SE). When classification (not a score) is the goal, optimize info AT the cut; KL selection suits this better than Fisher.* Cuts test length ≥22% at equal accuracy. — Eggen & Straetmans (2000), *EPM* 60(5):713–734, DOI 10.1177/00131640021970862 [VERIFIED]; Spray & Reckase (1996), DOI 10.3102/10769986021004405 [VERIFIED]; Wald (1947) [SECONDARY].
15. **Polytomous / graded / partial-credit information (Samejima).** Item info generalizes to a sum over category response functions (GRM/GPCM). *Rich high-ceiling constructed-response/performance tasks (well suited to gifted reasoning) are polytomous, carry more info per item, and can spread info higher on θ than guessing-capped MC.* — Samejima (1977), *Applied Psych. Measurement* 1(2):233–247, DOI 10.1177/014662167700100209 [VERIFIED via refs]; Samejima (1969) Monograph 17 [SECONDARY].
16. **Synthesis — how to quantify "how well does this test measure the top 1–2%":** (1) percentile→θ cut (top 10%→1.28; 5%→1.645; **2%→2.054; 1%→2.326;** 0.5%→2.576); (2) read I(θ_cut), SE=1/√I; (3) conditional reliability ρ(θ_cut); (4) **tail-restricted marginal reliability** (integrate SE² over θ≥cut); (5) **relative efficiency at the cut** vs a benchmark; (6) translate to classification: misclassification ≈ Φ(−|θ−θ_cut|/SE) and/or SPRT/CI simulation. **Require I(2.33)≥~10 (SE≤.316, ρ≈.90); high-stakes ≥~20 (SE≤.22, ρ≈.95); verify the bank clears this at the cut.** — composed from Baker & Kim (2017), Green et al. (1984), Lord (1980), Eggen & Straetmans (2000) [all VERIFIED]; composition itself [INFERENCE].

**(g) New dimensions → queue:** bank-information-function auditing (first-class acceptance test); estimator choice as tail-fairness lever (MLE/WLE/EAP); RE-at-cut as the "2×" home; tail-restricted marginal reliability; KL/global info + SPRT classification framing; polytomous/performance-task info; item-parameter-uncertainty propagation (nominal I overstates real precision; sparse tail doubly penalized); multidimensional Fisher information matrix for profile/tilt (spatial-vs-verbal) decisions.
**(h) Gap:** no located source gives empirical tail information / conditional SE(θ≥2) / bank-information-function for the specific instruments GT would use (CogAT, MAP) — publisher-controlled; the most material `[UNVERIFIED]` numbers. ### Wave 1 · A2 — Classification / decision-quality metrics for identification (2026-07-22)

*Reconstructed from the A2 worker's DOI-verified source list + canonical formulas (its re-dump returned a summary only). Confusion matrix at a cut: TP/FP/FN/TN. Labels as elsewhere.*

1. **Sensitivity (true-positive rate / recall)** = `TP/(TP+FN)` — P(identified | truly gifted). Its complement is the **miss rate**; the core "who did we lose" number for gifted ID. — Altman & Bland (1994), *BMJ* 308:1552, DOI 10.1136/bmj.308.6943.1552 [VERIFIED—A2].
2. **Specificity (true-negative rate)** = `TN/(TN+FP)` — P(not identified | not gifted); trades off against sensitivity as the cut moves. — Altman & Bland (1994) [VERIFIED—A2].
3. **False-negative rate** `FNR = FN/(TP+FN) = 1−sensitivity`; **false-positive rate** `FPR = FP/(FP+TN) = 1−specificity`. *FNR (capable kids screened out) is the headline harm and the **unbounded, tail-specific axis** a "2× better" screen targets (e.g., halve FNR at the cut).* — [INFERENCE from defs].
4. **PPV / NPV.** `PPV = TP/(TP+FP)`, `NPV = TN/(TN+FN)`. PPV = "of those identified, how many are truly gifted," and is **base-rate-dependent** (Bayes): `PPV = (sens·prev)/[sens·prev + (1−spec)(1−prev)]`. At giftedness prevalence 2–10%, even sens=spec=.90 yields modest PPV → many false positives. — Bayes/standard [VERIFIED—A2].
5. **Base rates govern efficiency (Meehl & Rosen).** A device must beat base-rate prediction; at extreme base rates a "valid" cut can *lower* the number of correct decisions. — Meehl & Rosen (1955), *Psychological Bulletin* 52(3):194–216, DOI 10.1037/h0048070 [VERIFIED—session].
6. **ROC curve & AUC.** Plot sensitivity vs. 1−specificity across all cuts; **AUC = P(a random gifted child scores above a random non-gifted child)** = the Mann–Whitney/Wilcoxon statistic — threshold-independent discrimination. Convention: .5 chance, .7–.8 acceptable, .8–.9 excellent. — Hanley & McNeil (1982), *Radiology* 143(1):29–36, DOI 10.1148/radiology.143.1.7063747 [VERIFIED—session].
7. **Youden's J** = `sensitivity + specificity − 1` (0–1); its max gives the optimal cut (ROC point farthest from the chance diagonal). — Youden (1950), *Cancer* 3(1):32–35, DOI 10.1002/1097-0142(1950)3:1<32::AID-CNCR2820030106>3.0.CO;2-3 [VERIFIED—A2].
8. **Likelihood ratios & post-test odds.** `LR+ = sens/(1−spec)`, `LR− = (1−sens)/spec`; `post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR`. LR+>10 / LR−<0.1 = strong shifts. — Deeks & Altman (2004), *BMJ* 329:168–169, DOI 10.1136/bmj.329.7458.168; Jaeschke, Guyatt & Sackett (1994) [VERIFIED—A2].
9. **Diagnostic Odds Ratio** = `LR+/LR− = (TP·TN)/(FP·FN)` — one summary of discrimination (1 = useless), independent of prevalence. — Glas et al. (2003), *J. Clinical Epidemiology* 56(11):1129–1135, DOI 10.1016/S0895-4356(03)00177-X [VERIFIED—A2].
10. **Taylor–Russell model.** Given base rate, **selection ratio**, and predictor validity, gives the **success ratio** (PPV analog) — quantifies the gain in % correctly selected from a valid predictor at a chosen selectivity; the basis of expectancy tables. — Taylor & Russell (1939), *J. Applied Psychology* 23(5):565–578, DOI 10.1037/h0057079 [VERIFIED—A2].
11. **Decision accuracy & decision consistency (single form).** Accuracy = % classified as their *true-score* status; consistency = % classified the same on a *parallel form*. — Livingston & Lewis (1995), *JEM* 32(2):179–197, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x [VERIFIED—session]; Hanson & Brennan (1990), *JEM* 27(4):345–359 [VERIFIED—A2]; Rudner (2001), *PARE* 7(14) [VERIFIED—A2].
12. **Cohen's kappa** = `(p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e)` — chance-corrected agreement (two raters/two forms classifying gifted vs not); **weighted kappa** for ordered categories; Huynh's decision-consistency indices. — Cohen (1960), *EPM* 20(1):37–46, DOI 10.1177/001316446002000104; Cohen (1968), *Psychological Bulletin* 70(4):213–220, DOI 10.1037/h0026256; Huynh (1976), *JEM* 13(4):253–264 [VERIFIED—A2].
13. **Conditional SEM → misclassification at the cut.** A child within ~1 SEM of the cut has a large misclassification probability ≈ `Φ(−|score−cut|/SEM)` — the bridge from A1 precision to A2 decisions. — [INFERENCE; composes Livingston–Lewis + IRT SE(θ)].
14. **Multi-stage / AND-gate sensitivity collapse.** Overall sensitivity ≈ the **product** of stage sensitivities, so a nomination/screen-then-test **AND** process caps identification and can push false negatives **>60%**; disjunctive (OR)/compensatory rules recover more. — McBee, Peters & Miller (2016), *GCQ* 60(4), DOI 10.1177/0016986216656256; McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014), *GCQ* 58(1):69–89, DOI 10.1177/0016986213513794 [VERIFIED—project].
15. **F1 / precision–recall (ML framing).** `F1 = 2·PPV·sensitivity/(PPV+sensitivity)` (harmonic mean of precision & recall); at low base rates, **precision–recall curves / PR-AUC** are more informative than ROC. — Powers (2011), *J. Machine Learning Technologies* 2(1):37–63 [VERIFIED—A2]; ties to A8.
16. **Net benefit / decision-curve analysis (discovered).** Beyond discrimination, weigh false negatives vs false positives at a threshold (`net benefit = (TP − w·FP)/n`, w = FP:FN cost ratio) — the explicit place to encode the equity–efficiency cost asymmetry of missing a capable child. — Vickers & Elkin (2006), *Medical Decision Making* 26(6):565–574, DOI 10.1177/0272989X06295361 [VERIFIED—A2].

**(g) New dimensions → queue:** precision–recall / PR-AUC and **F-beta** (weight recall to cut false negatives) for low-base-rate gifted ID; **decision-curve analysis / net benefit** with an explicit FN:FP cost ratio (equity–efficiency tradeoff); **expectancy tables** (Taylor–Russell) as a stakeholder-communication tool; multi-stage sensitivity math as a hard design constraint (avoid AND gates); calibration of predicted "gifted probability" (Brier score, calibration curves) → queue for A8.
**(h) Gap:** almost no gifted-ID study reports a full confusion matrix / AUC against a *defensible external "truly gifted" criterion* — the **criterion problem** (using an ability test to define the truth it's judged against) plus low base rates make PPV/AUC hard to pin empirically. Next: hunt any study with a genuine external gifted criterion + reported sensitivity/PPV.

### Wave 2 · A3 — Decision/composite reliability & the reliability–validity ceilings (2026-07-22)

*How single-score reliabilities **combine** (composites), **collapse** (difference/tilt/change scores), **cap classification** at a cut, and **bound** every downstream validity. Worked numbers labeled [INFERENCE].*

**Composite & test-length reliability**
1. **Mosier weighted-composite reliability (1943):** `ρ_CC = 1 − [Σ w_i²σ_i²(1−ρ_ii)] / σ_C²`. *Reliability of a weighted verbal+quant+spatial "general giftedness" index — can exceed any single subtest because inter-correlations add true-score variance.* Worked: 3 subtests (ρ .90/.88/.85, r_ij .5–.6) → **ρ_CC≈.94** > best part. Target ≥.90 (ideally ≥.95) for individual selection. Assumes uncorrelated component errors. — Mosier (1943), *Psychometrika* 8(3):161–168, DOI 10.1007/BF02288700 [VERIFIED].
2. **Spearman–Brown prophecy:** `ρ* = nρ/[1+(n−1)ρ]`. *How much a screener gains from length.* ρ=.70 tripled → .875; moving .70→.90 needs ≈3.9× length (steep diminishing returns). Assumes added items are parallel; can't fix validity. — Spearman (1910)/Brown (1910) [SECONDARY].
3. **Stratified alpha & Wang–Stanley:** stratified α `=1−[Σσ_i²(1−α_i)]/σ_C²`. *Correct reliability for a multidimensional battery — plain α underestimates it.* Triangulates with Mosier/Wang–Stanley (≈.85–.86 on the same demo). — Widhiarso & Ravand (2014), *Review of Psychology* 21(2):111–121 [VERIFIED]; Cronbach/Schönemann/McKie (1965); Wang & Stanley (1970) [SECONDARY].

**Difference / profile / change scores (the "tilt" penalty)**
4. **Difference-score reliability:** equal-variance `r_DD=(r_XX+r_YY−2r_XY)/(2−2r_XY)`. *Trustworthiness of a spatial−verbal "tilt": subtraction removes shared g true-variance but keeps both errors → usually far below either part.* Worked: parts .90/.85, r_XY=.80 → **r_DD=.375**. Don't base individual placement on a tilt unless r_DD≥.80 (needs reliable parts + modest inter-correlation). — Trafimow (2015), *Cogent Mathematics* 2(1):1064626, DOI 10.1080/23311835.2015.1064626 [VERIFIED]; Lord & Novick (1968); Feldt & Brennan (1989) [SECONDARY].
5. **Profile/shape reliability (Yarnold 1984; Conger–Lipshitz):** how reliably a *profile shape* replicates; typically **much lower** than the level (mean). Prefer level-based composites over shape-based high-stakes decisions. — Conger & Lipshitz, DOI 10.1177/0013164491511007 [VERIFIED existence]; Yarnold (1984), *EPM* 44(1):49–59 [SECONDARY].
6. **Standard error of the difference:** `SE_diff=√(SEM_X²+SEM_Y²)` (or minus 2r·SEM·SEM if correlated); critical gap = 1.96·SE_diff. *Is THIS child's tilt bigger than noise?* Worked (SD=15, r .90/.85): SE_diff≈7.5 → need ≈**14.7-pt** split to call a tilt statistically reliable — then check base rates. Significance ≠ abnormality ≠ meaning. — Cogn-IQ/SED [SECONDARY]; WISC-V report [PUB/COI]; *EPM* (1989) DOI 10.1177/001316448904900404 [VERIFIED existence].
7. **Reliable Change Index (Jacobson & Truax 1991):** `RCI=(x_post−x_pre)/S_diff`, `S_diff=SD·√2·√(1−r_xx)`; reliable if |RCI|≥1.96. *Guardrail against re-testing "bubble" kids until noise lifts them over the cut.* Worked (SD=15, r=.90): threshold ≈**13.2 pts**. Watch practice effects. — Jacobson & Truax (1991), *JCCP* 59(1):12–19, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12 [VERIFIED].
8. **Gain-score (un)reliability (Cronbach & Furby 1970):** raw pre/post gain reliability is low when pre–post correlate high; worked r_gain≈**.25**. Use residualized/latent-change or multi-wave growth models, not 2-wave individual gains. (Rogosa/Willett: not *inherently* doomed — depends on true individual differences in change.) — Cronbach & Furby (1970), *Psychological Bulletin* 74(1):68–80 [VERIFIED].

**Measurement error & decision consistency at a cut**
9. **SEM (global):** `SEM=SD·√(1−r_xx)`; 95% band ±1.96·SEM. Worked (SD=15): r=.95→SEM 3.35; .90→4.74; .80→6.71 (a 130 score spans ~117–143 at r=.80 — too wide for a hard cut). It's an *average* (see #10). — Harvill (1991), NCME *ITEMS* module [VERIFIED]; Crocker & Algina [SECONDARY].
10. **Conditional SEM & misclassification at the cut:** binomial `cSEM=√[X(k−X)/(k−1)]` (largest mid-range); misclassification for true score T at cut c ≈ `Φ(−|c−T|/cSEM)`. Worked (cut 130, SEM 3.35): true 135 → FN≈**6.8%**, true 125 → FP≈**6.8%**; true 130 → 50%. **Non-obvious: when the cut is near mid-scale, cSEM-at-cut > global SEM.** Argues for a confidence band / multiple criteria, not a single hard cut. — cut-score *JEM* practice paper [VERIFIED]; binomial cSEM DOI 10.1007/s40037-020-00586-0 [VERIFIED]; 5-methods comparison DOI 10.1177/014662168500900402 [VERIFIED existence].
11. **Kelley's regressed true score & regression to the mean:** `T̂=X̄+r_xx(X−X̄)`. *Extreme observed scores are partly luck → best true estimate is pulled toward the mean.* Worked (X̄=100, r=.90, X=130): **T̂=127** (< cut) → hard cuts over-select lucky borderline cases; explains retest regression. — Harvill (1991) NCME module (Kelley/Gulliksen) [VERIFIED].
12. **Decision accuracy vs consistency (Livingston & Lewis 1995; Subkoviak 1976):** accuracy = agreement with *true*-score class; consistency = agreement across *parallel forms* (report p₀ and κ; aim p₀≥~.85–.90). **Depends on cut location, not just reliability** — a deep-tail gifted cut can post high overall agreement yet high *conditional* error at the boundary; **κ is depressed/unstable at low base rates (kappa paradox).** — Livingston & Lewis (1995), *JEM* 32(2):179–197, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x [VERIFIED]; Subkoviak (1976), *JEM* 13(4):265–276, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1976.tb00017.x [VERIFIED existence].

**Attenuation, ceilings, and Generalizability Theory**
13. **Correction for attenuation & the validity ceiling (Spearman 1904):** disattenuated `ρ_TxTy=r_xy/√(r_xx·r_yy)`; **ceiling** `r_xy ≤ √(r_xx·r_yy) ≤ 1`; reliability index `√(r_xx)` caps validity vs a perfect criterion. *The math answer to "why can't reliability and validity 2×": both ≤1, and validity ≤ √(reliability).* Worked: r_xx=.90, r_yy=.70 → max observable validity **.79**. Disattenuated r can exceed 1 with error → report as 1.0; never operationalize a disattenuated value. — Spearman (1904), *Am. J. Psychology* 15(1):72–101 [VERIFIED]; Rasch.org derivation [VERIFIED].
14. **Generalizability Theory (Cronbach et al. 1972; Brennan 2001):** person×rater×task designs separate variance components; **G-coefficient** `Eρ²=σ²_p/(σ²_p+σ²_δ)` (relative/ranking) vs **dependability** `Φ=σ²_p/(σ²_p+σ²_Δ)` (absolute — the right index for a fixed gifted cut; Φ≤Eρ²). *For portfolio/performance-task giftedness with raters, tells you which facet is noisy and how many raters/tasks to add (D-study).* Worked: 2 raters×4 tasks → Eρ²≈.81, Φ≈.75; ×8 tasks → .87/.83 — **adding tasks beat adding raters** because person×task variance dominated. Aim Φ≥.90 for high-stakes cuts. — Cronbach, Gleser, Nanda & Rajaratnam (1972) [SECONDARY]; Brennan (2001), *Generalizability Theory*, Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4757-3456-0 [VERIFIED existence].

**(g) New dimensions → queue:** Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser **selection utility** + Taylor–Russell/Naylor–Shine (convert a validity coefficient into expected #additional truly-gifted selected at a given base rate × selection ratio); **range-restriction corrections** (Thorndike Case II/III — selecting the top tail deflates observed validity; interacts with the attenuation ceiling); **decision consistency of multiple-criteria (AND/OR) composite gates** (compounding error); **multivariate/nested G-theory & sub-score dependability** (Haberman's value-added sub-score criterion); **fairness-conditioned reliability** — conditional SEM/decision-consistency *by subgroup* near the cut (ties to applicant-rights guardrail).
**(h) Gap:** these quantify reliability *ceilings*, not *utility at a cut* — convert validity/reliability into selection **yield** (Taylor–Russell / Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser) so "how well does it separate the gifted?" becomes an expected-correct-classifications number (queued for A6/utility).

### Wave 2 · A4 — Predictive, criterion & incremental validity for gifted identification (2026-07-22)

*Does the screen predict a defensible criterion, add beyond CogAT, and do so without bias or self-fulfilling contamination? Worked numbers [INFERENCE]; "established" vs "contested" flagged.*

1. **Criterion validity (concurrent vs predictive), `r_xy`.** Predictive (Y measured later) is the design that licenses "forecasts who will excel"; concurrent understates it via range restriction. Report the **95% CI** (Fisher-z; single-cohort r is noisy). Ability→achievement ≈ .5–.6 (CogAT↔achievement .63, Ozen 2024). Omnibus r ≠ tail validity → pair with A1/A2. — *Standards* (2014) ch.1; Cohen (1988) [SECONDARY]. **Established.**
2. **Operational vs true validity / attenuation ceiling.** `ρ_true=r_xy/√(r_xx·r_yy)`; observed capped at `√(r_xx·r_yy)`. "Operational validity" corrects the *criterion* only (you keep your imperfect predictor). Worked: r=.50, r_yy=.64 → operational .625. Don't operationalize disattenuated values. — Spearman (1904), DOI 10.2307/1412159 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**
3. **Range-restriction correction (Thorndike Case II direct / Case III indirect).** Case II: `R=(rU)/√(1−r²+r²U²)`, `U=S_X/s_X`. Worked: restricted r=.30, U=2 → **R≈.53**. Gifted validity studies run on already-selected samples → mechanically shrunk r. "Selection is almost always indirect" (Le & Schmidt 2006) → naïve Case II under-corrects; some indirect over-correct. — Thorndike (1949) [SECONDARY]; Le & Schmidt (2006), DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.91.3.594 [VERIFIED]. **Established method, contested application.**
4. **Validity generalization (meta-analytic).** Remove sampling-error/RR/criterion-unreliability variance; small residual → validity generalizes (situational specificity mostly artifactual). Report ρ̄ + 80% credibility interval. GMA operational validity once reported **.51**. — Schmidt & Hunter (1977), DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.62.5.529; (1998), DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.124.2.262 [VERIFIED]. **Established method; headline numbers now contested (→#5).**
5. **Sackett et al. (2022) overcorrection critique.** The common RR artifact-distribution corrections **systematically overcorrect**; **cognitive-ability operational validity revised .51 → .31**; validities drop ~.10–.20 across predictors; **structured interviews overtake cognitive ability** as top predictor. Rule: prefer *observed* validity + a documented RR correction over off-the-shelf artifact distributions. — Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022), *JAP* 107(11):2040–2068, DOI 10.1037/apl0000994 [VERIFIED]. **Contested / major critique — cite alongside any VG claim.**
6. **Incremental validity — ΔR² = squared semipartial `sr²`.** The bar a 2nd measure clears beyond CogAT: unique criterion variance after partialling the first test; `F_change` for significance. Worked: CogAT R²≈.40; +spatial → .44 ⇒ **ΔR²=.04 (sr=.20)** ≈ Wai et al.'s ~4%. No fixed threshold; must **replicate**. Entry order/shared-method contamination can fake it. — Hunsley & Meyer (2003), DOI 10.1037/1040-3590.15.4.446 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**
7. **Construct validity & nomological network.** Embed the score in predicted relations (convergent + discriminant, MTMM); a screen can have criterion validity yet weak construct validity (proxy for opportunity/practice). Requires a falsifiable theory of giftedness (often missing). — Cronbach & Meehl (1955), DOI 10.1037/h0040957; Campbell & Fiske (1959), DOI 10.1037/h0046016 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**
8. **Messick's unified validity (6 aspects incl. consequential); 2014 Standards' 5 evidence sources.** Validity = justification of a *use/decision*, so false-negative harms & adverse impact are *part of validity*, not a footnote (maps R5/R9, A5). Build a validity **argument**. Consequential aspect itself debated. — Messick (1995), *American Psychologist* 50(9):741–749, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.50.9.741 [VERIFIED]. **Established (with live debate).**
9. **Predictive validity vs real long-run "ultimate criteria."** Validate against aggregated accomplishment decades out (doctorates/tenure/patents), not next-year grades — dodges circularity. SMPY top-1-in-10,000: **44% doctorates** by 38 (vs ~2%), dose-response *within* the top 1%. Useless for near-term QA; eminence confounded by opportunity (predictive validity ≠ program impact). — Kell, Lubinski & Benbow (2013), DOI 10.1177/0956797612457784; Lubinski & Benbow (2006), DOI 10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00019.x [VERIFIED]. **Established.**
10. **Above-level testing makes tail validity *estimable*.** A grade-level test everyone maxes has no variance to correlate → tail validity unmeasurable; above-level (older-normed) restores variance to "distinguish the able from the exceptionally able." Verify item information at target θ (A1). — Lubinski & Benbow (2006); Kell et al. (2013) [VERIFIED]. **Established.**
11. **The criterion problem & circularity.** Criteria suffer deficiency/contamination (Brogden & Taylor 1950); in gifted ID an ability test "validated" against an ability-loaded criterion inflates r via shared g + shared method — a manufactured coefficient. Fix: construct-distinct criteria (#9), independent collection (#16), partial the g-overlap. Some overlap is legitimate → matter of degree. — Sternberg (2022), *Roeper Review* 44(4):231–248, DOI 10.1080/02783193.2022.2115179 (critical essay) [VERIFIED]. **Contested framing / established mechanics.**
12. **Differential prediction — Cleary model.** Bias = consistent nonzero prediction errors for a subgroup under a common regression; test slope (`b3` interaction) & intercept (`b2`) differences. Differential validity ≠ differential prediction. Classic pattern: cognitive tests often **overpredict** minority criterion performance. — Cleary (1968), *JEM* 5(2):115–124, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1968.tb00613.x [VERIFIED]. **Established (standard model).**
13. **…but bias detectability is contested.** Monte Carlo (3.185M conditions): intercept/slope bias tests have **low power**, distorted by unequal n, unreliability, range restriction → frequently **miss real bias**; a null result may be power failure, not fairness. Report power; triangulate with DIF/invariance (A5). — Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce (2010), *JAP* 95(4):648–680, DOI 10.1037/a0018714 [VERIFIED]. **Contested / active debate.**
14. **Utility I — Taylor–Russell.** Base rate × selection ratio × validity → **success ratio (≈PPV)** among selectees. Worked: BR .60, SR .30, r=.70 → **91%** satisfactory (vs 60% base); BR .50, SR .20, r=.35 → PPV .70 but **sensitivity .28**. Low base rate + stringent cut = fragile PPV + many false negatives (with Meehl & Rosen, A2). Dichotomizes a continuous criterion. — Taylor & Russell (1939), *JAP* 23(5):565–578, DOI 10.1037/h0057079 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**
15. **Utility II — Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser.** Utility/selectee `= r_xy·SD_y·Z̄_s`; total `ΔU=N_s·T·r_xy·SD_y·Z̄_s − C`; for a 2nd measure use `(r_new−r_baseline)`. **Utility is linear in validity** and scales with selectivity (`Z̄_s=φ/SR`) and outcome variance `SD_y` → even modest validity gains have large aggregate value in a big selective pool. `SD_y` estimation is notoriously contested. — Brogden (1949); Cronbach & Gleser (1965); Schmidt, Hunter, McKenzie & Muldrow (1979), DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.64.6.609 [VERIFIED]. **Established (model); `SD_y` contested.**
16. **Synthesis — "predicts who benefits" without contaminating selection & outcome:** (i) predictive (not concurrent) design; (ii) construct-distinct criterion (#9/#11); (iii) criterion measured **blind** to the selection score (prevents self-fulfilling contamination); (iv) handle the **missing-Y/selection problem** (outcomes only for selected kids) via near-cut band / marginal lottery / waitlist; (v) report **incremental** benefit prediction (#6); (vi) express as **utility** (#14–15) + **tail classification** (A2), not omnibus r; (vii) audit consequences + differential prediction (#8,#12–13). Cleanest (marginal-randomization) designs collide with applicant-rights (charter stop-condition). — composed from verified sources; composition [INFERENCE]. **Established components, novel composition.**

**(g) New dimensions → queue:** observed-vs-corrected validity as a governance rule (post-Sackett: quote correction method; treat corrected as upper bound); the **missing-outcome/selection problem** as first-class (near-cut RD / marginal-lottery to get uncontaminated outcomes — bridges to the impact/counterfactual work); a **sequenced "ultimate-criteria" library** (pre-registered defensible gifted criteria with contamination/deficiency ratings); power-auditing fairness tests (Aguinis); **predictive validity AT the cut (local validity)** — tail-restricted analog of A1's tail reliability; **benefit-based validity** (does the screen predict who *gains from the program*, not just who scores high — aptitude×treatment).
**(h) Gap:** no located source reports a *predictive* validity or Cleary differential-prediction test for the exact instruments GT would deploy (CogAT, MAP) against a construct-distinct, contamination-controlled gifted criterion — the most material `[UNVERIFIED]` hole; next: instrument technical manuals + independent longitudinal re-analyses, and spec a near-cut comparison design.

### Wave 3 · A5 — Fairness, measurement invariance, DIF/DTF, adverse impact & representation metrics (2026-07-22)

*Scope: metrics that separate three legally/psychometrically distinct questions a gifted screen must answer — **measurement bias** (does the test measure the same construct the same way across groups: invariance/DIF/DTF), **prediction bias** (does it forecast the criterion the same way: Cleary/differential prediction, covered in A4), and **impact** (who actually gets identified: adverse impact/representation). Worked numbers labeled [INFERENCE]; "established" vs "contested" flagged. Labels as elsewhere.*

1. **Measurement-invariance hierarchy (configural → metric/weak → scalar/strong → strict), via multi-group CFA.** (a) A nested sequence of increasingly constrained MG-CFA models. (b) **Configural** = same pattern/number of factors & loading structure (baseline); **metric/weak** = equal loadings Λ (λ same across groups); **scalar/strong** = equal loadings + intercepts τ; **strict** = + equal residual variances Θ. (c) *Quantifies whether the same latent "giftedness" is being measured on the same scale across subgroups — the precondition for comparing scores/means at all.* Metric supports comparing associations; **scalar is required before comparing observed means or applying one cut score across groups**; Meredith proves **strict factorial invariance is the condition for fair selection**. (d) Test each step by nested-model fit change (see #2); if a step fails, localize noninvariant parameters and consider **partial invariance** (free the offending λ/τ while holding ≥2 anchors). (e) Limits: needs large per-group N, a correct factor model, continuous-normal (or WLSMV for categorical) indicators; "partial invariance" decisions can be data-driven/capitalize on chance. — Meredith, W. (1993), *Psychometrika* 58(4):525–543, DOI 10.1007/BF02294825 [VERIFIED]; Vandenberg, R. J., & Lance, C. E. (2000), *Organizational Research Methods* 3(1):4–70, DOI 10.1177/109442810031002 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

2. **ΔCFI / ΔRMSEA / ΔSRMR cutoffs for invariance testing (the alternative to the oversensitive Δχ²).** (a) Change in approximate-fit indices between adjacent invariance models. (b) Δχ² is N-inflated (rejects trivial noninvariance in large samples), so use ΔAFIs. (c) *Quantifies whether tightening cross-group constraints degrades fit enough to reject invariance — i.e., whether a common metric/cut is defensible.* (d) **Cheung & Rensvold (2002):** invariance retained if **ΔCFI ≤ .01** (drop ≤.01), **ΔGamma-hat ≤ .001**, **ΔMc ≤ .02** (these three are independent of model size & N). **Chen (2007):** for **N > 300 / equal groups**, flag noninvariance at **ΔCFI ≥ .010 + (ΔRMSEA ≥ .015 or ΔSRMR ≥ .030 for loadings / .010 for intercepts–residuals)**; for **N < 300 / unequal groups**, stricter — **ΔCFI ≥ .005, ΔRMSEA ≥ .010, ΔSRMR ≥ .025 (loadings)/.005**. (e) Limits: cutoffs come from specific simulations and are **contested** for IRT-calibrated dichotomous items, small/unequal groups, ordinal data, and many groups (Rutkowski & Svetina and others show they can miss real DIF); treat as heuristics, not laws. — Cheung & Rensvold (2002), *Structural Equation Modeling* 9(2):233–255, DOI 10.1207/s15328007sem0902_5 [VERIFIED]; Chen, F. F. (2007), *SEM* 14(3):464–504, DOI 10.1080/10705510701301834 [VERIFIED]. **Established methods; exact cutoffs contested.**

3. **Mantel–Haenszel DIF and MH D-DIF (the ETS workhorse for item-level measurement bias).** (a) A conditional (matched-on-total-score) 2×2×K contingency-table test of whether reference/focal examinees of equal ability have equal odds of a correct response. (b) Common odds ratio `α_MH = Σ_k(A_kD_k/N_k) / Σ_k(B_kC_k/N_k)` (A,B = ref right/wrong; C,D = focal right/wrong at score level k); **`χ²_MH = (|ΣA_k − ΣE(A_k)| − 0.5)² / ΣVar(A_k)`**, df = 1; effect size on the ETS delta metric (mean 13, SD 4): **`MH D-DIF = −2.35·ln(α_MH)`** (2.35 ≈ 4/1.7). (c) *Quantifies uniform item bias — an item systematically harder for equally-able members of a subgroup — the most common measurable form of measurement bias.* (d) Sign: MH D-DIF < 0 ⇒ harder for focal group; **1.0 = one delta point harder**. Worked [INFERENCE]: α_MH = 1.5 → MH D-DIF = −2.35·ln1.5 = −0.95 (|·|<1 ⇒ negligible); α_MH = 2.0 → −1.63 (large if significant). (e) Limits: total-score matching contaminated if the studied item is itself biased (needs purification/2-pass); **low power for nonuniform/crossing DIF** (MH ≈ 0 when effects cancel); needs adequate cell counts. — Holland, P. W., & Thayer, D. T. (1988), "Differential item performance and the Mantel–Haenszel procedure," in Wainer & Braun (Eds.), *Test Validity* (pp. 129–145), Erlbaum; method/formula also ETS RR-86-31, DOI 10.1002/j.2330-8516.1986.tb00186.x [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

4. **ETS A/B/C effect-size classification of DIF (turning a p-value into an action rule).** (a) A three-tier flag combining statistical significance with the MH D-DIF magnitude. (b)/(d) **Category A (negligible):** MH D-DIF not significantly ≠ 0 (p ≥ .05) **or** |MH D-DIF| < 1.0. **Category C (large):** |MH D-DIF| ≥ 1.5 **and** significantly > 1.0. **Category B (moderate):** everything in between (significant, |MH D-DIF| ≥ 1.0, not meeting C). (c) *Quantifies how much bias is "too much" — the operational bar ETS uses to revise/retire items; C-items are removed or re-reviewed, B-items reviewed, A-items retained.* (e) Limits: thresholds are ETS conventions (not universal); significance component reintroduces N-dependence; classification is per-item and can still leave **test-level** bias (see DTF, #8). — Dorans, N. J., & Holland, P. W. (1993), "DIF detection and description," in Holland & Wainer (Eds.), *Differential Item Functioning* (pp. 35–66), Erlbaum (ETS RR DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.1992.tb01440.x); Zieky, M. (1993), same volume (pp. 337–347) [VERIFIED via primary + multiple secondary]. **Established.**

5. **Logistic-regression DIF (uniform vs nonuniform) + ΔR² effect size.** (a) Regress item response on matching score, group, and their interaction: **`logit P(u=1) = β0 + β1·S + β2·G + β3·(S·G)`**. (b) **Uniform DIF ⇔ β2 ≠ 0** (test 1 df); **nonuniform (crossing) DIF ⇔ β3 ≠ 0** (interaction, 1 df); overall DIF = 2-df LR χ² (model with G,S·G vs S only). (c) *Quantifies both a constant subgroup disadvantage and an ability-dependent one (e.g., an item that is fair for moderately able focal students but biased against the highest-ability focal students — directly relevant to a gifted cut).* (d) Effect size = **ΔR²** (Nagelkerke / Zumbo–Thomas). **Jodoin & Gierl (2001) bands: A/negligible ΔR² < .035; B/moderate .035 ≤ ΔR² < .070; C/large ΔR² ≥ .070** (calibrated to the ETS A/B/C metric; stricter than Zumbo & Thomas's Cohen-based .13/.26, which over-flag). (e) Limits: observed-score matching (not latent θ); pseudo-R² choice matters; large N still over-detects without the effect-size gate. — Swaminathan, H., & Rogers, H. J. (1990), *Journal of Educational Measurement* 27(4):361–370, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00754.x [VERIFIED]; Jodoin, M. G., & Gierl, M. J. (2001), *Applied Measurement in Education* 14(4):329–349, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1404_2 [VERIFIED]; Zumbo & Thomas (1996)/Zumbo (1999) [SECONDARY]. **Established; superior to MH for nonuniform DIF.**

6. **SIBTEST (and its β̂_uni effect size) — regression-corrected, bundle-capable DIF/DTF.** (a) A nonparametric procedure that matches on a **regression-corrected true score of a "valid subtest"** rather than raw total, then estimates the group difference in adjusted item/bundle performance. (b) `β̂_uni = Σ_k p̂_k (Ȳ*_Rk − Ȳ*_Fk)`; statistic B = β̂_uni / SE(β̂_uni) ~ N(0,1). (c) *Quantifies bias while avoiding the Type-I inflation MH suffers when the two groups genuinely differ in target ability — the exact situation in gifted screening (real mean gaps), and it aggregates items into bundles to detect amplification/cancellation.* (d) **Roussos & Stout (1996) bands (mapped to ETS): A/negligible |β_uni| < .059; B/moderate .059 ≤ |β_uni| < .088; C/large |β_uni| ≥ .088** (with significance). (e) Limits: needs a defensible DIF-free "valid subtest" (circularity risk, handled iteratively); crossing DIF needs the crossing-SIBTEST variant. — Shealy, R., & Stout, W. (1993), *Psychometrika* 58(2):159–194, DOI 10.1007/BF02294572 [VERIFIED]; Roussos, L., & Stout, W. (1996), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 20(4):355–371, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000403 (bands [VERIFIED via Magis et al. 2010; PMC8850763]). **Established.**

7. **IRT-based DIF: Lord's χ² and Raju's signed/unsigned area between ICCs.** (a) Compare item characteristic curves (ICCs) estimated separately per group on a common metric. (b) **Lord's χ²** tests equality of item parameters (a,b,c). **Raju's signed area (SA)** = net area between the two ICCs (captures **uniform** DIF; crossing DIF partially cancels); **unsigned area (UA)** = ∫|P_R(θ)−P_F(θ)|dθ (captures **total** DIF including nonuniform). For equal discriminations and c=0, SA reduces to the difficulty difference (b_F − b_R). (c) *Quantifies bias as a curve-level discrepancy at every θ — so you can read DIF specifically at the gifted cut (θ≈2.0–2.33), not just on average.* (d) Significance via Raju (1990) Z(SA), Z(UA). **Key caveat: when lower asymptotes c differ, the area is infinite** — guessing-parameter differences can't be summarized as an area. (e) Limits: needs good calibration, common linking/equating, and large N for stable c; sensitive to linking error. — Raju, N. S. (1988), *Psychometrika* 53(4):495–502, DOI 10.1007/BF02294403 [VERIFIED]; Raju, N. S. (1990), *APM* 14(2):197–207, DOI 10.1177/014662169001400208 [VERIFIED]; Lord (1980) [SECONDARY]. **Established.**

8. **DIF vs DTF — differential test functioning and the DFIT framework (CDIF, NCDIF, DTF).** (a) DIF is item-level bias; **DTF is whether the biased items sum to a *test-score*-level difference for equally-able examinees** — the quantity that actually moves a pass/fail cut. (b) **DFIT (Raju, van der Linden & Fleer 1995):** `NCDIF_i = E_F[(P_iF(θ) − P_iR(θ))²]` (item-level, assumes other items unbiased); **`DTF = E_F[(T_F(θ) − T_R(θ))²]`** where T = test characteristic (true-score) curve; and the compensatory index **CDIF_i sums exactly to DTF (Σ_i CDIF_i = DTF)**. (c) *Quantifies whether item biases **amplify** (same-direction) into real test-level unfairness or **cancel** — a test can have several flagged DIF items yet negligible DTF, or vice versa; DTF is the honest fairness number for a composite gifted score.* (d) Common practice: NCDIF < ~.006 (dichotomous) treated as negligible; DTF tested by χ². (e) Limits: parametric, needs linking to a common metric and item-parameter estimation; NCDIF's "others unbiased" assumption is exactly what CDIF/DTF relax. — Raju, N. S., van der Linden, W. J., & Fleer, P. F. (1995), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 19(4):353–368, DOI 10.1177/014662169501900405 [VERIFIED]; DTF concept originates in Shealy & Stout (1993) [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

9. **The measurement–prediction duality paradox (why "no DIF" ≠ "no predictive bias").** (a) A theorem relating measurement invariance (MI; #1) and predictive/Cleary invariance (PI; A4). (b)/(c) Millsap proves that **under realistic conditions MI and PI are mutually incompatible** — a test that is measurement-invariant will generally show Cleary intercept/slope differences, and a test with no differential prediction generally violates factorial invariance. *This is the single most important conceptual result for this project: it means a screen can pass DIF/invariance audits yet still "overpredict"/"underpredict" a subgroup's criterion, and fixing one form of bias can create the other. The three buckets (measurement bias, prediction bias, impact) cannot be collapsed and must be reported separately.* (d) Practical rule: report **both** an invariance/DIF result **and** a differential-prediction result, and state which fairness definition governs the decision; never treat one as proof of the other. (e) Limits: proven cleanly for the linear/single-factor case; magnitude of the conflict depends on reliability and group ability gap. — Millsap, R. E. (1997), *Psychological Methods* 2(3):248–260, DOI 10.1037/1082-989X.2.3.248 [VERIFIED]; Millsap (2007), *Psychometrika* 72(4):461–473, DOI 10.1007/s11336-007-9039-7 [VERIFIED]; "Duality Paradox," *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 30(4), DOI 10.1207/s15327906mbr3004_6 [VERIFIED]. **Established (and under-applied).**

10. **Adverse impact — the four-fifths (80%) rule.** (a) A disparate-impact screen on *outcomes* (who gets selected), independent of item content. (b) **Impact ratio `IR = (selection rate of focal group) / (selection rate of highest-rate group)`; adverse impact flagged if IR < 0.80.** (c) *Quantifies impact — the bottom-line question "are subgroups identified at comparable rates?" — regardless of whether the test is measurement- or prediction-unbiased.* (d) Worked (EEOC's own example): rates White 60%, Black 51%, Hispanic 48%, American Indian 45% → IRs 85%, 80%, 75% → adverse impact indicated for American Indians only. (e) Limits: **a rule of thumb, not a legal definition of discrimination**; unstable at small N (one decision flips it); ignores statistical significance (pair with a 2-SD/z-test on the rate difference); **an unbiased test can still fail it** when real ability gaps exist (impact ≠ bias). — EEOC (1978), *Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures*, 29 C.F.R. § 1607.4(D) [VERIFIED]. **Established (legal standard).**

11. **Standardized mean difference d — the magnitude of the group gap the cut acts on (with tail multiplication).** (a) **`d = (M_ref − M_focal)/SD_pooled`** (Cohen's d / signal-detection separation of the two group score distributions). (b) Cohen bands: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. (c) *Quantifies the raw group difference that any single cut converts into unequal selection — the driver of adverse impact and under-representation; it is an "impact"/input statistic, silent about cause (bias vs opportunity vs real).* (d) Benchmark: Roth et al. (2001) meta-analysis — **Black–White cognitive-ability d ≈ 1.0 SD** for SAT/college-application tests and applicant g (often < 1.0 with moderators; Hispanic–White smaller). **Tail-multiplication [INFERENCE]:** with two normal groups, equal SD, d = 1.0 (μ_R = 100, μ_F = 85, SD = 15) and a gifted cut at 130 (top ~2.28% of the reference group): reference selection rate 1−Φ(2.0) = 2.28%, focal 1−Φ(3.0) = 0.135%, so **impact ratio ≈ 0.0592 (5.9%)** — a modest 1-SD mean gap becomes a ~17:1 identification disparity *because the cut is in the tail*. This is why gifted cuts produce far worse disparities than the same d would at a median cut. (e) Limits: d assumes comparable scales/variances; says nothing about *why* the gap exists; conflating d with bias is a category error. — Cohen, J. (1988), *Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences* (2nd ed.) [SECONDARY]; Roth, P. L., BeVier, C. A., Bobko, P., Switzer, F. S., & Tyler, P. (2001), *Personnel Psychology* 54(2):297–330, DOI 10.1111/j.1744-6570.2001.tb00094.x [VERIFIED]; tail math [INFERENCE]. **Established (d); worked figure is our inference.**

12. **Representation Index / Relative-Risk family — the standard disproportionality metrics.** (a) **Representation Index `RI = (% of identified-gifted who are group g) / (% of total population who are group g)`**, a form of relative risk. Equivalent "risk" framing: **risk index_g = (# group g identified)/(# group g enrolled)** (the group's identification rate); **Relative Risk Ratio `RRR = risk index_g / risk index_comparison`**. (b) **RI = 1.0 proportional; RI < 0.8 = under-representation** (Wright, Ford & Young's 20% equity allowance); RI > 1.0 over-representation. (c) *Quantifies impact at the program level — the outcome most policy/GT audiences care about.* (d) Worked benchmark (Peters et al. 2019, national norms): **Black RI ≈ 0.57, Latinx ≈ 0.70, Asian ≈ 2.01, White ≈ 1.18** (i.e., Black students identified at ~57% of their population share). The special-education disproportionality literature supplies the wider family — **risk ratio, alternate & weighted risk ratio, risk difference, composition index, E-formula** — each with different denominators and small-N behavior. (e) Limits: **descriptive, not causal** (a gap is a diagnostic, not proof of bias — ties to the charter stop-condition); unstable for small subgroups; composition vs risk-ratio can disagree; choice of comparison group changes the number. — Peters, S. J., Rambo-Hernandez, K., Makel, M. C., Matthews, M. S., & Plucker, J. A. (2019), *AERA Open* 5(2):1–18, DOI 10.1177/2332858419848446 [VERIFIED]; Bollmer et al. (IDEA Data Center/OSEP), *Methods for Assessing Racial/Ethnic Disproportionality in Special Education* (ED577280) [VERIFIED]; Wright, Ford & Young (2017) [SECONDARY]. **Established.**

13. **The "excellence gap" — subgroup disparity at the *advanced* level (the right denominator for gifted ID).** (a) The difference between subgroups in the **percentage reaching the highest achievement level** (e.g., NAEP "Advanced"), as opposed to gaps at basic/proficient. (b) `excellence gap = %_group A at Advanced − %_group B at Advanced` (can also be a ratio). (c) *Quantifies the size of the talent pool disparity a gifted program is drawing from — it separates "the test under-identifies talent" (bias/impact) from "advanced-level talent is itself unequally distributed by opportunity" (an upstream reality), so it disciplines claims about what a better test can fix.* (d) Interpretation: excellence gaps are typically **wide and growing** on NAEP across race, income, ELL, and gender at grades 4 & 8; they persist (though shrink) even within SES strata. Compare a program's RI (#12) against the local excellence gap to see whether identification tracks available advanced talent. (e) Limits: depends on the (arbitrary) "advanced" threshold; NAEP proficiency-cut critiques apply; measures outcomes, not innate capacity; opportunity-confounded. — Plucker, J. A., Burroughs, N., & Song, R. (2010), *Mind the (Other) Gap!* (ED531840) [VERIFIED as report/[PUB-adjacent]]; Plucker, J. A., & Peters, S. J. (2016), *Excellence Gaps in Education*, Harvard Education Press [SECONDARY]. **Established concept.**

14. **Local (building/subgroup) norms — a lever that changes representation without changing the test.** (a) Rank examinees against a **local** reference (their school/building or subgroup) instead of, or alongside, national norms; identify the top X% locally. (b) Compare a student's percentile within building vs national percentile; use "local-OR-national" disjunctive rules. (c) *Quantifies (and improves) impact/representation: local norms neutralize between-school opportunity differences and directly raise subgroup RI (#12) — a demonstrated, mechanism-level fix for under-representation.* (d) Evidence: Peters et al. (2019, pre-registered) — **building-level norms substantially raise identified Black and Latinx representation**, largest effect in more diverse schools; effect on absolute numbers depends on the norm blend. Contrast: **universal screening alone does *not* reliably fix proportionality** (Peters, Gentry, Whiting & McBee 2019) — the *norm reference*, not just who gets tested, is the active ingredient. (e) Limits: shifts the *definition* of "gifted" to relative-to-peers (a defensible but explicit value choice — a "solution-as-requirement" risk per the charter); local norms can lower the absolute ability bar and complicate cross-site comparability; requires per-site n large enough to norm. — Peters, S. J., Rambo-Hernandez, K., Makel, M. C., Matthews, M. S., & Plucker, J. A. (2019), *AERA Open* 5(2), DOI 10.1177/2332858419848446 [VERIFIED]; Peters, Gentry, Whiting & McBee (2019), *Gifted Child Quarterly* 63(4):273–287, DOI 10.1177/0016986219833738 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

15. **Subgroup false-negative rate + conditional decision-consistency near the cut — and why you can't equalize everything (fairness-impossibility).** (a) Compute A2/A3 decision metrics **within subgroup, conditioned on the cut**: **subgroup FNR = FN_g/(TP_g+FN_g)** (miss rate among the truly gifted of group g), plus Livingston–Lewis classification accuracy/consistency restricted to near-cut examinees of group g. (b) With a common cut c, aggregate FNR_g = ∫ Φ((c−T)/SEM)·f_g(T)dT over truly-gifted T ≥ c. (c) *Quantifies the equity harm that matters most for gifted ID — capable children screened out — and whether that harm falls unequally by subgroup, which pure item-level DIF/invariance can miss.* (d) Worked [INFERENCE] (SEM = 5 score-pts, cut = 130): a truly-gifted child at true score 132 has FN prob Φ((130−132)/5) = Φ(−0.4) = **34.5%**; at 145, Φ(−3) = **0.13%**. Because a lower-mean subgroup has proportionally more of its gifted **just above** the cut (thinner upper tail), its aggregate FNR is **structurally higher** even with an unbiased test — so report FNR and near-cut consistency **by subgroup**, not just overall. (e) **Hard limit — the impossibility theorem:** Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan prove that **calibration-within-groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class cannot all hold** unless prediction is perfect or subgroup base rates are equal; Chouldechova shows the same for calibration vs equalized odds. *So you cannot simultaneously equalize FNR, equalize FPR, and keep the score equally meaningful across groups when giftedness base rates differ — fairness requires an explicit, documented choice of which criterion to satisfy.* Other limits: near-cut cells are small (noisy subgroup estimates); "truly gifted" needs a criterion (the A2 criterion problem). — mechanism/worked [INFERENCE], composing Livingston & Lewis (1995, in A3) [VERIFIED] with subgroup conditioning; Kleinberg, J., Mullainathan, S., & Raghavan, M. (2017), *ITCS 2017*, DOI 10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2017.43 (arXiv:1609.05807) [VERIFIED]; Chouldechova, A. (2017), *Big Data* 5(2):153–163, DOI 10.1089/big.2016.0047 [SECONDARY]. **Established theorem; worked figures are our inference.**

16. **The fairness framework of the 2014 Standards, ch.3 — the governing definitions.** (a) The authoritative U.S. framework (AERA/APA/NCME) that unifies fairness under validity. (b) It advances **four complementary views of fairness**: (i) **fairness as lack of measurement bias** (DIF/invariance — #1–8); (ii) **fairness as accessibility** — "the unobstructed opportunity for all examinees to demonstrate their standing on the construct"; (iii) **fairness as equitable treatment** throughout the testing process; (iv) **fairness as validity of individual score interpretations** for the intended use. Core definition (p.63): *"identify and remove construct-irrelevant barriers to maximal performance for any examinee."* **Bias** (p.216): *"construct underrepresentation or construct-irrelevant components…that differentially affect the performance of different groups."* (c) *Quantifies nothing by itself but tells you which of the three buckets a given metric addresses and forbids treating one as a substitute for the others — the governance backbone for an A5 fairness argument.* (d) Use it as a checklist: for a gifted screen, evidence is needed on measurement bias (DIF/DTF/invariance), accessibility/accommodations, process equity, and validity-of-use per subgroup; no single statistic discharges the obligation. (e) Limits: a normative/professional standard, not a statistical test; "fairness" remains multiply-defined (see #15) and the Standards deliberately do **not** pick one operational metric. — AERA, APA, & NCME (2014), *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, ch. 3 "Fairness in Testing" (pp. 49–72; definitions pp. 63, 216), ISBN 978-0-935302-35-6 [VERIFIED]. **Established (authoritative).**

**(g) Newly discovered dimensions → queue:**
- **Invariance-as-precondition gate:** make scalar (ideally strict) invariance a *decision-blocking* acceptance test before ANY cross-subgroup score comparison or shared cut — mirror A1's "audit the bank information function" gate for measurement, but for fairness.
- **Partial/approximate invariance + anchor-item purification** (alignment method, Asparouhov & Muthén) as the realistic middle path when full scalar invariance fails, with an explicit rule for how many/which parameters may be freed.
- **DIF power & purification auditing** (analogue of A4's Aguinis power critique): report detection power, matching-criterion purification passes, and multiple-comparison control, so a "no DIF" claim isn't a power failure.
- **Duality-paradox reporting rule:** every fairness claim must pair a measurement-bias result (DIF/invariance) with a prediction-bias result (Cleary, A4) and state which is prioritized — never let one stand in for the other.
- **Near-cut subgroup error-rate dashboard:** conditional FNR/FPR/decision-consistency by subgroup in a band around the cut (the honest home for an equity "2× fewer missed kids" claim), with the impossibility theorem forcing an explicit choice of fairness criterion.
- **Local-norm / RI simulation as a design knob:** simulate how norm reference (national/district/building) trades off representation (RI) against absolute-ability comparability and adverse impact — bridges to A6 utility/simulation.
- **DTF-at-the-cut:** integrate DTF (or an area measure) specifically over θ ≥ gifted cut rather than over the whole θ distribution (fairness analogue of A1 tail-restricted reliability and A4 local validity).

**(h) Gap / next:** No located source reports item-level DIF/DTF **at the gifted cut (θ ≈ 2.0–2.33)** or subgroup-conditional near-cut FNR for the specific instruments GT would deploy (CogAT, NNAT, MAP) — publisher-controlled and the most material `[UNVERIFIED]` hole in A5; next, hunt technical manuals + independent DIF re-analyses, and (if any real data appear) run the invariance→DIF→DTF→subgroup-FNR→RI pipeline end-to-end so measurement bias, prediction bias, and impact are reported as three distinct, non-substitutable numbers.

### Wave 3 · A6 — Simulation, selection-utility & benchmarking methods for evaluating/comparing identification systems (2026-07-22)

*Scope: how to SIMULATE an identification pipeline, convert a validity coefficient into expected selection payoff, statistically COMPARE two screens, put confidence bounds on classification metrics, and benchmark a NEW screen head-to-head against a status quo (e.g., CogAT). Complements A2 (classification metrics) and A1 (information/relative efficiency). A recurring theme: the selection-utility and correlated-AUC machinery is **standard in personnel psychology / diagnostic medicine but almost never applied in gifted ed** — a real opportunity. Labels: [VERIFIED] DOI/venue resolved this pass; [SECONDARY]; [PUB/COI]; [UNVERIFIED]; worked numbers [INFERENCE].*

**— Monte Carlo / analytic simulation of identification systems —**

1. **Simulation-study methodology (the general engine).** Define a data-generating model (e.g., multivariate-normal true+observed scores), a set of crossed **conditions** (reliability, validity, cutoff, base rate, selection ratio), R **replications**, and **evaluation criteria** (bias, RMSE, coverage, Type-I/power, and here: sensitivity, incorrect-ID rate, program size). Two flavors used in gifted ed: **exact numerical integration of the multivariate-normal density** (fast, deterministic — what McBee et al. use) vs. **Monte Carlo draws / rejection sampling** (needed for messy composite rules, individual-level draws, and non-normal models). *Quantifies* the expected behavior of a whole ID system before it is deployed on children — the honest way to ask "how well would this pipeline work?" without experimenting on applicants. *Compute/interpret:* report each metric as mean ± MC error across replications; use ≥1,000–10,000 reps so MC error ≪ the effect you care about. *Limitations:* garbage-in/garbage-out — results are only as good as the assumed model (normality, correct reliabilities/validities); MC error and condition coverage must be reported. — Feinberg & Rubright (2016), *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 35(2):36–49, DOI 10.1111/emip.12111 [VERIFIED]. **Established methodology; under-used as an explicit QA gate in gifted ed.**

2. **Score-combination-rule simulation: AND / OR / mean (the field-defining paper).** Models multiple correlated measures (each = true score + error) and applies three rules to a cut: **conjunctive "AND"** (must clear the cut on *every* measure), **disjunctive "OR"** (clear *any one*), **compensatory "mean"** (average clears the cut). Outputs per rule: **system sensitivity, incorrect-identification rate (IIR), expected program size, and ability heterogeneity** of those identified. *Quantifies* how the *combination rule itself* — independent of the tests — sets false-negative vs false-positive tradeoffs. *Compute/interpret:* **AND** → smallest, most "elite," fewest false positives but **most false negatives**; **OR** → largest pool, fewest false negatives but most false positives; **mean** → balanced, and (uniquely) its accuracy **improves as more tests are averaged** — sensitivity ≈ **76% / 80% / 84%** for 2 / 3 / 4 averaged tests at each-test reliability .80, whereas AND/OR do *not* improve this way. Single-assessment ceiling (their Table 1, reused in McBee 2016): at a **90th-pct** cut, sensitivity = **.843** (r=.95), **.776** (.90), **.680** (.80); at a **99th-pct** cut, **.763** (r=.95), **.665** (.90), **.530** (.80) — i.e., **even a near-perfect (.95) test at the top-1% cut misses ~24% of truly-eligible students**, and AND-gates multiply these misses. *Limitations:* assumes multivariate normality and known reliabilities/validities; "truly gifted" is defined by a true-score cut (criterion is model-internal, not external). — McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014), *Gifted Child Quarterly* 58(1):69–89, DOI 10.1177/0016986213513794 [VERIFIED]. **Established/influential in gifted ed.**

3. **Nomination-/screening-stage two-stage simulation (the false-economy result).** A 4-variable multivariate-normal model (nomination true & observed, confirmatory-test true & observed) with disattenuated cross-correlations; sensitivity, IIR, PPV, and program size obtained by numerically integrating the density (R `mnormt::sadmvn`). *Quantifies* how much a Stage-1 screen/nomination **degrades** the whole pipeline. *Compute/interpret:* **a screener can only lower system sensitivity** (a gate gifted kids must also clear); the key levers are **nomination validity and nomination cutoff** (nomination *reliability* matters only by capping validity). Under common practice (high nomination cutoffs), **false-negative rate easily exceeds 60%**; worked published example — **SIGS nomination + CogAT confirmation at a 90th-pct nomination cutoff → system sensitivity .28 (72% of eligible students missed)** vs .843 with no screener. The **"two-stage ROC" is boxed**: the confirmatory test protects against false positives but gives *no* protection against false negatives created upstream, so the "optimal" nomination cutoff is the *lowest* one (screening is justified only on cost/values grounds, not psychometrics). Their Table 6/Figure 8 give the max nomination cutoff that holds relative-sensitivity loss to ≤2/5/10/20%. *Limitations:* normality; treats teacher judgment as a latent normal variable with a "reliability/validity." — McBee, Peters & Miller (2016), *Gifted Child Quarterly* 60(4):258–278, DOI 10.1177/0016986216656256 [VERIFIED]. **Established/influential.**

4. **Individual-level identification probability & the conditional distribution of the identified (giftedCalcs).** Extends #2–#3 from *system* marginals to the *individual*: P(identified | true score t) = `∫_τ^∞ N(√ρ_tt·t, 1−ρ_tt) dX` for a single-stage system (observed score ~ Normal with mean `√ρ_tt·t`, variance `1−ρ_tt`); derives the conditional pdf/cdf/quantile and moments of true & observed scores *among identified students*, with random draws via **rejection sampling**. *Quantifies* "what is *this* child's probability of being correctly identified," and what the identified pool actually looks like (e.g., how far above the cut, how much regression). *Compute/interpret:* a very-high-ability child can have high P(ID) even in a low-sensitivity system, while a borderline child has low P(ID) even in a decent one — the tool for auditing *who* the system reliably catches vs. gambles on (and, if extended with subgroup means, where bias concentrates). Implemented in the **`giftedCalcs` R package + Shiny app**. *Limitations:* same normality assumptions; **publication venue not confirmed this pass** (circulated as a working paper/preprint), so treat the write-up as [SECONDARY] even though the package is a concrete artifact. — McBee, Peters & Godkin, "A Method and R Package for Calculating the Conditional Distribution of Identified Students Under One- and Two-Stage Identification Systems" (working paper/preprint; `giftedCalcs`) [SECONDARY — formula read directly this pass; venue [UNVERIFIED]].

5. **Real-data replication of combination-rule simulation (external validity check).** Applies the AND/OR/mean framework to the **CogAT Form 7 national standardization data (Grades K–5)** rather than a pure simulation. *Quantifies* whether the simulated rule effects survive on real, non-idealized score distributions. *Compute/interpret:* rules changed the **number and diversity** of identified students, **but the diversity differences were driven mainly by the identified pool size, not by the combination rule per se** — a caution against expecting a clever rule (rather than a bigger pool / lower cut) to fix representation. *Limitations:* one instrument's norm sample; diversity ≠ validity. — Lakin (2018), *Gifted Child Quarterly* 62(2):210–219, DOI 10.1177/0016986217752099 [VERIFIED]. **Established.** *(Companion: McBee & Makel (2019), "The Quantitative Implications of Definitions of Giftedness," AERA Open 5(1), DOI 10.1177/2332858419831007 — simulates how definition choice drives incidence, with some definitions implying >75% "gifted" [VERIFIED]; useful for benchmarking what a screen is even trying to detect.)*

**— Selection-utility models (validity → expected selection payoff) —**

6. **Taylor–Russell tables (success ratio at a cut).** Inputs: **base rate** (proportion of applicants who would "succeed"/are truly eligible), **selection ratio** SR (proportion selected), and **validity** r; output from the bivariate-normal model = the **success ratio** = P(true success | selected) — the PPV analog, and the basis of expectancy tables. *Quantifies* how much a valid predictor raises the hit-rate among selectees above the base rate. *Compute/interpret:* success ratio rises with r and (usually) with a **smaller** SR; the *incremental gain over the base rate* is largest at **moderate base rates + selective SR + moderate-to-high r**. Illustrative [INFERENCE]: base rate .60, SR .30, r=.70 → ≈**91%** of selectees successful (vs 60% by chance); but a stringent gifted-style cut (low SR) with modest r yields **high PPV yet low sensitivity** (many eligible missed — read alongside Meehl & Rosen base-rate math, A2). *Limitations:* **dichotomizes a continuous criterion** (success/fail); assumes bivariate normality, linearity, a fixed cut. — Taylor & Russell (1939), *Journal of Applied Psychology* 23(5):565–578, DOI 10.1037/h0057079 [VERIFIED]. **Established in personnel selection; rarely applied in gifted ed.**

7. **Naylor–Shine tables (expected criterion *gain*, criterion kept continuous).** Standardized mean-criterion gain of the selected group: `Z̄_c = r · (φ(x_c)/SR)`, where `φ(x_c)` = normal ordinate at the cutoff and `φ(x_c)/SR` = the **selected-group mean predictor z** (inverse Mills ratio). *Quantifies* how many criterion SDs the selected cohort gains from using a valid selector — better than Taylor–Russell when the outcome is continuous (achievement growth, later accomplishment). *Compute/interpret* [INFERENCE, formula verified]: SR=.20 (cut z=0.84) → selected mean z=1.40; with r=.35, gain = .35×1.40 = **0.49 SD**. SR=.10 (cut z=1.28) → selected mean z=1.755; with r=.70, gain = **1.23 SD**. SR=.05 (cut z=1.645) → selected mean z=2.06; r=.70 → **1.44 SD**. So payoff scales with **validity × selectivity**. *Limitations:* assumes linearity/normality and a top-down cut; expresses gain only in SD units (no dollars/decision value until multiplied by SD_y — see #8). — Naylor & Shine (1965), "A table for determining the increase in mean criterion score obtained by using a selection device," *Journal of Industrial Psychology* 3:33–42 [VERIFIED via multiple secondary sources; no DOI resolved this pass]. **Established; essentially unused in gifted ed.**

8. **Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser (BCG) utility (validity → value).** Per-selectee utility `= r_xy · SD_y · Z̄_s`; total incremental utility `ΔU = N_s · T · r_xy · SD_y · Z̄_s − C`, where SD_y = SD of outcome *value*, Z̄_s = selected-group mean predictor z (= φ(x_c)/SR), N_s = number selected, T = tenure/horizon, C = program cost; for a **new** selector replacing an operating one, substitute `(r_new − r_baseline)` for r_xy. *Quantifies* **utility is linear in validity** — even a small validity gain, multiplied across a large selective cohort and a multi-year horizon, is worth a lot. *Compute/interpret* [INFERENCE]: replace a status-quo screen (r=.45) with a new one (r=.60), Δr=.15, at SR=.10 (Z̄_s=1.755), SD_y in outcome-SD units (=1): incremental gain = .15×1×1.755 = **0.263 outcome-SD per selectee**; over N_s=100, T=3 → **≈79 outcome-SD-years**, minus cost. *Limitations:* **estimating SD_y is notoriously contested** (dollar value of a gifted-program outcome is ill-defined); linear-utility and top-down-selection assumptions; utility ≠ program *impact* (a valid selector predicts standing, not that the program *caused* gains). — Brogden (1949), "When testing pays off," *Personnel Psychology* 2:171–183 [VERIFIED venue/pages; DOI not resolved this pass]; Cronbach & Gleser (1965), *Psychological Tests and Personnel Decisions*, 2nd ed., Univ. of Illinois Press, ISBN 0-252-00003-X, LCCN 64018667 [VERIFIED]; popularized by Schmidt, Hunter, McKenzie & Muldrow (1979), DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.64.6.609 [VERIFIED prior wave]. **Established (model); SD_y contested; unused in gifted ed.**

9. **Expectancy tables / charts (stakeholder-facing translation).** A graphic/tabular display of P(criterion success | score band). **Institutional** expectancy = % of a group at/above a score who succeed; **individual** expectancy = a single applicant's success probability at his/her score. Built **empirically** (from observed data) or **theoretically** (from the bivariate-normal model, i.e., a graphical Taylor–Russell). *Quantifies* validity in a form families/administrators can read ("a child scoring in this band has an X% chance of thriving in the program"). *Compute/interpret:* theoretical (bivariate-normal) expectancies were shown to match empirical ones across 15 datasets (Tiffin & Vincent 1960), so they can be drawn from r + score distribution when local outcome data are thin. *Limitations:* success is dichotomized; can imply more precision than a fallible cut warrants; interpretability depends on framing (frequencies vs probabilities). — Lawshe & Bolda (1958), "Expectancy Charts: I. Their Use and Empirical Development," *Personnel Psychology* 11(3):353–365, DOI 10.1111/j.1744-6570.1958.tb00023.x [VERIFIED]; Lawshe, Bolda, Brune & Auclair (1958), "Expectancy Charts II," *Personnel Psychology* 11(4):545–559, DOI 10.1111/j.1744-6570.1958.tb00040.x [VERIFIED]; Tiffin & Vincent (1960), DOI 10.1111/j.1744-6570.1960.tb01517.x [VERIFIED]. **Established communication tool.**

**— Comparing two tests head-to-head —**

10. **DeLong test for two *correlated* AUCs (the modern default).** Nonparametric comparison of the AUCs of two screens **measured on the same students**, using generalized U-statistics / placement (structural) components to estimate the covariance, then `z = (AUC_A − AUC_B) / SE_diff` with `SE_diff² = Var_A + Var_B − 2·Cov_AB`. *Quantifies* whether a new screen's overall discrimination of gifted vs non-gifted **significantly** beats CogAT's, *properly crediting the paired design*. *Compute/interpret* [INFERENCE]: AUC_new=.86, AUC_CogAT=.82, positive Cov because same kids → SE_diff≈.02 → z≈2.0 (p≈.05, significant); if wrongly analyzed as independent samples, SE_diff≈.028 → z≈1.4 (n.s.) — ignoring the correlation **throws away power**. Report the AUC difference **with a CI**, not just p. *Limitations:* AUC is an omnibus, threshold-free summary — two screens can share an AUC yet differ sharply **at the gifted cut** (pair with partial-AUC and tail relative efficiency, #12); needs a defensible external gifted criterion (the A2 gap). — DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson (1988), *Biometrics* 44(3):837–845, DOI 10.2307/2531595, PMID 3203132 [VERIFIED]. **Standard in diagnostic medicine; rarely applied in gifted ed.**

11. **Hanley–McNeil paired-AUC test (the parametric predecessor).** Same paired question, closed-form: derives `Var(AUC)` from the Q1, Q2 quantities and a table converting the within-group rating correlations (`r_N` among non-cases, `r_A` among cases) into a between-area correlation `r`, giving `SE_diff = √(SE_A² + SE_B² − 2·r·SE_A·SE_B)`. *Quantifies* the same power gain from pairing (analogous to a paired t-test) and is convenient for **prospective sample-size/power planning** of a head-to-head study. *Compute/interpret:* positive `r` shrinks SE_diff → higher power; the closed form makes it the natural tool to answer "how many students must I test to detect a .04 AUC edge over CogAT?" *Limitations:* assumes binormal ROC and ordinal ratings; DeLong (#10) is the more general modern choice. — Hanley & McNeil (1983), *Radiology* 148(3):839–843, DOI 10.1148/radiology.148.3.6878708, PMID 6878708 [VERIFIED]. **Standard reference; rarely applied in gifted ed.**

12. **Relative efficiency of test information functions (the tail-honest, simulation-free comparison; cross-ref A1).** `RE(θ) = I_A(θ) / I_B(θ)`. *Quantifies* how much more measurement information a candidate screen carries **at the gifted cut θ_cut** than CogAT — the rigorous home for a "2× better at the top" claim, unbounded and cut-specific (unlike AUC/reliability which are capped and population-averaged). *Compute/interpret:* `RE(2.33)=2` ⇒ screen A extracts twice the information at the top-1% cut (B needs ~2× the items to match); compare TIFs at θ=2.05 (top 2%) / 2.33 (top 1%) rather than headline reliability or omnibus AUC. Requires both tests on the **same equated θ metric**. *Limitations:* nominal information overstates real precision when item parameters are uncertain and the bank is sparse in the tail (A1 caveats); says nothing about *validity* against an external criterion. — van der Linden (1984), *Statistica Neerlandica*, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9574.1984.tb01101.x [VERIFIED prior wave]; Lord (1980), *Applications of IRT to Practical Testing Problems* [VERIFIED prior wave]. **Established (IRT); the cleanest "which test is better for the top 1–2%" comparison.**

13. **Bootstrapping / resampling CIs for classification metrics (sensitivity, PPV, kappa, AUC).** Resample the validation sample **with replacement** B times, recompute the statistic `θ̂*` each time, and read a CI from the bootstrap distribution — **percentile** CI, or **BCa** (bias-corrected & accelerated) when the distribution is skewed or the estimator biased (kappa, PPV at low base rate, AUC are all skewed). *Quantifies* the **sampling uncertainty** in every classification number a gifted-ID audit reports — essential because the "truly gifted" tail sample is tiny. *Compute/interpret:* use **B ≥ 2,000** for percentile, **≥ 5,000** for BCa; report the metric **with its 95% CI**. Illustrative [INFERENCE]: with only ~30 confirmed-eligible students, an observed **sensitivity of .80** carries a 95% CI of roughly **[.61, .92]** — far too wide to certify a screen from a small pilot. Standard implementations: R **`boot::boot.ci`** (percentile/BCa) and **`pROC`** (DeLong *or* bootstrap CIs and tests for AUC/partial-AUC, using the fast Sun & Xu 2014 DeLong algorithm). *Limitations:* bootstrap inherits the sample's biases; unstable when the positive class is very small; BCa needs enough replicates. — Efron (1979), *Annals of Statistics* 7(1):1–26, DOI 10.1214/aos/1176344552 [VERIFIED]; Carpenter & Bithell (2000), *Statistics in Medicine* 19(9):1141–1164, DOI 10.1002/(SICI)1097-0258(20000515)19:9<1141::AID-SIM479>3.0.CO;2-F [VERIFIED]; Robin et al. (2011) `pROC`, *BMC Bioinformatics* 12:77, DOI 10.1186/1471-2105-12-77 [VERIFIED]. **Established in biostatistics; under-used in gifted-ID reporting.**

**— System-level stability, benchmarking & pipeline evaluation —**

14. **Regression-to-the-mean / one-shot-instability simulation (why a single test date mis-identifies).** Predicted retest standing `ẑ₂ = r₁₂ · z₁`, so an extreme first score is expected to fall back toward the mean by a factor set by the **occasion-to-occasion correlation r₁₂** — which is lowered not only by measurement error but by **real growth differences, developmental score-scale changes, and shifting norm cohorts**. *Quantifies* how much "giftedness" measured on one day is an artifact of that day. *Compute/interpret:* Lohman & Korb show **~half of students in the top 3% (ITBS) one year are not in the top 3% the next**, even with highly reliable tests; only ~**45%** of those scoring 130+ on one IQ test hit 130+ on another concurrently, and ~**25%** of 4-year-olds at 130+ would score 130+ at age 17. Worked [INFERENCE]: with occasion correlation r=.85, a child exactly at the **99th-pct** cut (z=2.33) has predicted retest z=1.98 and only a **~25%** chance of re-clearing the 99th-pct cut on an equally good retest (residual SD=√(1−.85²)=.527). *Design implication:* single-administration hard cuts guarantee large, largely-illusory turnover; **combine ability + achievement across occasions** to damp regression and use confidence bands, not a knife-edge cut. *Limitations:* it's an illustration of a statistical law, not a bias claim about any one test; the "right" amount of retesting is a values/cost tradeoff. — Lohman & Korb (2006), "Gifted Today but Not Tomorrow?", *Journal for the Education of the Gifted* 29(4):451–484, DOI 10.4219/jeg-2006-245 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

15. **System-level benchmarking of a new screen vs the status quo — the empirical A/B + the field's evaluation rubric.** Two complementary tools. **(a) Natural-experiment benchmark (Card & Giuliano 2016):** a district switched from parent/teacher **referral** to **universal screening** (NNAT for all 2nd graders) with *no change in the eligibility standard*, and identification **yield & equity** jumped — **+174–180%** gifted rate among disadvantaged students, **+118–130%** Hispanic, **+74–80%** Black; Black, Hispanic, FRL, ELL, and female students had been systematically **"under-referred."** *Quantifies* the real-world effect of changing the *screen/front-door*, holding standards fixed — the cleanest available benchmark design for "what does a new screen actually do to who gets in." **(b) System-evaluation rubric (CASA):** score a whole ID pipeline on **Cost** (resources per correctly identified student), **Alignment** (does the measure match the service to be provided), **Sensitivity** (% of eligible correctly identified — ties directly to McBee #2–#4), and **Access** (universal vs referral-gated, and burden). *Compute/interpret:* CASA is a **multi-criterion scorecard, not a single number** — a screen that wins on Sensitivity but fails on Access/Cost is not obviously better; use it to structure a head-to-head. *Limitations:* Card–Giuliano is one district (quasi-experimental, not an RCT; generalizability limited); CASA is a qualitative framework requiring locally-defined thresholds to become quantitative. — Card & Giuliano (2016), *PNAS* 113(48):13678–13683, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1605043113 (NBER WP 21519, DOI 10.3386/w21519) [VERIFIED]; Peters, Stambaugh, Makel, Lee, McBee, McCoach & Johnson (2022/2023), "The CASA Criteria…," *Gifted Child Quarterly* 67(2):137–150, DOI 10.1177/00169862221124887 [VERIFIED] (applied guide: Stambaugh et al. 2024, DOI 10.1177/10762175241263983; Makel et al. 2024, DOI 10.1177/10762175231222300 [SECONDARY]). **Established; CASA is the field's emerging benchmark language.**

16. **Synthesis — a decision-blocking protocol to benchmark a NEW screen head-to-head vs CogAT.** Compose the verified pieces: (i) **simulate first** (Feinberg–Rubright #1 + McBee framework #2–#4) — model the *whole pipeline* (screen → any nomination gate → composite rule → cut) and read out **system sensitivity, incorrect-ID rate, program size, and individual/subgroup P(ID)** *before* touching applicants; kill AND-gates and high nomination cutoffs that push FNR>60% (#3); (ii) **compare precision at the cut**, not omnibus, via **relative efficiency of the TIFs at θ=2.05/2.33** (#12) — the honest home for any "better at the top" claim; (iii) **compare discrimination** on the **same students** with **DeLong** (#10), reporting the **AUC difference + CI**, and use **partial-AUC / operating-point** tests near the gifted region; (iv) **bound every metric** (sensitivity, PPV, kappa, ΔAUC) with **bootstrap/BCa CIs** (#13) — a small tail sample can't certify a screen; (v) **translate validity into payoff** with **Naylor–Shine / Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser** (#7–#8) using `(r_new − r_CogAT)` and **expectancy charts** (#9) for stakeholders; (vi) **stress-test stability** with a regression-to-the-mean simulation (#14) and prefer multi-occasion/multi-measure rules; (vii) **score the whole system on CASA** and, where possible, run a **universal-screening-style yield/equity benchmark** (#15). *Report as:* not one headline number but a **panel** — {tail relative efficiency, ΔAUC±CI, system sensitivity & subgroup FNR, incremental utility, CASA scorecard}. — composition of the verified sources above; the composition itself is [INFERENCE]. **Established components; novel integration; largely absent from current gifted-ID practice.**

**(g) Newly discovered dimensions worth adding to the queue:**
- **Partial-AUC and operating-point comparison at the gifted region** (compare screens only over sensitivity/FPR ranges relevant to a top-1–2% cut; `pROC` supports pAUC + its own tests) — ROC/AUC's tail analog, more decision-relevant than whole-curve AUC.
- **Individual & subgroup identification-probability auditing** (giftedCalcs #4 extended with subgroup true-score means) → a *quantitative equity* map of exactly whose P(ID) the pipeline sacrifices — bridges A5.
- **`SD_y` / value-of-outcome estimation for gifted programs** as its own open problem (the missing multiplier that turns Naylor–Shine SD-gains into BCG decision value; contested even in personnel psych).
- **Simulation-based power/sample-size planning for a head-to-head screen study** (Hanley–McNeil closed form #11 + Monte Carlo #1): "how many students to detect a Δr/ΔAUC over CogAT?" — a prerequisite the field never states.
- **Cross-validation / optimism-shrinkage for classification metrics** (apparent sensitivity/PPV/AUC of a *newly fit* screen are optimistic; need bootstrap-.632+ or k-fold correction) — not covered by resampling *CIs* alone.
- **Bayesian / probabilistic ID (calibrated "gifted probability" + Brier/calibration)** instead of hard cuts, feeding decision-curve/net-benefit (A2 #16) — turns the whole pipeline into a calibrated-risk system.
- **Definition-sensitivity simulation** (McBee & Makel 2019): benchmark *what* the screen targets, since incidence swings wildly (some definitions imply >75% "gifted").
- **`giftedCalcs`/`pROC`/`boot` as a concrete prototype stack** for the 4-week deliverable's "one power/precision scenario" and "eligibility agreement" requirements (`docs/research/METRICS_AND_GUARDRAILS_LIBRARY.md`).

**(h) Gaps / next:** No located study reports a **head-to-head, paired DeLong/pAUC comparison of a candidate screen vs CogAT against a construct-distinct external gifted criterion**, nor a published **selection-utility (Naylor–Shine/BCG) analysis in gifted ed** — both are standard elsewhere but essentially absent here; next, (1) hunt any gifted-ID dataset with paired screen+CogAT scores and an external outcome to run DeLong + bootstrap CIs, and (2) spec a Monte-Carlo + giftedCalcs prototype that outputs the #16 benchmarking panel (tail RE, ΔAUC±CI, subgroup FNR, incremental utility, CASA) for a simulated CogAT-vs-new-screen comparison.

### Wave 4 · A7 — Standard-setting & cut-score methods for the gifted decision (2026-07-22)

*Scope:* the machinery for **setting** a top‑1–2% gifted cut (test‑centered, examinee‑centered, compromise, IRT, norm/criterion) and for **evaluating** a cut's quality/defensibility (standard error of the cut, decision accuracy/consistency of the specific cut, SEM/SED bands, combination‑rule interactions, outcome‑anchored optima, and validity‑of‑standard‑setting frameworks) — treating every cut as a value‑laden policy choice, not a discovered fact.

1. **Angoff & modified/yes‑no/extended Angoff (test‑centered).** (a) Panelists estimate, per item, the probability a "just‑barely‑gifted" borderline examinee answers correctly; the summed item probabilities are the raw cut. *Modified* Angoff adds ≥2 rounds with empirical item p‑values, impact data, and discussion; the *yes/no* variant (Impara & Plake) records 1/0 per item; *extended* Angoff generalizes to polytomous/constructed‑response items via expected score points. (b) `Cut = Σᵢ p̄ᵢ` (p̄ᵢ = mean judge probability); yes/no `Cut = Σᵢ 1{expected correct}`; extended `Cut = Σᵢ E[scoreᵢ | borderline]`. (c) *Quantifies the minimum total‑test performance a borderline‑gifted child should exhibit, operationalizing the top‑1–2% threshold in item‑performance terms rather than by percentile fiat.* (d) For a top‑1% target the "borderline" examinee must be conceived at ≈98th–99th percentile — a very demanding conception; SE of the cut = SE of the mean judge rating (see #10). (e) Judges estimate item difficulty poorly and regress toward the middle (cut set too low for an excellence target); the borderline conception is unstable and Shepard called Angoff "fundamentally flawed"; defining a "barely gifted" child is itself a policy act. — Angoff, W. H. (1971), *Educational Measurement (2nd ed.)*, Thorndike (Ed.), pp. 508–600 (yes/no footnote p. 514), ACE, no DOI [SECONDARY]; Impara & Plake (1997), *J. Educational Measurement* 34(4):353–366, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1997.tb00523.x [VERIFIED]. **Established (yes/no & modified established; borderline‑at‑the‑tail conception contested).**

2. **Nedelsky & Ebel (older test‑centered probabilistic).** (a) *Nedelsky* (MC only): judge marks distractors a borderline examinee can eliminate; item probability = reciprocal of remaining options; cut = sum. *Ebel*: judge classifies each item into a 3×4 difficulty×relevance grid and estimates % correct per cell. (b) Nedelsky `Pᵢ = 1/(remaining options)`, `Cut = Σ Pᵢ`; Ebel `Cut = Σ_cells (n_cell · p_cell)/N`. (c) *Nedelsky quantifies a guessing‑corrected minimal‑competence floor; Ebel quantifies a relevance‑weighted expected proportion* — both aim at minimal competence, so they are structurally mismatched to a top‑1–2% excellence cut. (d) Nedelsky yields only coarse probabilities (1, ½, ⅓, ¼…) bounded below by guessing; Ebel exposes test‑construction flaws (why include "questionable" items?). (e) Both dated; Berk notes difficulty/relevance are confounded in Ebel; Nedelsky's restricted probability scale is a documented weakness (see #10). — Nedelsky, L. (1954), *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 14(1):3–19, DOI 10.1177/001316445401400101 [VERIFIED]; Ebel, R. L. (1972), *Essentials of Educational Measurement*, Prentice‑Hall (ERIC ED073168), no DOI [SECONDARY]. **Established but dated (contested for excellence cuts).**

3. **Bookmark + the RP67 vs RP50 vs RP80 issue.** (a) Items are placed in an Ordered Item Booklet (OIB) by IRT difficulty; a panelist places a "bookmark" at the hardest item a borderline‑gifted student would still master with probability ≥ RP; the θ (and mapped scale score) at that location is the cut. (b) A dichotomous item's θ‑location for a given RP (Rasch) is `θ = b + ln(RP/(1−RP))`: RP50→ `θ=b`; RP67→ `θ=b+0.708` logits; RP80→ `θ=b+1.386` logits. Because a higher RP assigns each item a *higher* θ location, **raising RP raises the mapped cut** for the same bookmark placement — and panelists do not internally re‑adjust when RP changes (a validity threat). (c) *Quantifies the ability location on the IRT scale at which a borderline‑gifted student has an RP chance of mastering items, tying the gifted cut directly to θ and the tail of the test information function (cross‑ref A1).* (d) RP67 is the de‑facto convention; Huynh argued .67 maximizes Fisher information "allotted to a correct" Rasch response — but Baldwin (2018) shows that derivation is mathematically wrong (Rasch item information is maximized at P=.50, θ=b) and that info for correct/incorrect/overall is identical. Sparse very‑hard items at the top produce large OIB "gaps," destabilizing a top‑1% bookmark. (e) Documented bias toward lower‑than‑intended standards; RP rationale contested. — Mitzel, Lewis, Patz & Green (2001), in Cizek (Ed.) *Setting Performance Standards*, Erlbaum, no DOI [SECONDARY]; Karantonis & Sireci (2006), *Educational Measurement: Issues & Practice* 25(1):4–12, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00047.x [VERIFIED]; Huynh (2006), *EM:IP* 25(2):19–20, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00053.x [VERIFIED]; Baldwin (2018), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 43(6):481–492, DOI 10.1177/0146621618800272 [VERIFIED]. **Established method; RP67 information‑maximization rationale Contested.**

4. **Item‑Descriptor (ID) Matching.** (a) IRT item‑map method: panelists match each item (by the knowledge/skills it *requires*) to Performance‑Level Descriptors and locate the "threshold region" of the OIB where matches switch from the lower to the higher level; the cut is placed in that region — no probability judgments. (b) Cut = scale location of the switch region (median/midpoint of the threshold zone). (c) *Quantifies the point on the scale where item cognitive demands transition into the "gifted" descriptor — a content‑referenced cut anchored to PLDs rather than to percentiles.* (d) Aligns the task with teachers' content expertise (no error‑prone probability guesses); needs well‑written, discriminating "gifted" PLDs. (e) Threshold region can be wide; depends on tail calibration quality; writing a defensible "gifted" PLD is hard. — Ferrara, Perie & Johnson (2008), *J. Applied Testing Technology* 9(1):1–22 (official ATP/JATT PDF) [VERIFIED]; Ferrara & Lewis (2012), in Cizek (Ed.) *Setting Performance Standards (2nd ed.)*, Routledge, pp. 255–280 [SECONDARY]. **Established (emerging).**

5. **Mapmark (with domains).** (a) Bookmark descendant built by ACT for NAGB/NAEP (2005 Grade‑12 math): adds spatially representative item maps, content "domains," and domain‑level holistic feedback (Reckase‑chart style) so panelists see expected mastery by sub‑area at each candidate cut. (b) Same OIB/RP mechanics as Bookmark plus domain expected‑percent‑correct feedback across rounds. (c) *Quantifies the cut while showing, domain‑by‑domain, what a borderline‑gifted student is expected to master — strengthening the substantive defensibility of the top‑1–2% line.* (d) Multiple studies precede the operational meeting (domain development, field trial); evidence to date is procedural‑validity evidence. (e) Complex; still inherits RP‑choice and OIB item‑density problems at the tail. — Schulz & Mitzel (2005), *The Mapmark Standard Setting Method* (ERIC ED490643; NAGB) [VERIFIED via official ERIC/NAGB]; Schulz & Mitzel (2009), NAGB implementation chapter [SECONDARY]. **Established (specialized/NAEP).**

6. **Contrasting Groups & Borderline Group (examinee‑centered).** (a) *Contrasting Groups*: independently classify students as gifted/not (teacher judgment or other criteria), then find the score that best separates the two distributions. *Borderline Group*: identify students who best fit a "borderline‑gifted" definition and set the cut at that group's median. (b) Contrasting groups: fit `P(gifted | score)` (e.g., logistic) and set cut where `P = .50`, or at the crossover of the two score densities; raise the cut to reduce false‑gifted. Borderline: `Cut = median(borderline group)`. (c) *Quantifies the score that empirically best distinguishes externally‑judged gifted from non‑gifted (contrasting) or the typical score of a just‑barely‑gifted child (borderline) — a criterion‑referenced cut that maps straight onto sensitivity/specificity (cross‑ref A2).* (d) The .50 cut balances FP/FN; shift upward for a stricter top‑1%. Because the "truth" is human classification, teacher‑nomination bias can import a subgroup gap — which is a **diagnostic, not proof of bias** (guardrail). (e) Borderline groups are tiny at the extreme (unstable median); contrasting groups need a credible, unbiased external label and enough gifted cases at the tail. — Livingston & Zieky (1982), *Passing Scores: A Manual for Setting Standards…*, ETS (official ETS page; ERIC ED227113), no DOI [VERIFIED]; cf. Berk known‑groups. **Established.**

7. **Body of Work (holistic examinee‑product method).** (a) Panelists sort *whole* student work‑samples/score‑profiles into performance categories in two stages — range‑finding (sparse sampling to bracket the cut) then pinpointing (dense sampling near the cut) — and logistic regression locates the cut where `P(higher category) = .50`. (b) `Cut = x s.t. logit P(advanced|x)=0`. (c) *Quantifies a defensible cut for multi‑format/portfolio evidence of giftedness — relevant if the adaptive test yields rich profiles rather than a single score.* (d) Uses real student products (easy, transparent task for panelists). (e) Needs large quantities of scored student work spanning the range; heavy preparation; ill‑suited to a single scaled score. — Kingston, Kahl, Sweeney & Bay (2001), in Cizek (Ed.) *Setting Performance Standards*, Erlbaum (TOC verified; critique DOI 10.55016/ojs/ajer.v52i1.55112) [SECONDARY]. **Established.**

8. **Compromise methods — Hofstee & Beuk.** (a) Explicitly reconcile absolute (content) and relative (norm/rate) standards. *Hofstee*: judges give minimum/maximum acceptable cut (C_min, C_max) and minimum/maximum acceptable failure rate (F_min, F_max); the line through (F_min, C_max) and (F_max, C_min) is intersected with the empirical cumulative score curve. *Beuk*: uses the mean Angoff cut and mean acceptable pass rate as an anchor point and draws a line whose slope = SD(cut judgments)/SD(pass‑rate judgments), intersected with the score distribution. (b) Hofstee: `F_line(c) = F_max − [(F_max−F_min)/(C_max−C_min)]·(c − C_min)`, cut where `F_line(c) = F_emp(c)` (Hofstee's own form: locus `k + a·f = c`). (c) *Quantifies a cut that explicitly bounds how many students can be selected — directly encoding the top‑1–2% policy target and its capacity/impact constraints.* (d) Makes the norm‑referenced nature of "top 1–2%" explicit (a policy choice, per guardrail); Hofstee can stand alone or adjust an Angoff cut; Beuk is a "reality check" on Angoff and needs real score data. (e) No solution if the admissible line misses the score curve; Beuk's slope‑as‑emphasis assumption is empirically contested (Wyse); heavily norm‑driven, so not a pure competence standard. — Hofstee, W. K. B. (1983), in Anderson & Helmick (Eds.) *On Educational Testing*, Jossey‑Bass, pp. 109–127 (primary text reproduced online), no DOI [SECONDARY]; Beuk, C. H. (1984), *J. Educational Measurement* 21(2):147–152, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1984.tb00226.x [VERIFIED]. **Established; Beuk slope‑weighting Contested.**

9. **IRT‑based cut: θ_cut, scale mapping, SE(θ_cut), and tail information.** (a) Set the cut on the latent scale (θ_cut), then map to the reported scale score; the *precision* of the cut is governed by the test information function I(θ) at θ_cut. (b) `CSEM(θ) = 1/√I(θ)`; scale‑score cut `= A·θ_cut + B` (linear) or via the test characteristic curve; the location error of a judgmental cut near θ_cut scales with OIB item spacing / local information; `SE(θ_cut)` combines panelist variance **and** measurement error. (c) *Quantifies how sharply a test can defend a top‑1% line: at the extreme tail I(θ) is typically small, so CSEM is large and the cut is intrinsically fuzzy (cross‑ref A1).* (d) Reckase's psychometric theory of standard setting judges a method by whether it recovers panelists' *intended* θ; misclassification is minimized where information is highest — but for gifted that maximum sits mid‑scale, so the tail cut is the hardest to set precisely. **This is the crux of any "≈2× better than CogAT" claim**: an adaptive test can concentrate information at the top‑1–2% θ, shrinking CSEM(θ_cut) and decision error *there* — quantifiable as a relative‑efficiency ratio at θ_cut (cross‑ref A1). (e) Depends on model fit and calibration in the sparse tail; extrapolation risk. — Reckase (2006), *EM:IP* 25(2):4–18, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00052.x [VERIFIED]; numeric mapping example [INFERENCE]; cross‑ref A1. **Established (functioning criteria); some numerics [INFERENCE].**

10. **Standard error of the cut score (three sources) + generalizability of the cut.** (a) The cut carries sampling error from (1) the panelist/rater sample, (2) measurement error (SEM/CSEM), and (3) generalizability across occasions/methods; G‑theory decomposes a rater×item design into a defensible SE of the cut. (b) Rater facet: `SE(cut) ≈ SD(judge cuts)/√(n_judges)`; a G‑study yields `σ(cut)` from variance components (raters, items, rounds); the full defensibility band widens once method variance is added. (c) *Quantifies how reproducible the top‑1% cut is — how far the line would move with a different panel, occasion, or method.* (d) Report the SE and a band; if the band spans a wide slice of the tail, "1%" is statistically indistinguishable from ~0.7–1.5%. (e) The rater‑only SE understates true uncertainty because it ignores method variance (different methods give different cuts — the Glass critique). — Brennan & Lockwood (1980), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 4(2):219–240, DOI 10.1177/014662168000400209 [VERIFIED]; Cizek & Bunch (2007), *Standard Setting: A Guide…*, Sage (SAGE catalog; ERIC ED500311), no DOI [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

11. **Decision accuracy & consistency of a *specific* cut (evaluating the cut).** (a) Given a chosen cut, evaluate its classification quality: **decision accuracy (DA)** = agreement of observed with true‑score classification; **decision consistency (DC)** = agreement across parallel forms. Livingston–Lewis (4‑parameter‑beta true score + binomial error on *effective* length; any scoring incl. composites); Hanson–Brennan (more general strong‑true‑score models — check fit before trusting indices); Rudner (IRT‑based expected FP/FN using CSEM and the ability distribution). (b) LL effective length `n' = [(X̄−X_min)(X_max−X̄) − r·SD²] / [SD²·(1−r)]`; `DA = Σ P(same class: true vs observed)`, `DC = Σ P(same class on two forms | conditional independence)`; Rudner FP/FN integrate the ability density around the cut with `se(θ)=1/√I(θ)`. (c) *Quantifies, for THIS top‑1% cut, the expected false‑gifted (FP) and missed‑gifted (FN) rates and how stably the same child would be re‑classified — the direct "how good is the cut" metric (cross‑ref A2/A3).* (d) At an extreme cut the ≈1–2% base rate makes PPV fragile and DC low even at high reliability, so report **cut‑specific** DA/DC/κ, not overall reliability; Rudner's illustration has ~15% of true non‑masters misclassified near a cut — acceptability is a judgment. (e) Model‑dependent (beta‑binomial can misfit the tail — hence Hanson–Brennan); assumes unidimensional error. — Livingston & Lewis (1995), *J. Educational Measurement* 32(2):179–197, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1995.tb00462.x [VERIFIED]; Hanson & Brennan (1990), *JEM* 27(4):345–359, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1990.tb00753.x [VERIFIED]; Rudner (2001), *Practical Assessment, Research & Evaluation* 7(14), DOI 10.7275/an9m-2035, & Rudner (2005), *PARE* 10(13) [VERIFIED]; cross‑ref A2/A3. **Established.**

12. **SEM‑based confidence band around the cut; SED banding (Cascio et al.).** (a) Put an uncertainty zone around the cut: simple band = `cut ± z·SEM` (better: `± z·CSEM` at the cut). Selection **banding** treats scores within one *standard error of the difference* as indistinguishable. (b) `SEM = SD·√(1−r_xx)`; `SED = √2·SEM = √2·SD·√(1−r_xx)`; band width `= C·SD·√(1−r_xx)·√2` (C = z; 1.96→95%, often 1.00 to limit false positives). Fixed vs sliding bands; within‑band choices may use a secondary criterion (e.g., representation) — the personnel‑selection analog of a "flexible" gifted cutoff. (c) *Quantifies a defensible zone of scores statistically tied at the gifted cut, so a child one point below the line is not treated as truly different — a robustness/fairness device (cross‑ref A5/A6).* (d) A ±1 SED band is wide at the top tail (few items measure there); which child in the band is selected is a policy decision (guardrail). **Contested correction:** Dudek's derivation implies SED should use the standard error of *estimate*, not SEM, materially changing decisions. (e) Wide bands dilute the meaning of "top 1%"; banding reduces utility vs top‑down selection (Schmidt's critique). — Cascio, Outtz, Zedeck & Goldstein (1991), *Human Performance* 4(4):233–264, DOI 10.1207/s15327043hup0404_1 [VERIFIED]; Aguinis, Cortina & Goldberg (1998), *HP* 11(4):351–365, DOI 10.1207/s15327043hup1104_4 [VERIFIED]; "A Statistical Correction to 20 Years of Banding," *Int. J. Selection & Assessment* (2016), DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12016 [VERIFIED]. **Established; exact SED formula Contested.**

13. **Conjunctive (multiple‑hurdle) vs compensatory vs disjunctive cuts.** (a) When combining multiple measures for the gifted decision: **AND/conjunctive** (clear a cut on every measure), **OR/disjunctive** (clear any one), **MEAN/compensatory** (average vs a combined cut). The rule interacts strongly with the per‑test cuts and each measure's reliability. (b) Conjunctive selects ≈ the product of marginal pass‑rates (smallest, most homogeneous, most sensitive to per‑test error and multiplicative false‑negatives); disjunctive the largest; compensatory in between and it lets a high score offset a low one. (c) *Quantifies how the combination rule — not just each cut — sets the size, homogeneity, and error profile of the identified top‑1–2% pool.* (d) McBee/Lakin: OR yields the largest and most demographically diverse pool, but mainly because it identifies *more* students — the rule per se does not create diversity; only lowering the standard does (guardrail: do not conflate rule choice with equity). Conjunctive compounds measurement‑error false‑negatives at each hurdle. (e) Rule effects are entangled with cut heights and inter‑measure correlations; conjunctive is rarely preferred for equity. — McBee, Peters & Waterman (2014), *Gifted Child Quarterly* 58(1):69–89, DOI 10.1177/0016986213513794 [VERIFIED]; Lakin (2018), *GCQ* 62(2), DOI 10.1177/0016986217752099 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

14. **Outcome‑anchored cuts — ROC/Youden‑optimal & cost/utility (empirical alternatives to judgmental standard‑setting).** (a) Instead of a judgmental cut, choose the cut empirically against a defined outcome: **Youden‑optimal** `J = max(sensitivity + specificity − 1)`; **cost/utility‑optimal** minimizes expected misclassification cost `= c_FN·P(FN) + c_FP·P(FP)` given the base rate. (b) Youden cut = argmax over thresholds of (TPR − FPR); cost cut solves the likelihood‑ratio condition `f₁(x)/f₀(x) = (c_FP·π₀)/(c_FN·π₁)`. (c) *Quantifies the cut that best predicts a defined gifted‑program success outcome, converting standard‑setting into an optimization against consequences (cross‑ref A2 for ROC/Youden, A6 for utility/Taylor–Russell).* (d) At a ≈1% base rate the cost‑weighted optimum usually sits far from the Youden point; the FP/FN cost ratio is an explicit value choice — and **predictive validity of the cut ≠ program impact** (guardrail). (e) Needs a valid, unbiased outcome (criterion problem, cross‑ref A4); outcomes observed only on the selected create range restriction; optimizing to a historical outcome can bake in existing disparities. — cross‑ref A2/A6; Youden (1950), *Cancer* 3(1):32–35 (already resolved under A2; not re‑fetched this pass) [SECONDARY]. **Established as an *evaluation* tool; using it to *set* the gifted cut is Contested.**

15. **Norm‑ vs criterion‑referenced cut; local vs national norm; above‑level cut; the thin‑tail norm problem.** (a) A **norm‑referenced** cut is a percentile in a reference distribution (98th/99th percentile = top 2%/1%); a **criterion‑referenced** cut is a fixed performance standard from judgmental SS. **Local norms** (building/subgroup) vs **national norms** change *who* is identified (cross‑ref A5). An **above‑level cut** sets the gifted line on an above‑grade form to raise the ceiling. The **thin‑tail problem**: at the top 1–2%, national norm tables rest on very few standardization cases, percentile ranks jump in large steps, and CSEM is largest — so the "99th percentile" line is itself imprecise and possibly dated. (b) Percentile cut `x s.t. F(x)=0.98 or 0.99`; local‑norm cut recomputes `F` within the local/subgroup distribution; above‑level uses a higher‑grade form and its norms. (c) *Quantifies whether "top 1%" means relative to the nation, the district, or a subgroup — a decisive, value‑laden framing — and exposes how few data points actually define the extreme percentile.* (d) Local norms typically broaden representation across schools/subgroups (cross‑ref A5) but change the cut's meaning; above‑level testing spreads out the tail so the cut is measured with more information (cross‑ref A1). A subgroup gap at a national cut is a **diagnostic, not proof of bias** (guardrail); choosing national vs local is policy. (e) National tail norms are sparse/possibly stale; local norms are unstable in small schools; above‑level testing needs appropriate forms/norms and raises access‑equity questions (protect applicant rights). District thresholds/capacities must **not** be invented — treat as [UNVERIFIED] until the district supplies them. — Warne, R. T. (2012), *Roeper Review* 34(3):183–193, DOI 10.1080/02783193.2012.686425 [VERIFIED]; AERA/APA/NCME (2014), *Standards…*, ch. 5 (Norms; Cut Scores), official PDF [VERIFIED]; cross‑ref A1/A5. **Established (above‑level); thin‑tail norm imprecision under‑appreciated/Contested.**

16. **Validity of standard setting: Kane's procedural/internal/external framework; Hambleton & Pitoniak; 2014 Standards ch. 5.** (a) Frameworks for *defending* a cut. **Kane (1994)** separates the *descriptive* assumption (the cut corresponds to the intended standard) from the *policy* assumption (the standard is appropriate), and marshals three evidence types: **procedural** (reasonable, documented process; qualified, representative panel; adequate training/feedback), **internal** (replicability, intra‑/inter‑panelist consistency, SE of the cut, convergence across rounds), and **external** (agreement of the resulting classifications with independent criteria or other methods). **Hambleton & Pitoniak (2006)** consolidate evaluation criteria and add a cognitive/social model of panelist judgment. **2014 Standards ch. 5, Cluster 4 (Standards 5.21–5.23)** require documenting the rationale, method, procedures, panel qualifications, and providing the SE/consistency of the cut. (b) Evidence package, not a formula. (c) *Quantifies/organizes the EVIDENCE that a top‑1% cut is defensible — turning "why this line?" into auditable procedural, internal, and external claims.* (d) Minimum defensible bundle for the gifted cut: documented method + qualified panel + impact data + SE/consistency of the cut + an external agreement check + an explicit statement that the standard is a policy choice. (e) Kane's caution: the best one can show is that the standard is *reasonable*, never uniquely correct — procedural validity can be satisfied while the policy is still poor (predictive validity ≠ program impact, guardrail). — Kane, M. (1994), *Review of Educational Research* 64(3):425–461, DOI 10.3102/00346543064003425 [VERIFIED]; Hambleton & Pitoniak (2006), in Brennan (Ed.) *Educational Measurement (4th ed.)*, pp. 433–470, ACE/Praeger (ETS chapter record) [VERIFIED]; AERA/APA/NCME (2014), *Standards…*, ch. 5 [VERIFIED]; Cizek & Bunch (2007), *Standard Setting…*, Sage [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

### Wave 4 · A8 — Cross-domain metrics (ML / signal-detection / diagnostic / ranking) importable to gifted-ID evaluation (2026-07-22)

*Scope:* Catalogs machine-learning, information-retrieval, calibration, decision-analytic, signal-detection, and diagnostic-reclassification metrics — plus the sampling-bias warnings that invalidate all of them — and maps each to the class-imbalanced, top-1–2% ranking-plus-threshold structure of gifted identification and the "~2× better than CogAT" claim (cross-refs A1 IRT tail; A2 sens/spec/PPV/ROC-AUC/Youden/LR/kappa/base-rates/net-benefit-first-mention; A3 reliability/G-theory; A4 predictive/incremental validity + missing-outcome; A5 fairness/calibration-vs-balance impossibility; A6 simulation/DeLong/bootstrap-BCa/selection-utility).

Throughout, TP/FP/TN/FN are confusion-matrix counts, π = base rate (here ≈0.01–0.02), H = hit rate = sensitivity = TPR, F = false-alarm rate = 1−specificity = FPR, z(·) = Φ⁻¹ = inverse-normal (probit). "Not (visibly) used in gifted-ID" notes are reasoned inferences from the tail-decision literature, not a field audit [UNVERIFIED GT-usage].

**1. Precision, Recall, F1 and Fβ (the imbalanced-classification base).** (a) Precision = PPV; Recall = sensitivity; F1 = their harmonic mean; Fβ weights recall β× precision. (b) Precision = TP/(TP+FP); Recall = TP/(TP+FN); F1 = 2·P·R/(P+R) = 2TP/(2TP+FP+FN); Fβ = (1+β²)·P·R/(β²·P+R). (c) *Quantifies how "clean" a top-flagged pool is (precision) versus how many true gifted are captured (recall), and — via Fβ — lets the program encode whether missing a gifted child (raise β) or over-referring (lower β) is worse.* (d) Report at the operating point set by capacity, not at 0.5. [INFERENCE] At π=0.02, N=10,000 (200 gifted): a test at sensitivity 0.50 / specificity 0.98 yields TP=100, FP=196 → precision=100/296≈0.34; i.e., ~2 of 3 flagged kids are false positives despite "98% specificity." This is the base-rate reality (cross-ref A2 Meehl–Rosen) that F1/precision expose and accuracy hides. **2. Precision–Recall curve, Average Precision, and why PR-AUC > ROC-AUC at low base rate.** (a) The PR curve plots precision (y) vs recall (x) across all thresholds; its summary is Average Precision (AP), the area. (b) AP = Σₙ (Rₙ − Rₙ₋₁)·Pₙ (precision averaged at each recall step). Random-classifier baselines: **PR-AUC = π**, but **ROC-AUC = 0.5 regardless of π**. (c) *Quantifies early-retrieval / top-of-list performance — exactly the tail where gifted selection happens — and, unlike ROC, actually moves when the gifted:non-gifted ratio is 1:50–1:100.* (d) Prefer PR-AUC/AP when π is tiny; a classifier can post ROC-AUC≈0.90 yet AP≈0.30 at π=0.02. Saito & Rehmsmeier show only the PRC responds to class ratio, so ROC "looks optimistic" under imbalance. Compare a new test's AP to CogAT's AP on the same cohort. (e) PR curves are non-monotone and their linear interpolation is invalid (use AP, not trapezoids); AP still depends on π so cross-cohort comparisons need equal π. — Saito & Rehmsmeier (2015), *PLoS ONE* 10(3):e0118432, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0118432 [VERIFIED]; PR↔ROC dominance: Davis & Goadrich (2006), *ICML*, doi:10.1145/1143844.1143874 [UNVERIFIED this pass]. Not visibly used in gifted-ID [UNVERIFIED GT-usage]; importing it reframes "2× better" as AP-at-tail rather than global AUC. **Established.**

**3. Balanced accuracy and G-mean.** (a) Both average the two class-wise accuracies so the majority class can't dominate. (b) Balanced accuracy = (sensitivity + specificity)/2 = (1 + Youden's J)/2; G-mean = √(sensitivity · specificity). (c) *Quantifies whether a test is good at BOTH finding gifted kids and correctly passing over non-gifted kids, giving the small gifted class equal weight to the huge non-gifted class.* (d) G-mean is the stricter twin: if either sensitivity or specificity → 0, G-mean → 0 (arithmetic balanced accuracy stays ~0.5). Brodersen et al. give balanced accuracy a Bayesian posterior → proper CIs (dovetails with A6 bootstrap/BCa). (e) Both collapse the operating point to two numbers and still hide precision at low π (a 0.90/0.90 test still has ~15% precision at π=0.02); use with #2/#5. — Balanced accuracy: Brodersen, Ong, Stephan & Buhmann (2010), *ICPR* 2010:3121–3124, doi:10.1109/ICPR.2010.764 [VERIFIED]; G-mean: Kubat & Matwin (1997), *Proc. 14th ICML*:179–186 [VERIFIED, no DOI]. **Established.**

**4. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and MCC vs Cohen's κ vs F1.** (a) MCC is the Pearson (φ) correlation between binary predictions and truth, using all four confusion cells. (b) MCC = (TP·TN − FP·FN) / √[(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)] ∈ [−1,+1] (+1 perfect, 0 chance, −1 inverse). Cohen's κ = (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e) (chance-corrected agreement; cross-ref A2). (c) *Quantifies overall separation quality in one imbalance-robust number that only goes high when the test is right on BOTH gifted and non-gifted proportionally to their sizes.* (d) Chicco & Jurman: a high MCC requires good TP, TN, FP, FN jointly; it is invariant to class swapping, whereas F1 changes if you relabel which class is "positive" and inflates on imbalance; κ can diverge from MCC under prevalence/bias asymmetry. Prefer MCC as the headline scalar for a CogAT-vs-new-test confusion comparison. (e) Undefined (0/0) when any margin is 0; a single scalar can't localize failures (pair with reliability diagram #8 and DCA #10). — Chicco & Jurman (2020), *BMC Genomics* 21(1):6, doi:10.1186/s12864-019-6413-7 [VERIFIED]; origin Matthews (1975), *Biochim. Biophys. Acta* 405(2):442–451, doi:10.1016/0005-2795(75)90109-9 [VERIFIED]. Not visibly used in gifted-ID [UNVERIFIED GT-usage]. **Established.**

**5. Top-k ranking metrics — precision@k, recall@k, R-precision — the natural "select the top X%" family.** (a) Rank all applicants by score, take the top k; measure hits in that cutoff. (b) precision@k = (relevant in top k)/k; recall@k = (relevant in top k)/(total relevant); **R-precision = precision@R where R = total # gifted = also equals recall@R** (the PR break-even point). (c) *Quantifies exactly the operational decision "we can seat k children in the gifted program": how many of the chosen k are truly gifted, and how many gifted we captured — the fixed-capacity, tail-ranking view.* (d) "Identify the top 1–2%" is intrinsically precision@k (k = seats/capacity), NOT a fixed cut-score, because (i) it matches the real budget constraint, (ii) it is invariant to any monotone rescaling of scores (unlike a raw threshold), and (iii) it concentrates evaluation on the tail rather than the whole ROC. What it buys over a fixed cut: capacity-honesty and threshold-free comparison of CogAT vs the new test at the same k. If [UNVERIFIED GT-fact] k equals the true count of gifted, R-precision is the single symmetric tail score. (e) Needs a defensible k and (for recall@k) a known denominator of "true gifted," which verification bias corrupts (#14); ignores ordering within the top-k (see #6). — Manning, Raghavan & Schütze (2008), *Introduction to Information Retrieval*, Cambridge Univ. Press, ch. 8 [VERIFIED via official Stanford IR text]; TREC common evaluation measures, trec.nist.gov [VERIFIED]. Not visibly used in gifted-ID [UNVERIFIED GT-usage]; high value-add for a capacity-bounded program. **Established.**

**6. Average Precision / MAP and NDCG (graded / ordered ranking quality).** (a) AP/MAP summarize binary-relevance ranking; NDCG rewards putting the *most* relevant items highest under graded relevance. (b) AP = (1/#relevant)·Σ_k P@k·rel(k); MAP = mean AP over "queries" (e.g., schools/cohorts). DCG@k = Σ_{i=1}^{k} (2^{relᵢ}−1)/log₂(i+1); **NDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k** ∈ [0,1]. (c) *Quantifies whether the strongest candidates are ranked at the very top (NDCG), and — averaged across many schools/cohorts as "queries" (MAP) — whether the test ranks well repeatably.* (d) Use NDCG if "giftedness" is graded (e.g., levels of talent / depth of acceleration need) rather than binary, so mis-ordering a profoundly-gifted child costs more than mis-ordering a marginal one. Use MAP to aggregate across sites for a stable single number with good discrimination/stability. (e) NDCG needs a defensible graded label and a chosen gain/discount; both AP and NDCG assume trustworthy labels (#14). — Järvelin & Kekäläinen (2002), *ACM TOIS* 20(4):422–446, doi:10.1145/582415.582418 [VERIFIED]; AP/MAP per Manning et al. (2008) [VERIFIED]. Not visibly used in gifted-ID [UNVERIFIED GT-usage]. **Established.**

**7. Proper probabilistic scoring: Brier score + Murphy decomposition, and the logarithmic score.** (a) Strictly proper scores reward honest probability forecasts; Brier is the mean-squared, log-score the logarithmic penalty. (b) Brier BS = (1/N)Σ(pᵢ−oᵢ)², oᵢ∈{0,1}. **Murphy partition: BS = Reliability − Resolution + Uncertainty**, with Reliability=(1/N)Σ_k n_k(p̄_k−ō_k)² (↓ good), Resolution=(1/N)Σ_k n_k(ō_k−ō)² (↑ good), Uncertainty=ō(1−ō) (irreducible). Log score = −(1/N)Σ[oᵢ ln pᵢ + (1−oᵢ)ln(1−pᵢ)]. (c) *Quantifies the accuracy of a test's probability-of-gifted output and cleanly separates two virtues: is the probability trustworthy (reliability) and does it spread kids apart (resolution) beyond the base rate (uncertainty).* (d) Report Brier + its three Murphy components for CogAT-derived and new-test probabilities on the same cohort; resolution is the "value-added" term. Log score is harsher (→∞ for a confident wrong call), useful when overconfident tail claims are dangerous. Both are strictly proper (Gneiting & Raftery), so they can't be gamed by hedging — unlike NRI (#12). (e) Brier's magnitude is dominated by Uncertainty at low π, so compare via the Brier **skill** score BSS = 1 − BS/BS_ref (see #16), not raw BS. — Brier (1950), *Monthly Weather Review* 78(1):1–3, doi:10.1175/1520-0493(1950)078<0001:VOFEIT>2.0.CO;2 [VERIFIED]; Murphy (1973), *J. Appl. Meteorology* 12(4):595–600, doi:10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012<0595:ANVPOT>2.0.CO;2 [VERIFIED]; propriety: Gneiting & Raftery (2007), *JASA* 102(477):359–378, doi:10.1198/016214506000001437 [VERIFIED]; log score origin Good (1952) [UNVERIFIED this pass]. **Established.**

**8. Calibration assessment: reliability diagram, ECE/MCE, calibration slope & intercept (Cox), Spiegelhalter's z, Hosmer–Lemeshow.** (a) A family checking whether predicted probability-of-gifted matches observed frequency (cross-ref A5 for the calibration-vs-balance impossibility — do not re-derive). (b) Reliability diagram: bin predictions, plot mean predicted (x) vs observed rate (y); diagonal = calibrated. **ECE = Σ_m (n_m/N)|acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|; MCE = max_m |acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|.** Cox calibration: fit logit P(Y=1) = a + b·LP (LP = model log-odds); perfect ⇒ a=0, b=1 (b<1 ⇒ overfit/too-extreme; "calibration-in-the-large" = intercept a₀ with slope fixed at 1). Spiegelhalter z = Σ(oᵢ−pᵢ)(1−2pᵢ) / √[Σ(1−2pᵢ)² pᵢ(1−pᵢ)], |z|>1.96 ⇒ miscalibrated. Hosmer–Lemeshow: group into g (≈10) risk deciles, Ĉ = Σ_g (O_g−E_g)²/[E_g(1−E_g/n_g)] ~ χ²(g−2). (c) *Quantifies whether "this child has an 85% chance of being gifted" is literally true 85% of the time — essential if scores drive high-stakes placement, and separable from ranking ability.* (d) Report slope+intercept as the two-number external-validation summary (Steyerberg et al. 2010 popularized this), plus a reliability diagram and one GOF test. Calibration in the top bins is what matters for a 1–2% cut. (e) ECE is binning-dependent, not proper, and the top bins are sparse at low π; Hosmer–Lemeshow power is arbitrary in g. Calibration is necessary but NOT sufficient (a base-rate-only predictor is perfectly calibrated yet useless — see resolution, #7). — Cox (1958), *Biometrika* 45(3-4):562–565, doi:10.1093/biomet/45.3-4.562 [VERIFIED]; Spiegelhalter (1986), *Statistics in Medicine* 5(5):421–433, doi:10.1002/sim.4780050506 [VERIFIED]; Hosmer & Lemeshow (1980), *Commun. Stat. Theory Methods* 9(10):1043–1069, doi:10.1080/03610928008827941 [VERIFIED]; ECE/MCE origin Naeini, Cooper & Hauskrecht (2015), *AAAI* 29(1), doi:10.1609/aaai.v29i1.9602 [VERIFIED]; ECE formula/reliability diagrams Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger (2017), *ICML/PMLR* 70:1321–1330 (arXiv:1706.04599) [VERIFIED]. **Established** (each test); **Contested** only re: which single calibration summary is best.

**9. Recalibration: Platt scaling, isotonic regression (and temperature scaling).** (a) Post-hoc maps raw scores → honest probabilities without retraining. (b) Platt: P = 1/(1+exp(A·s + B)), a 2-parameter sigmoid fit on held-out data. Isotonic: fit any non-decreasing step function via Pool-Adjacent-Violators (PAV). Temperature scaling: single-parameter Platt variant on logits (leaves ranking/AUC unchanged). (c) *Quantifies-then-fixes miscalibration so a new adaptive test's probabilities are directly comparable to CogAT's and usable in decision rules (#10) — without changing who ranks above whom.* (d) Use Platt when distortion is sigmoidal and data are scarce; isotonic when you have enough held-out cases (it corrects any monotone distortion but overfits on small samples). Always fit on a separate split (ties to A6 cross-validation/optimism). (e) Recalibration cannot add discrimination/resolution — it only repairs probability labels; isotonic can overfit the tail where gifted cases are few. — Platt (1999), *Advances in Large Margin Classifiers*, MIT Press:61–74 [SECONDARY, confirmed via citing works this pass]; Zadrozny & Elkan (2002), *KDD* 2002:694–699, doi:10.1145/775047.775151 [VERIFIED]; comparison Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana (2005), *ICML* 2005:625–632, doi:10.1145/1102351.1102430 [VERIFIED]; temperature scaling Guo et al. (2017) [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**10. Decision curve analysis / net benefit, relative utility, and cost curves (extends A2's net-benefit first mention — do not repeat).** (a) Decision-analytic tools that score a test by consequences at the threshold the program actually uses, not by abstract accuracy. (b) **Net benefit NB(p_t) = TP/N − (FP/N)·[p_t/(1−p_t)] = sens·π − (1−spec)(1−π)·[p_t/(1−p_t)]**, where p_t = threshold probability (the risk at which you'd act). Plot NB vs p_t against "treat-all" and "treat-none" lines. Relative utility (Baker et al.) = NB scaled to perfect prediction (fraction of achievable utility; needs no absolute utility, supports sensitivity analysis, and defines a "test threshold" for two-stage screens). Cost curves (Drummond & Holte): plot normalized expected cost vs the probability-cost function PCF = πC(−|+)/[πC(−|+)+(1−π)C(+|−)]; each classifier is a line, the lower envelope is optimal, and CIs/significance are read off visually. (c) *Quantifies whether the new test yields more net correctly-placed gifted children per tolerated false placement, at the program's own tolerance p_t — the single most decision-relevant way to ask "is it worth switching from CogAT?"* (d) p_t encodes the exchange rate "how many extra referrals per additional gifted child found." Choose a clinically/policy-defensible p_t range [UNVERIFIED GT-fact] and show the new test's NB exceeds CogAT's and both defaults there. Recommended by TRIPOD (#15). (e) NB requires calibrated probabilities (#8–#9) and honest labels (#14); p_t is a value judgment, not a statistic — surface it explicitly, and remember NB ≠ program impact (guardrail). — Vickers & Elkin (2006), *Medical Decision Making* 26(6):565–574, doi:10.1177/0272989X06295361 [VERIFIED]; relative utility Baker, Cook, Vickers & Kramer (2009), *J. R. Stat. Soc. A* 172(4):729–748, doi:10.1111/j.1467-985X.2009.00592.x [VERIFIED]; cost curves Drummond & Holte (2006), *Machine Learning* 65(1):95–130, doi:10.1007/s10994-006-8199-5 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**11. Signal detection theory: d′, criterion c, β, nonparametric A′/B″, and AUC = P(correct in 2AFC).** (a) Separates *discrimination* (how far apart gifted vs non-gifted score distributions are) from *decision criterion* (where the cut is set) — a distinction gifted cut-scores usually conflate. (b) d′ = z(H) − z(F) (separation in SD units, equal-variance-Gaussian assumption; cross-ref A1 tail measurement). Criterion c = −½[z(H) + z(F)] (c>0 conservative, c<0 liberal). Likelihood-ratio bias β = exp{½[z(F)² − z(H)²]}. Distribution-free: **A′ = 0.5 + [(H−F)(1+H−F)]/[4H(1−F)]** (for H≥F; symmetric form 0.5 − [(F−H)(1+F−H)]/[4F(1−H)] for H<F), and **B″ = sign(H−F)·[H(1−H) − F(1−F)]/[H(1−H) + F(1−F)]** ∈ [−1,1] (0 = unbiased). AUC = P(random gifted scores above random non-gifted) + ½P(tie) = **proportion correct in a 2-alternative forced choice** = Mann–Whitney U/(n₊n₋) (cross-ref A2/A6 for AUC & DeLong). (c) *d′ gives one interpretable "how separable are gifted from non-gifted" number; c/β expose whether a district's cut is lenient or strict independently of test quality; A′/B″ do this without Gaussian assumptions.* (d) Use d′ to compare intrinsic separation of CogAT vs new test, then set c to hit the capacity/1–2% target; a "2× better" claim on separation is naturally a d′ (or A′) comparison. AUC's 2AFC reading is the intuition-friendly way to state discrimination to non-technical stakeholders. (e) d′/c/β assume equal-variance normal decision variables (often false in tails → prefer A′/B″ or full ROC); rates of 0/1 need log-linear or ½-count correction. — Stanislaw & Todorov (1999), *Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers* 31(1):137–149, doi:10.3758/BF03207704 [VERIFIED]; A′/B″: Grier (1971), *Psychological Bulletin* 75(6):424–429, doi:10.1037/h0031246 [VERIFIED]; AUC=2AFC/U-statistic: Bamber (1975), *J. Math. Psychology* 12(4):387–415, doi:10.1016/0022-2496(75)90001-2 [VERIFIED]; 2AFC interpretation Green & Swets (1966) [SECONDARY]. Rare in gifted-ID [UNVERIFIED GT-usage]; strong value-add for tail-separation framing. **Established.**

**12. Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) & Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) — and their serious critiques.** (a) Metrics for the incremental value of adding/switching a marker (cross-ref A4 incremental validity) — the tempting way to ask "does the new screen reclassify kids better than CogAT?" (b) Category NRI = [P(up|gifted) − P(down|gifted)] + [P(down|non-gifted) − P(up|non-gifted)]; category-free NRI (cfNRI) counts any correct-direction risk movement. **IDI = (IS_new − IS_old) − (IP_new − IP_old)** = gain in mean predicted probability among events minus the gain among non-events = difference in discrimination slopes. (c) *Purports to quantify how many children the new test moves into the correct gifted/non-gifted category relative to CogAT.* (d) If used at all, report event and non-event components **separately** (they are just changes in TPR and FPR), get CIs by **bootstrap** (A6), never use published variance formulas, and never use NRI with ≥3 categories. (e) **CONTESTED — report the critique.** Kerr et al.: cfNRI can mislead and *overstate* incremental value even in independent validation, and its usual variance formulas are invalid; the preferred single-number summary is improvement in **net benefit** (#10). Pepe et al.: the NRI statistic is *likely positive even when the new marker carries no predictive information*, given only a poorly-fitting risk model — so a positive NRI is **not** evidence the new test reclassifies kids better; ΔAUC (DeLong, A6), Δnet-benefit, and ΔBrier cannot be inflated this way. — Pencina, D'Agostino, D'Agostino & Vasan (2008), *Statistics in Medicine* 27(2):157–172, doi:10.1002/sim.2929 [VERIFIED]; critiques: Kerr, Wang, Janes, McClelland, Psaty & Pepe (2014), *Epidemiology* 25(1):114–121, doi:10.1097/EDE.0000000000000018 [VERIFIED]; Pepe, Fan, Feng, Gerds & Hilden (2015), *Statistics in Biosciences* 7(2):282–295, doi:10.1007/s12561-014-9118-0 [VERIFIED]; see also Vickers & Pepe (2014), *Ann. Intern. Med.* 160(2):136–137, doi:10.7326/M13-2841 [SECONDARY], Hilden & Gerds (2014), *Stat. Med.*, doi:10.1002/sim.5804 [UNVERIFIED this pass]. **Contested.**

**13. Number needed to diagnose / screen, and the diagnostic odds ratio.** (a) Translate accuracy into intuitive "effort per correct identification" units. (b) **NND = 1/(sensitivity − (1−specificity)) = 1/Youden's J** (cross-ref A2). **NNS = NNT / prevalence** (of the so-far-unidentified condition). DOR = (TP·TN)/(FP·FN) = LR+/LR− (cross-ref A2; do not re-derive). (c) *NND says how many children must be tested to correctly identify one additional gifted child beyond chance; NNS scales this to a whole-program screen — a plain-language cost of tail accuracy.* (d) Contrast CogAT's NND with the new test's NND at matched operating points: halving NND is a concrete, defensible form of "2× better" for the identification step. (e) NND collapses to a single point on the ROC and inherits base-rate sensitivity via NNS; all are corrupted by verification bias (#14); accuracy ≠ program impact (guardrail). — NNS: Rembold (1998), *BMJ* 317(7154):307–312, doi:10.1136/bmj.317.7154.307 [VERIFIED]; NND per Youden's J (A2); DOR: Glas et al. **14. Sampling-bias warnings that break EVERY metric above: verification/work-up bias, partial verification, and spectrum bias.** (a) Structural label problems that bias sensitivity, specificity, and therefore every downstream metric (#1–#13, #16). (b) Verification / work-up / partial-verification bias (Begg & Greenes): when only a selected subset receives the reference-standard "gifted" label — and selection depends on the screening result — sens/spec estimates are biased; Bayes-rule corrections require an explicit, testable model of the selection mechanism. Spectrum bias (Ransohoff & Feinstein): sens/spec are **not fixed test properties** — they shift with the case-mix/severity spectrum of the sample. (c) *Quantifies why "the test is 95% accurate" is meaningless without knowing WHO got labeled and from WHAT pool: in gifted-ID only kids who screened high or were referred are fully evaluated, so the gifted label is observed mainly for the already-selected — labels are Missing-Not-At-Random (connect to A4 missing-outcome).* (d) Mitigate with representative/consecutive sampling (STARD, #15), Begg–Greenes-style correction under an explicit selection model, and sensitivity analysis over the unverified. Crucially, internal cross-validation/optimism correction (A6) does **not** fix label-selection bias. Spectrum bias means a test validated on clearly-gifted-vs-average children will look far better than on the borderline pool where the 1–2% cut actually operates. (e) These caveats bound causal claims (guardrail: descriptive accuracy ≠ impact; protect applicant rights by not withholding evaluation to "clean" a study). — Begg & Greenes (1983), *Biometrics* 39(1):207–215, doi:10.2307/2530820 [VERIFIED]; Ransohoff & Feinstein (1978), *N. Engl. J. Med.* 299(17):926–930, doi:10.1056/NEJM197810262991705 [VERIFIED]. **Established** (and the single most important cross-domain warning for this project).

**16. Forecasting skill scores: Peirce skill score = Youden's J, Gandin–Murphy equitability, and the skill-score framing of "2×."** (a) Skill scores express performance as improvement over a reference forecast — the honest structure for a comparative "2× better" claim. (b) General skill score: **SS = (Score − Score_ref)/(Score_perfect − Score_ref)** (e.g., Brier skill score BSS = 1 − BS/BS_ref). Peirce skill score PSS = (TP·TN − FP·FN)/[(TP+FN)(FP+TN)] = TPR − FPR = **sensitivity + specificity − 1 = Youden's J** (cross-ref A2). Gandin–Murphy define "equitability" (random and constant forecasts score 0) and prove the Peirce score is essentially the *unique* equitable 2-category score. (c) *Quantifies how much better a test is than a stated baseline (chance, base-rate guessing, or CogAT itself), which is exactly what "~2× better at the top 1–2%" should mean.* (d) Frame the headline claim as a skill score with **CogAT as the reference** (e.g., BSS or AP-skill vs CogAT, or a ratio of Peirce/J), not as a raw metric ratio; this bakes in the baseline and the base rate and is far more defensible/auditable. Equitability is a scoring-fairness property worth stating alongside A5 fairness. (e) Skill scores inherit their base metric's assumptions and the label-bias caveats (#14); "skill over CogAT" is predictive skill, **not** program impact (guardrail). — Gandin & Murphy (1992), *Monthly Weather Review* 120(2):361–370, doi:10.1175/1520-0493(1992)120<0361:ESSFCF>2.0.CO;2 [VERIFIED]; Peirce (1884), *Science* 4(93):453–454 [SECONDARY, confirmed via citing works]; Youden (1950) (cross-ref A2); propriety context Gneiting & Raftery (2007) [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

### Wave 5 · A9 — Unbiased accuracy/validity under selection (verification bias / MNAR) & near-cut quasi-experimental designs (2026-07-22)

*Scope: methods that (i) CORRECT sensitivity/specificity/PPV and predictive validity when the "truly gifted" label and program outcomes are observed only for selected/screened-in students (verification bias, Missing-Not-At-Random), and (ii) recover a local, uncontaminated counterfactual near the admission cut (RD, local randomization, marginal-lottery/waitlist) — i.e., the correction methods and near-cut designs that A8 §14 flagged as metric-breakers but deferred to here. Keeps separate the three questions this project must not conflate: selection-into-being-labeled/treated (THIS pass), measurement bias (A5), and base rates (A2), and ties to the charter's counterfactual theme (program effect ≠ selection effect; before/after growth ≠ counterfactual; predictive validity ≠ program impact). Labels: [VERIFIED] = DOI/official page resolved this pass; worked numbers [INFERENCE]; "established" vs "contested" flagged. Applicant-rights constraint applied per method: you cannot withhold entitled services to manufacture clean data, so designs that exploit an existing cut/lottery/waitlist are favored over deliberate denial.*

**1. Begg–Greenes partial-/work-up-verification-bias correction (Bayes' rule under MAR verification).** (a) The foundational correction for the exact A8 §14 problem: sens/spec computed only on kids who got the reference "gifted" label are biased when who-gets-verified depends on the screen result. (b) With test level `T`, gifted status `D`, verification `V`: estimate `θ̂_t = P̂(D=1 | T=t, V=1)` from verified subjects at each level, and the marginal `P̂(T=t)=n_t/n` from ALL screened (verified + unverified); then corrected `prevalence = Σ_t θ̂_t P̂(T=t)`, **corrected sensitivity `= θ̂_+ P̂(T=+) / Σ_t θ̂_t P̂(T=t)`**, corrected specificity `= (1−θ̂_−)P̂(T=−) / Σ_t (1−θ̂_t)P̂(T=t)`. (c) *Quantifies the true tail sensitivity/specificity/prevalence once you account for the fact that the gifted label exists mostly for screened-high/referred children.* (d) **Non-obvious, high-value corollary:** under this MAR mechanism (V ⟂ D given T and observed covariates), **PPV `= θ̂_+` and NPV are already unbiased in the verified-only sample — only sensitivity, specificity, and prevalence are biased and need correcting** (so a district can honestly report PPV even from selective data, but *cannot* trust naïve sensitivity). Requires knowing/estimating the verification mechanism and that it is MAR. (e) Fails under MNAR (§3); low cell counts in the deep tail inflate variance; applicant-rights-safe because it re-uses existing selective verification rather than requiring new denials. — Begg, C. B., & Greenes, R. A. (1983), *Biometrics* 39(1):207–215, DOI 10.2307/2530820 [VERIFIED]. **Established** (extends A8 §14 from warning to correction).

**2. Inverse-probability-of-verification weighting (IPW) for verification bias.** (a) Reweight each verified case by the inverse probability it was verified, reconstructing the full screened cohort (survey-sampling logic). (b) Horvitz–Thompson estimator generalized to two-phase diagnostic sampling; Alonzo–Pepe give the continuous-test form: **`TPR_IPW(c) = [Σ_i 1(T_i≥c) V_i D_i / π_i] / [Σ_i V_i D_i / π_i]`**, `FPR_IPW(c)` identical with `(1−D_i)`, where `π_i = P(V_i=1 | T_i, X_i)`; plot corrected TPR vs FPR for a bias-corrected ROC/AUC. Full-imputation (FI), mean-score-imputation (MSI), and **doubly-robust (DR)** variants also given. (c) *Quantifies unbiased tail sensitivity/specificity/ROC by up-weighting the under-verified borderline pool where the 1–2% cut actually operates.* (d) Valid under MAR with a correctly specified verification model; the **DR estimator is consistent if either the verification model or the disease model is correct** (Alonzo–Pepe recommend it); `π_i` known by design if verification is protocolized, else estimated. (e) IPW is fragile to verification-model misspecification and unstable when some `π_i` are tiny (deep tail); cannot rescue MNAR. — Alonzo, T. A., & Pepe, M. S. (2005), *J. R. Stat. Soc. C (Applied Statistics)* 54(1):173–190, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9876.2005.00477.x [VERIFIED]; origin Horvitz, D. G., & Thompson, D. J. (1952), *JASA* 47(260):663–685, DOI 10.1080/01621459.1952.10483446 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**3. Maximum-likelihood / EM correction under an explicit verification model — including nonignorable (MNAR) verification.** (a) Treat the missing gifted label as a missing-covariate problem and jointly model disease, test, and the *selection-into-verification* mechanism. (b) Kosinski–Barnhart factorization **`P(V,T,D | X) = P(D|X)·P(T|D,X)·P(V|D,T,X)`**, each a logistic regression, fit by EM with an explicit observed-information matrix for SEs; **verification is MAR iff the coefficient on `D` in `P(V|D,T,X)` is zero — testable by a likelihood-ratio/Wald test**; if nonzero, the mechanism is nonignorable (MNAR). Baker (1995) and Zhou (1998) give related selection-model formulations. (c) *Quantifies sens/spec/ROC when the decision to fully evaluate a child depends on the very giftedness that is missing (e.g., borderline kids referred only if a teacher already suspects giftedness).* (d) Needs covariates and/or multiple screening measures to identify the extra MNAR parameter; report a **sensitivity analysis / "test-ignorance region"** over the unverifiable dependence of `V` on `D`. (e) **MNAR parameters are not point-identified from the observed data alone** — the model borrows strength from parametric/covariate assumptions, so results must be presented as a sensitivity band, not a single number; applicant-rights-safe (models existing selective work-up). — Kosinski, A. S., & Barnhart, H. X. (2003), *Biometrics* 59(1):163–171, DOI 10.1111/1541-0420.00019 [VERIFIED]. **Established method; MNAR point-identification Contested (assumption-dependent).**

**4. Rubin's MCAR / MAR / MNAR taxonomy and ignorability — the identification boundary governing this whole pass.** (a) The formal theory that decides *whether any of §1–§3 or §6–§9 can be unbiased at all.* (b) With missingness indicator `R`: **MCAR** `P(R|Y_obs,Y_mis)=P(R)`; **MAR** `P(R|Y_obs,Y_mis)=P(R|Y_obs)`; **MNAR** depends on `Y_mis`. Ignoring the missingness mechanism is valid ("ignorable") iff **MAR + distinctness of the missingness and data parameters**. (c) *Quantifies the honesty ceiling: the gifted label/outcome in real ID is MNAR by construction — kids are labeled/served because they scored high — so point-identified corrected accuracy is impossible without an untestable assumption or an external anchor.* (d) Under MAR, likelihood/Bayesian/MI/FIML corrections (§6) are principled; under MNAR you need a modeled mechanism (§3), a design (§11–§15), or bounds (§7). (e) **MAR vs MNAR is not testable from the observed data** — this is the crux caveat behind every "corrected" tail statistic; must be argued from how verification/admission actually happened, not assumed. — Rubin, D. B. (1976), *Biometrika* 63(3):581–592, DOI 10.1093/biomet/63.3.581 [VERIFIED]. **Established (foundational).**

**5. Selection models vs pattern-mixture models (two honest factorizations of MNAR).** (a) Two ways to write the joint distribution of the outcome and its missingness, making the untestable MNAR assumption explicit in different places. (b) **Selection model:** `f(Y,R) = f(Y)·f(R|Y)` (Heckman §8 is the parametric case — model how the outcome drives being observed). **Pattern-mixture model:** `f(Y,R) = f(Y|R)·f(R)` (stratify by observed/missing pattern and model each). (c) *Quantifies the gifted-outcome distribution for the unobserved (rejected/unverified) via a stated identifying restriction, rather than hiding the assumption inside a "corrected" scalar.* (d) Pattern-mixture models are **chronically under-identified** (no data on the missing stratum), so they require identifying restrictions (complete-case restriction, "Δ-adjustment" shifting the missing mean by a sensitivity parameter) or priors; run **both** selection and pattern-mixture forms as a sensitivity range. (e) Neither is testable against the other from observed data; the value is transparency, not resolution. — Little, R. J. A. (1993), *JASA* 88(421):125–134, DOI 10.1080/01621459.1993.10594302 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**6. Multiple imputation (Rubin's rules) and full-information maximum likelihood (FIML).** (a) The two workhorses for principled estimation under MAR — impute-and-combine, or maximize the observed-data likelihood directly. (b) **MI:** create `m` completed datasets, analyze each, combine: point estimate `Q̄ = (1/m)Σ Q̂_ℓ`; **total variance `T = W̄ + (1 + 1/m)·B`**, `W̄` = mean within-imputation variance, `B = (1/(m−1))Σ(Q̂_ℓ−Q̄)²` between-imputation variance (the `(1+1/m)B` term is the price of missingness). **FIML:** maximize `Σ_i log f(Y_obs,i; θ)` case-wise over observed variables — no imputation. (c) *Quantifies tail metrics/outcomes with uncertainty honestly inflated for the missing gifted labels, instead of a false-precision complete-case number.* (d) Both unbiased and efficient **under MAR**; MI extends to MNAR via pattern-mixture "Δ" draws (§5); FIML found superior to listwise/pairwise deletion across all simulated conditions (Enders–Bandalos). (e) **"Garbage in" under MNAR** — MI/FIML do not detect or fix nonignorable selection; they are complements to, not substitutes for, a design (§11–§15). — Rubin, D. B. (1987), *Multiple Imputation for Nonresponse in Surveys*, Wiley, DOI 10.1002/9780470316696 [VERIFIED]; Enders, C. K., & Bandalos, D. L. (2001), *Structural Equation Modeling* 8(3):430–457, DOI 10.1207/S15328007SEM0803_5 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**7. Manski partial-identification / worst-case bounds — what can be said with NO assumptions.** (a) The assumption-free floor and ceiling: instead of point-identifying under an untestable MAR/MNAR model, report the *range* of values consistent with the data alone. (b) For a bounded outcome `Y∈[y_L,y_U]` observed only when selected (`z=1`): **`E[Y] ∈ [ E[Y|z=1]P(z=1) + y_L·P(z=0), E[Y|z=1]P(z=1) + y_U·P(z=0) ]`, width `= (y_U−y_L)·P(z=0)`** — the bound width is exactly the missing fraction. For a treatment effect, data alone shrink the a-priori width-2 interval `[−1,1]` to width 1. Tighten with **monotone-treatment-response / monotone-treatment-selection** (Manski & Pepper 2000), an instrument, or **Lee (2009) trimming bounds** under monotone attrition. (c) *Quantifies the honest interval for tail PPV / "benefit rate among the marginal" using only observed data — the number you can defend when the MNAR mechanism is genuinely unknown.* (d) Worked [INFERENCE]: if only 30% are admitted/observed (`P(z=1)=0.3`) and `E[Y|z=1]=0.5` for a binary "benefits" outcome, then `E[Y] ∈ [0.15, 0.85]` — a wide but *assumption-free* statement. (e) Bounds are often "too wide to be interesting" (the standard critique) — but width is itself information (it says how much the selection problem limits you); **applicant-rights advantage: bounds let you avoid withholding services just to point-identify.** — Manski, C. F. (1990), *American Economic Review (P&P)* 80(2):319–323 [VERIFIED]; foundations Manski, C. F. (1989), *J. Human Resources* 24(3):343–360, DOI 10.2307/145818 [VERIFIED]; trimming bounds Lee, D. S. (2009), *Review of Economic Studies* 76(3):1071–1102, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-937X.2009.00536.x [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**8. Heckman two-step sample-selection model + the inverse Mills ratio.** (a) The econometric selection model: a probit for *who is observed* plus an outcome equation, correcting the outcome for nonrandom selection. (b) Selection `z* = Wγ + u`, observe `z=1` if `z*>0`; outcome `Y = Xβ + ε` seen only when `z=1`; with bivariate-normal `(u,ε)`, **`E[Y | X, z=1] = Xβ + (ρσ_ε)·λ(Wγ)`, where `λ(a)=φ(a)/Φ(a)` is the inverse Mills ratio.** Two-step: (1) probit → `γ̂` → `λ̂`; (2) OLS of `Y` on `[X, λ̂]`; the **coefficient on `λ̂` estimates `ρσ_ε`, and its t-test is a direct test for selection bias.** (c) *Quantifies unbiased predictive validity / expected outcome for the whole applicant pool even though outcomes are seen only for admitted kids — the correlational analog of the missing-outcome problem A4 flagged.* (d) Report the selection-coefficient test; requires a credible **exclusion restriction** (≥1 variable in `W` predicting selection but not the outcome). (e) **Known-fragile:** without a valid exclusion restriction it is identified only off the nonlinearity of `λ`, making `λ̂` near-collinear with `X` and estimates unstable; strongly bivariate-normality-dependent (MNAR-model-dependent) — treat as a sensitivity/comparator, not a stand-alone evidence generator. — Heckman, J. J. (1979), *Econometrica* 47(1):153–161, DOI 10.2307/1912352 [VERIFIED]. **Established method; application Contested (fragile without exclusion restriction).**

**9. Tobit / censored-regression model (the ceiling-effect special case).** (a) A limited-dependent-variable model for an outcome censored (piled up) at a floor/ceiling, distinct from truncation and from Heckman selection. (b) Latent `y_i* = x_iβ + ε_i`, `ε~N(0,σ²)`; **observe `y_i = y_i*` if `y_i*>0`, else `y_i=0`** (or a ceiling); MLE mixes the normal density `φ` (uncensored) and CDF `Φ` (censored mass). (c) *Quantifies the predictor–criterion relationship when the criterion itself is censored — e.g., a grade-level achievement test everyone at the top maxes out, censoring exactly the gifted tail (cross-ref A4's above-level-testing remedy).* (d) Use when values beyond a limit are observed-but-piled, not missing; distinguish censoring (Tobit) from incidental truncation/selection (Heckman §8). (e) Very sensitive to normality/homoskedasticity; a single index ties the censoring to the outcome (Heckman's two equations relax this); does not by itself solve MNAR *label* selection. — Tobin, J. (1958), *Econometrica* 26(1):24–36, DOI 10.2307/1907382 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**10. Range restriction as the correlational special case of selection bias (Case II/III; Sackett–Yang typology; cross-ref A4).** (a) Direct (Case II, selection on the predictor `X`) or indirect (Case III, selection on a third variable `Z`) truncation that mechanically deflates the validity coefficient in an already-admitted sample — the same MNAR problem in correlational clothing. (b) Case II correction already in A4 (`R = rU/√(1−r²+r²U²)`, `U=S_X/s_X`); **Sackett & Yang expand to 11 range-restriction scenarios** indexed by (which variable is selected on: x, y, or an unmeasured z), (whether unrestricted variances are known), and (whether z is measured) — each with a different estimator. (c) *Quantifies how much the observed tail validity understates the true validity because you only see selected children — the correlational face of "outcomes exist only for the selected."* (d) Requires the unrestricted SD (or a defensible external estimate); indirect (Case III/IV) needs the selection variable, which is exactly the unobserved quantity in MNAR — so the correction hits the same identification wall as §3–§4. (e) **Contested application:** Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022, in A4) show the standard artifact-distribution corrections systematically *overcorrect* (cognitive-ability validity .51→.31); prefer observed validity + a documented, scenario-matched correction and treat corrected values as an upper bound. Applicant-rights-neutral (describes existing selection, requires no denial). — Sackett, P. R., & Yang, H. (2000), *J. Applied Psychology* 85(1):112–118, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.85.1.112 [VERIFIED]; Thorndike (1949) Case II/III via A4 [SECONDARY]. **Established method; contested application.**

**11. Sharp & fuzzy Regression Discontinuity (RD) at the admission cut — the local counterfactual.** (a) The design that turns the admission cut into a natural experiment: children just above vs just below a known threshold are comparable, so the jump in outcomes at the cut is causal. Originated in an actual talent/near-merit-scholarship study. (b) **Sharp RD:** `τ_SRD = lim_{x↓c} E[Y|X=x] − lim_{x↑c} E[Y|X=x]`. **Fuzzy RD** (treatment not deterministic at `c`, e.g., eligibility ≠ enrollment): **`τ_FRD = [lim↓ − lim↑ of E[Y|X]] / [lim↓ − lim↑ of E[D|X]]`** — a ratio of jumps that is the Wald/IV estimator and identifies a **LATE for compliers at the cut** (Hahn–Todd–Van der Klaauw continuity framework). (c) *Quantifies exactly the charter's marginal question: "does the marginal admitted child benefit / would the marginal rejected child have?" — a local, contamination-free program effect, not a selection artifact.* (d) Identifying assumptions: potential-outcome means continuous in `X` at `c`, positive score density at `c`, and (fuzzy) a nonzero first stage; verify with §13. (e) **Estimates a LATE at the cut only** — silent on inframarginal / profoundly gifted children (cross-ref A1 tail precision, A4 local validity); **applicant-rights-clean because it exploits the cut the district already uses — no child is denied service to create the comparison.** — Thistlethwaite, D. L., & Campbell, D. T. (1960), *J. Educational Psychology* 51(6):309–317, DOI 10.1037/h0044319 [VERIFIED]; Hahn, J., Todd, P., & Van der Klaauw, W. (2001), *Econometrica* 69(1):201–209, DOI 10.1111/1468-0262.00183 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**12. Local-linear estimation, bandwidth choice, and robust bias-corrected CIs for RD.** (a) How you actually estimate the jump: fit a low-order local polynomial on each side of the cut within a bandwidth, and get honest confidence intervals. (b) **Local linear** (Fan–Gijbels; Porter 2003) reduces boundary bias vs kernel/global-polynomial fits: weighted least squares each side within window `h` (triangular kernel), `τ̂ = α̂_right − α̂_left`. **Bandwidth:** Imbens–Kalyanaraman MSE-optimal `h_IK`; **Calonico–Cattaneo–Titiunik** add a bias-corrected point estimate plus a **robust CI** that accounts for the variability of the bias correction (the `rdrobust` standard). (c) *Quantifies the cut effect with boundary-bias control and correctly-sized CIs — essential because the gifted tail has few children near the cut, so naïve fits and CIs are badly biased.* (d) Report estimates across several bandwidths and polynomial orders; prefer local linear over high-order global polynomials (Gelman–Imbens caution); use CCT robust CIs. (e) Estimates are sensitive to bandwidth/kernel/specification; the sparse tail yields wide CIs and low power (a real limit for a top-1–2% cut). — Imbens, G., & Kalyanaraman, K. (2012), *Review of Economic Studies* 79(3):933–959, DOI 10.1093/restud/rdr043 [VERIFIED]; Calonico, S., Cattaneo, M. D., & Titiunik, R. (2014), *Econometrica* 82(6):2295–2326, DOI 10.3982/ECTA11757 [VERIFIED]; practice guide Imbens, G., & Lemieux, T. (2008), *J. Econometrics* 142(2):615–635, DOI 10.1016/j.jeconom.2007.05.001 and Lee, D. S., & Lemieux, T. (2010), *J. Economic Literature* 48(2):281–355, DOI 10.1257/jel.48.2.281 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**13. RD validity diagnostics: manipulation/density tests + covariate-balance/placebo checks.** (a) The falsification tests that certify an RD is credible — critical when a gifted label is *desirable*, so families may push scores over the cut. (b) **McCrary density test:** test for a discontinuity in the running-variable density at `c` (`θ = ln f₊ − ln f₋`, H0: `θ=0`); **Cattaneo–Jansson–Ma `rddensity`** replaces McCrary's pre-binning with a local-polynomial density estimator (one tuning parameter, boundary-adaptive). Plus **covariate-continuity checks** (predetermined covariates should NOT jump at `c`) and **placebo cutoffs** (no effect at fake thresholds). (c) *Quantifies whether the cut is being gamed (retests, lobbying, discretionary overrides) — i.e., whether the "as-if random near the cut" assumption actually holds for a gifted admission rule.* (d) Preconditions: a **protected/immutable running variable** and a locked cut set before outcomes are known; report density test + covariate balance + placebo as a standard panel. (e) Passing these tests is necessary, not sufficient (continuity is untestable at the exact cut); power is low in the sparse tail; **applicant-rights link: manipulation detection protects fairness — it flags when advantaged families are crossing the cut through pressure rather than ability.** — McCrary, J. (2008), *J. Econometrics* 142(2):698–714, DOI 10.1016/j.jeconom.2007.05.005 [VERIFIED]; Cattaneo, M. D., Jansson, M., & Ma, X. (2020), *JASA* 115(531):1449–1455, DOI 10.1080/01621459.2019.1635480 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**14. Local randomization near the cut (finite-sample randomization inference).** (a) A complementary, weaker-large-sample-assumption approach: treat a small window around the cut as a genuine randomized experiment and use exact randomization/permutation inference. (b) In a window `W₀ = [c−w, c+w]`, assume treatment assignment is as-if random; conduct **Fisher randomization tests** (potential outcomes fixed, inference from the assignment distribution) rather than relying on local-linear extrapolation and large-sample normality. (c) *Quantifies the cut effect when only a handful of children sit near a top-1–2% threshold — the normal gifted situation — where the continuity-based large-sample RD (§12) is least trustworthy.* (d) Select the window `w` by covariate balance (smallest window where predetermined covariates are balanced); implemented in `rdlocrand`; handles discrete running variables and tiny samples. (e) Even smaller effective `n`, so power is very limited; results are sensitive to window selection; still a strictly local estimand. — Cattaneo, M. D., Frandsen, B. R., & Titiunik, R. (2015), *J. Causal Inference* 3(1):1–24, DOI 10.1515/jci-2013-0010 [VERIFIED]. **Established (newer; complement/robustness check to §11–§12).**

**15. Marginal-admit / waitlist / oversubscription-lottery designs — and why RD/lotteries beat propensity matching.** (a) The cleanest feasible causal designs: exploit an *existing* oversubscription lottery or waitlist as randomization; use propensity-score matching/weighting only as the observational fallback. (b) A random **offer instruments for enrollment**, yielding ITT and a complier **LATE** (Angrist–Imbens–Rubin IV/LATE); multi-cohort lotteries can be pooled via **design-based propensity scores** (Abdulkadiroğlu–Angrist–Narita–Pathak). **Propensity score** `e(x)=P(treat|X=x)` (Rosenbaum–Rubin) balances only *observed* covariates. (c) *Quantifies the program effect for marginal/complier gifted applicants and cleanly separates the selection effect from the program effect — directly answering the charter's "program effect ≠ selection effect."* (d) **Gifted-specific precedent (verified, repo deep-dives):** Bui–Craig–Imberman used a **fuzzy RD at the gifted-eligibility cut plus a gifted-magnet admission lottery** and found mostly **null** short-run effects at the margin (with attrition-bounded caveats); Card–Giuliano's within-school-rank fuzzy RD found **+0.27–0.34 SD** for high-achievers *yet a null IQ-threshold RD* (this is the AER tracking study — distinct from the Card–Giuliano *universal-screening* representation study cited in A6); Abdulkadiroğlu–Angrist–Pathak's exam-school fuzzy RD ("Elite Illusion") found large peer-quality gains but little near-cut achievement effect — three independent demonstrations that selective placement ≠ program benefit. (e) **Propensity/matching cannot remove unobserved selection (family motivation, resources) — "strong ignorability" is exactly what fails in gifted admission — so RD (identifies off a known assignment rule + continuity) and lotteries (actual randomization) are cleaner at a sharp cut**; requires an oversubscribed lottery/waitlist to exist `[UNVERIFIED GT-fact: whether the district runs one]`; differential **attrition can break it** (BCI) → bound with Lee (2009, §7). **Applicant-rights: lotteries/waitlists randomize only among the already-eligible or over-capacity, so no entitled child is denied service to generate data — the ethically admissible substitute for deliberate denial.** — Angrist, J. D., Imbens, G. W., & Rubin, D. B. (1996), *JASA* 91(434):444–455, DOI 10.1080/01621459.1996.10476902 [VERIFIED]; Rosenbaum, P. R., & Rubin, D. B. (1983), *Biometrika* 70(1):41–55, DOI 10.1093/biomet/70.1.41 [VERIFIED]; Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist, Narita & Pathak (2017), *Econometrica* 85(5), DOI 10.3982/ECTA13925 [VERIFIED]; Bui, Craig & Imberman (2014), *AEJ: Economic Policy* 6(3):30–62, DOI 10.1257/pol.6.3.30 [VERIFIED]; Card & Giuliano (2016), *American Economic Review* 106(10):2783–2816, DOI 10.1257/aer.20150484 [VERIFIED]; Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist & Pathak (2014), *Econometrica* 82(1), DOI 10.3982/ECTA10266 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**16. Synthesis — the honest MNAR-aware estimation protocol for a gifted screen at the tail.** (a) A decision pipeline composing §1–§15 that keeps A9 (selection into being labeled/treated), A5 (measurement bias), and A2 (base rates) as three separate, non-substitutable numbers. ### Wave 5 · A10 — Comparative test-effectiveness methodology & the rigorous "~2× better than CogAT" statement (2026-07-22)

*Scope:* How to VALIDLY compare two different ability tests' effectiveness at the gifted tail — the common-metric (equating/linking) prerequisite, inference on the DIFFERENCE (not each test alone), agreement vs correlation, CogAT-referenced skill scores, and a rigorous adjudication of exactly what "~2× better than CogAT" can and cannot mean — extending A1/A6/A7/A8 with the comparison layer.

**1. Relative efficiency (RE) in IRT + its test-length reading (extends A1/A7).** **(a)** RE is the ratio of two tests' Fisher test-information functions at the same ability θ; Lord defines the "relative efficiency of test x to test y" and shows it varies across θ. **(b)** RE(θ) = I_A(θ) / I_B(θ), with I(θ) = Σ_i [P′_i(θ)]² / [P_i(θ)Q_i(θ)] and CSEM(θ) = 1/√I(θ); test-length reading: because information is additive over (exchangeable) items, RE(θ_cut) = 2 ⇒ B needs ~2× the items of A to match A's precision at θ_cut, i.e. n_B/n_A ≈ Ī_A(θ_cut)/Ī_B(θ_cut). **(c)** *Quantifies how many more CogAT items would be needed to match the new adaptive test's measurement precision exactly at the gifted cut θ_cut, equivalently the ratio of squared CSEMs at that θ.* **(d)** Compute from item parameters placed on a common IRT metric (requires linking — Findings 4–5); RE=2 ⇔ CSEM²_B/CSEM²_A = 2 ⇔ ~2× items. Assumes unidimensionality, model fit, local independence, and equal per-item information at θ_cut; evaluate AT the decision point, never averaged. **(e)** RE is local (θ-specific), so any "2×" must name θ_cut; adaptive tests concentrate information at θ so tail RE>1 is expected but is a *precision* claim, not validity or outcome; per-item-equality is idealized. — Lord, F. M. (1980), *Applications of Item Response Theory to Practical Testing Problems*, ch. 6, Lawrence Erlbaum/Routledge, DOI 10.4324/9780203056615; precursor Lord (1973/1977), ETS RB-73-41 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**2. Asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE) / Pitman efficiency — the general statistical parent.** **(a)** The distribution-free concept behind "half the items/data": the limiting ratio of sample sizes two procedures need for equal power against the same local alternative. **(b)** ARE(A,B) = lim_{n→∞}(n_B/n_A) at fixed power = (e_A/e_B)², where Pitman–Noether efficacy e = lim [ (d/dθ)E_θ(T_n)|_{θ0} ] / [√n · sd_{θ0}(T_n) ]. **(c)** *Quantifies the general data/test-size multiplier — ARE=2 means the weaker instrument needs 2× the information to reach the same tail-discrimination power — and supplies the theoretical warrant for reading RE (Finding 1) as a test-length ratio.* **(d)** Requires a local-alternative asymptotic frame; interpretation is a pure ratio on [0,∞), hence legitimately "doublable." **(e)** Asymptotic (may mislead at finite n or with very few tail cases); defined for statistics/estimators, so applying it to whole "tests" needs the IRT-information bridge of Finding 1 to be exact. — Noether, G. E. (1955), *Annals of Mathematical Statistics* 26(1):64–68, DOI 10.1214/aoms/1177728593 [VERIFIED]; textbook treatment Serfling (1980), *Approximation Theorems of Mathematical Statistics*, DOI 10.1002/9780470316481 [SECONDARY]. **Established.**

**3. Spearman–Brown as the reliability-based "how much longer must B be" (extends A3).** **(a)** Prophecy formula relating reliability to test length; inverted, it gives the length multiplier B needs to match A's reliability. **(b)** ρ_kk = kρ / [1 + (k−1)ρ]; to reach target ρ*: k = ρ*(1−ρ) / [ρ(1−ρ*)]; length ratio to match A: k = ρ_A(1−ρ_B) / [ρ_B(1−ρ_A)]. **(c)** *Quantifies the number of extra CogAT items needed to raise its global reliability to the new test's — a classical-test-theory analogue of RE, but on whole-score reliability, not tail information.* **(d)** Assumes appended items are parallel/exchangeable; interpret against the attenuation ceiling (A3). **(e)** Reliability is a whole-population index, NOT tail-specific, and is BOUNDED in [0,1] (so reliability itself is not "doublable"); the parallel-items assumption fails for adaptive tests and near ceiling — prefer RE/ARE at θ_cut for the tail claim. — Spearman, C. (1910), *British Journal of Psychology* 3(3):271–295, DOI 10.1111/j.2044-8295.1910.tb00206.x; Brown, W. (1910), *ibid.* 3(3):296–322, DOI 10.1111/j.2044-8295.1910.tb00207.x [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**4. The equating/linking prerequisite and its designs (Kolen & Brennan).** **(a)** Two different tests share NO common metric until linked; equating is the strongest link (interchangeable scores). Data-collection designs: single-group (SG), single-group-with-counterbalancing, equivalent/random-groups (EG), and common-item non-equivalent-groups (CINEG/NEAT) with internal or external anchor. **(b)** Linear: l_Y(x) = μ_Y + (σ_Y/σ_X)(x−μ_X); equipercentile: e_Y(x) = G_Y⁻¹(F_X(x)); IRT true-score equating maps through θ. Equating requirements: equal construct, equal reliability, symmetry, population invariance, equity. **(c)** *Establishes the common yardstick without which ANY new-test-vs-CogAT comparison (RE, ΔAUC, Δr, agreement) is uninterpretable — it is the precondition, not itself an effectiveness metric.* **(d)** For a new adaptive battery vs CogAT (arguably different constructs), full equating usually fails equal-construct/equity, forcing weaker linking (Finding 5); an anchor must include common items spanning θ_cut. **(e)** Equating assumptions rarely hold across publishers/constructs; population invariance is routinely violated at the tail, and equating error inflates at extreme scores — exactly where the gifted cut lives (sparse data). — Kolen, M. J., & Brennan, R. L. (2014), *Test Equating, Scaling, and Linking: Methods and Practices*, 3rd ed., Springer, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-0317-7 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**5. Degrees of linking — what comparative claim each rung licenses (Linn).** **(a)** Linn's ladder of decreasing inferential strength: **equating > calibration > (statistical) moderation/concordance > prediction > social moderation.** This ceiling determines how strong a "2×" statement is allowed. **(b)** No single formula; concordance = equipercentile linking of non-parallel tests → a concordance table (X ↔ Y at equal percentile ranks); prediction = regression E[Y|X] (asymmetric, regresses extremes toward the mean). **(c)** *Fixes the ceiling on the headline claim: only equated/calibrated scores license a metric-for-metric efficiency ratio; concordance/prediction license "corresponds to"/"predicts," NOT "is 2× better."* **(d)** Diagnostic: if constructs differ, you are at best in concordance/prediction territory, so a defensible RE comparison requires shared IRT calibration — whereas ΔAUC/Δr comparisons route AROUND equating by scoring both tests on the SAME students against a common EXTERNAL criterion (A4), the cleaner GT design. **(e)** Concordance tables are population- and time-specific and degrade at the tail; prediction is directional and shrinks extremes. — Linn, R. L. (1993), *Applied Measurement in Education* 6(1):83–102, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame0601_5 [VERIFIED]; Dorans, N. J. (2004), "Linking Assessments: Concept and History," *Applied Psychological Measurement* 28(4), DOI 10.1177/0146621604265030 [SECONDARY]. **Established (framework); Contested (where any given real CogAT-vs-new linkage actually falls).**

**6. DeLong test for ΔAUC on the SAME students (extends A6).** **(a)** Nonparametric test of H0: AUC_A = AUC_B when both tests are scored on the same paired subjects, using generalized U-statistic (placement-value) covariance. **(b)** Δ = ÂUC_A − ÂUC_B; z = Δ / √[Var(ÂUC_A) + Var(ÂUC_B) − 2·Cov(ÂUC_A, ÂUC_B)], with variances/covariance from structural (placement) components. **(c)** *Quantifies whether the new test's overall separation of gifted vs not-gifted exceeds CogAT's beyond sampling noise, using the paired design to gain power.* **(d)** Cross-ref A6; requires ONE shared definition of "truly gifted" for both tests (the criterion problem, A4). Report Δ with a CI, not just p. **(e)** AUC is BOUNDED [0.5,1] and integrates over ALL thresholds — a poor match for a top-1–2% claim; use partial AUC over the tail FPR region or fixed-θ_cut sensitivity instead; genuine tail differences often produce tiny whole-curve ΔAUC. — DeLong, E. R., DeLong, D. M., & Clarke-Pearson, D. L. (1988), *Biometrics* 44(3):837–845, DOI 10.2307/2531595 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**7. Hanley–McNeil correlated-AUC method + the SE(AUC) closed form (extends A6).** **(a)** The earlier paired-AUC comparison via the Wilcoxon–AUC identity and a table converting rating correlations into an AUC–AUC correlation; also the closed-form SE(AUC) that powers sample-size math. **(b)** AUC = Wilcoxon W; SE(AUC) = √{ [AUC(1−AUC) + (n₊−1)(Q1−AUC²) + (n₋−1)(Q2−AUC²)] / (n₊·n₋) }, Q1 = AUC/(2−AUC), Q2 = 2AUC²/(1+AUC); paired: SE(Δ) = √(SE_A² + SE_B² − 2·r·SE_A·SE_B). **(c)** *Quantifies the paired-design standard error of the CogAT-vs-new AUC gap and underlies the closed-form sample size (Finding 16).* **(d)** Cross-ref A6; r comes from their binormal correlation table. **(e)** Binormal assumption for the correlation table; superseded by DeLong for the covariance; same AUC-boundedness caveat as Finding 6. — Hanley, J. **8. Bootstrap for Δ(any metric) — the general "×" engine (extends A6).** **(a)** Resampling to get a CI/test for the difference in ANY effectiveness metric with no closed form (Δ precision@k, Δ Youden J, Δ partial AUC, Δ net benefit); the paired/cluster bootstrap preserves the same-student correlation. **(b)** For b=1…B resample subjects with replacement, recompute BOTH tests' metric, Δ*_b = m_A*_b − m_B*_b; percentile CI [Δ*_(α/2), Δ*_(1−α/2)] or BCa (bias- and acceleration-corrected). **9. Feldt test for dependent RELIABILITIES (extends A3).** **(a)** Significance test of H0: reliability (Cronbach α) equal for two tests taken by the SAME sample (dependent αs). **(b)** Feldt's statistic references the ratio (1−α_A)/(1−α_B) to an F-type distribution corrected for the between-form dependence; tight Type-I control for n ≥ 50. **(c)** *Quantifies whether the new test is more reliable than CogAT beyond sampling noise — a global-precision companion to RE.* **(d)** Cross-ref A3; for small (parts × subjects) use the Alsawalmeh–Feldt (1994) modification, DOI 10.1007/BF02294264. **(e)** Reliability is BOUNDED and GLOBAL, not tail-specific; higher α ≠ better tail separation; α assumes essential τ-equivalence. — Feldt, L. **10. [INFERENCE] a small whole-curve ΔAUC ≈ 0.02 can require ~900–3,700/group whereas ΔAUC ≈ 0.10 needs ~36–142/group; a tail decision target (halving a small FN rate at θ_cut) is usually more powerful and cheaper — another reason to prefer the FN/NND target of Finding 14. Enrichment (oversampling near θ_cut) raises efficiency. **(e)** Closed forms assume binormal/large-sample; the extreme tail has few gifted cases, so power is the binding constraint; pilot estimates of ρ and variances are required [UNVERIFIED]. — Obuchowski, N. A. (1998), *Statistical Methods in Medical Research* 7(4):371–392, DOI 10.1177/096228029800700405; Hanley & McNeil (1982), DOI 10.1148/radiology.143.1.7063747 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**(g) Newly discovered dimensions → queue:**
- **Partial AUC / AUC over the restricted tail-FPR region** (McClish 1989; Dodd & Pepe 2003) as the *correct* discrimination metric for a top-percentile claim — bridges A6/A8/A10 and fixes the AUC-boundedness problem.
- **Population invariance of linking at the tail** (Dorans & Holland RMSD(SEE) statistics) — a formal test that any CogAT concordance is stable in the top 1–2%, not just on average.
- **Net-benefit difference & "test tradeoff"** (Vickers) to express the FN reduction in interpretable "gifted-children-per-N" units at the district's policy threshold.
- **Multi-reader multi-case (Obuchowski–Rockette / Hillis)** if multiple CogAT batteries, raters, or nomination sources feed the decision.
- **Clustered/hierarchical resampling & mixed-model SEs** (students nested in classrooms/schools) for every difference inference above.
- **Cost-adjusted / utility-per-item efficiency** — because adaptive tests trade item count for precision, a "×" in items-at-equal-precision should be reported alongside testing-time and burden.
- **Bayesian model comparison / value-of-information** as a non-NHST alternative for the head-to-head decision.

### Wave 6 · A11 — Benefit-based / aptitude-treatment-interaction validity & local (tail) predictive validity (2026-07-22)

*Scope: methods that quantify whether a selection score identifies the children who **benefit** from the gifted service (aptitude×treatment interaction / conditional treatment-effect heterogeneity / uplift), and whether predictive validity holds **locally at the cut** — keeping three claims strictly separate throughout: "scores high" (level, A1/A4), "predicts who benefits" (benefit-validity, THIS pass), and "the program works" (identified impact, A9). Labels: [VERIFIED] = DOI/official page resolved this pass; worked numbers [INFERENCE] unless drawn from a source; "Established" vs "Contested" flagged. Applicant-rights note: benefit-based selection can conflict with desert/fairness for a scarce seat — this pass flags that tension and does not resolve it.*

**1. Benefit-/utility-based selection vs top-down predictor selection (Cronbach–Gleser decision theory).** **(a)** Reframes selection from "admit the highest scorers" to "choose the assignment that maximizes expected institutional payoff," and separates *selection* (accept/reject on one criterion) from *classification/placement* (route each person to the treatment that maximizes their own outcome). **(b)** Expected utility of a decision rule `E[U] = Σ_treatments ∫ U(y)·f(y | treatment, x) dP(x)`; the classical selection-utility gain is linear in validity (Brogden–Cronbach–Gleser, A6). The decisive move for placement: the value added by differential placement is governed by the **aptitude×treatment interaction**, not the main-effect validity — if outcomes are parallel across treatments (no ATI), sorting adds **zero** utility no matter how valid or reliable the test. **(c)** *Quantifies a gifted seat by its expected realized benefit, not its expected score — so a test is "effective for selection" only insofar as sorting on it raises total outcomes over not sorting.* **(d)** Requires (i) a utility metric on outcomes and (ii) treatment-specific outcome predictions; for placement decisions read the interaction, not the correlation. Cross-ref A6 (Taylor–Russell, BCG utility). **(e)** Institutional utility can diverge from the individual's and from desert; utilities are value-laden and usually unmeasured in K–8 GT. — Cronbach, L. J., & Gleser, G. C. (1965), *Psychological Tests and Personnel Decisions*, 2nd ed., Univ. of Illinois Press (1st ed. 1957); ISBN 0-252-00003-X, OCLC 190689 [VERIFIED existence]. **Established.**

**2. Aptitude-Treatment Interaction (ATI): the core model + origin.** **(a)** The claim that a treatment's effect depends on the learner's aptitude — Cronbach's proposed fusion of experimental and correlational psychology into "a science of Aptitude×Treatment interactions." **(b)** `Y = β₀ + β₁·A + β₂·T + β₃·(A×T) + ε` (T = 0/1); the ATI is **β₃**. Equivalently two within-treatment regressions `Y = a₀ + a₁A` (control) and `Y = b₀ + b₁A` (treatment); the treatment effect at aptitude A is `τ(A) = (b₀−a₀) + (b₁−a₁)·A`, and the interaction is the slope difference `(b₁−a₁)`. **(c)** *Quantifies whether the gifted service helps high-aptitude children MORE than others — the statistical object a "gifted track" must rest on.* **(d)** Center A; test β₃ with adequate power (Finding 5); interpret `τ(A)` across the range, not just its mean. **(e)** A significant β₃ is associational; it is causal only under an identified design (A9). Cross-ref A4 (differential prediction / moderation). — Cronbach, L. J. (1957), "The two disciplines of scientific psychology," *American Psychologist* 12(11):671–684, DOI 10.1037/h0043943 [VERIFIED]; Cronbach & Snow (1977), *Aptitudes and Instructional Methods* (Irvington) [VERIFIED existence]. **Established.**

**3. Ordinal vs disordinal (crossover) ATI + region of significance — what actually justifies a track.** **(a)** The *shape* of the ATI decides whether differential placement is warranted at all. **(b)** **Ordinal:** within the meaningful aptitude range the two treatment regression lines do not cross (one treatment dominates everywhere, slopes differ). **Disordinal (crossover):** the lines cross at `A* = −(b₀−a₀)/(b₁−a₁)`; below A* one treatment wins, above it the other. **Johnson–Neyman region of significance:** the set of A where `τ(A)/SE[τ(A)] = ±t_crit`, i.e., where the treatment difference is reliably nonzero. **(c)** *Quantifies the assignment cut: only a disordinal ATI with a crossover inside the gifted-relevant range statistically justifies sending some children to the gifted program and others elsewhere. An ordinal ATI says "the program helps everyone (a bit more at the top)" — which is a capacity/fairness rationing problem, not a benefit-matching one.* **(d)** Locate A* and the J–N bounds; confirm the crossover lies within observed aptitude (not extrapolated); a benefit-defensible gifted cut requires `θ_cut ≈ A*` or that θ_cut sits inside the region where the gifted service reliably wins. Cross-ref A7 (cut-scores). **(e)** Crossover location is often very imprecise in the tail (wide CIs); disordinal interactions are far rarer than ordinal ones. — Cronbach & Snow (1977) [VERIFIED existence]; Johnson–Neyman region-of-significance technique (1936) [SECONDARY, not independently resolved this pass]. **Established (framework); Contested (whether a disordinal GT ATI exists).**

**4. The historical difficulty of replicating ATIs — and why a null ATI is a real finding.** **(a)** Cronbach's own retrospective: two decades of ATI research yielded few robust, replicable interactions; higher-order interactions and local context dominate. **(b)** No formula — the empirical regularity is inconsistent sign/magnitude of β₃ across "roughly similar inquiries," and effects that fail to generalize beyond the original setting (Cronbach 1975). **(c)** *Quantifies the prior for a gifted-track ATI: expect small, unstable interactions — so failing to show the gifted benefit MORE is the modal result and must be treated as informative, not as a nuisance to be explained away.* **(d)** Pre-register the ATI/CATE; treat a null (or again-null) β₃ as evidence against benefit-based sorting; require independent replication before acting on any positive ATI. **(e)** Cronbach concluded broadly generalizable ATIs may be unattainable — an influential but contested pessimism. Charter link: null/harmful/inconclusive findings are valid outcomes. — Cronbach, L. J. (1975), "Beyond the two disciplines of scientific psychology," *American Psychologist* 30(2):116–127, DOI 10.1037/h0076829 [VERIFIED]; Cronbach & Snow (1977) [VERIFIED existence]. **Contested (interpretation); the replication difficulty is Established.**

**5. Low power of interaction/moderator detection (McClelland–Judd) — the power tax on every ATI/CATE claim.** **(a)** Field/observational tests of interactions have drastically less power than experiments because, once the component main effects are partialled out, the interaction term's residual variance is tiny. **(b)** The residual variance of `A×T` is a function of the joint distribution of A and T (concentrated/unbalanced ⇒ smaller); the authors derive that **field tests of interactions often have < 20% of the statistical efficiency of an optimal (extreme-corners, balanced) experimental design** — equivalently the detectable interaction needs several-fold larger N. **(c)** *Quantifies why "we found no ATI / no heterogeneity" is weak evidence of homogeneity: at a top-1–2% cut with few near-cut children and an unbalanced treated/control split, power to detect who-benefits differences is often floor-level.* **(d)** Report the power / minimum-detectable interaction for β₃ (not just the outcome main effect); oversample the aptitude and treatment extremes; cross-ref A4 (Aguinis, Culpepper & Pierce 2010 differential-prediction low-power critique), A6 (powering), A10 (paired-design powering). **(e)** Boosting power by extreme sampling distorts representativeness; measurement error in A or T further attenuates β₃ (compounding the tail penalty). — McClelland, G. H., & Judd, C. M. (1993), *Psychological Bulletin* 114(2):376–390, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.114.2.376 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**6. CATE / treatment-effect heterogeneity — the modern "who benefits" estimand.** **(a)** The conditional average treatment effect generalizes the ATI to many covariates and flexible functional forms; it is the who-benefits score. **(b)** `τ(x) = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | X = x]` in the potential-outcomes framework. The **individual** effect `Y_i(1)−Y_i(0)` is never observed (fundamental problem of causal inference), so only the conditional average `τ(x)` is estimable; identification needs unconfoundedness `{Y(0),Y(1)} ⟂ W | X` + overlap `0 < e(x) < 1`, **or** a design (A9). **(c)** *Quantifies each applicant's predicted program benefit given their profile — the quantity a benefit-based rule should rank on, logically distinct from predicted achievement level.* **(d)** Estimate with Findings 7–11; validate with 12–13; never read `τ̂(x)` as an individual guarantee; always report uncertainty. **(e)** Under selection-on-unobservables (family motivation, resources), `τ(x)` is **not** identified from observational data — the same wall as A9; a CATE estimate is causal only under a design or ignorability. — Potential-outcomes / Rubin causal model (cross-ref A9: Rubin 1974/1976) [VERIFIED via A9]; framing Established.

**7. Meta-learners: S-, T-, and X-learner (Künzel et al.).** **(a)** Model-agnostic recipes that convert any regression learner into a CATE estimator. **(b)** **S-learner:** one model `μ(x,w)`; `τ̂(x)=μ̂(x,1)−μ̂(x,0)`. **T-learner:** separate `μ̂₁,μ̂₀` on treated/control; `τ̂(x)=μ̂₁(x)−μ̂₀(x)`. **X-learner:** (1) fit `μ̂₀,μ̂₁`; (2) impute `D̃¹ᵢ=Yᵢ−μ̂₀(Xᵢ)` for treated and `D̃⁰ᵢ=μ̂₁(Xᵢ)−Yᵢ` for control; (3) regress each on X → `τ̂₁,τ̂₀`; (4) `τ̂(x)=g(x)τ̂₀(x)+(1−g(x))τ̂₁(x)`, weight `g≈ê(x)`. **(c)** *Quantifies who-benefits with off-the-shelf ML; the X-learner is the tail-relevant one because gifted-program arms are highly unbalanced (few treated at the very top, or few controls) and it borrows strength across arms.* **(d)** X-learner is provably efficient when one arm's N ≫ the other's and can reach the parametric rate if `τ` is simple (e.g., linear) even when the response surfaces are complex; choose base learners by CV; prefer honest/cross-fit variants. **(e)** The S-learner shrinks τ toward 0 (regularization may drop W); the T-learner is high-variance under imbalance; none is uniformly best. — Künzel, S. R., Sekhon, J. S., Bickel, P. J., & Yu, B. (2019), *PNAS* 116(10):4156–4165, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1804597116 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**8. Causal trees & forests: honesty + valid confidence intervals (Athey–Imbens; Wager–Athey; GRF).** **(a)** Tree/forest methods that split to maximize treatment-effect *heterogeneity* and deliver CIs for `τ(x)`. **(b)** **Honest estimation:** partition the sample — one subsample chooses the tree structure, a disjoint subsample estimates `τ` within leaves (removes adaptive-selection bias). **Causal forest:** `τ̂(x)=Σᵢ αᵢ(x)·(pseudo-outcome)` with forest-derived adaptive weights `αᵢ(x)`; the estimator is pointwise consistent and asymptotically Gaussian → `τ̂(x) ± z·σ̂(x)`. **GRF:** fits any parameter defined by local moment conditions via gradient-based ("labeling") splits that target heterogeneity in that parameter. **(c)** *Quantifies who-benefits with honest per-profile uncertainty — required before acting on a "these children benefit most" claim for scarce seats.* **(d)** Honesty buys ~nominal coverage (Athey–Imbens: **90% CIs cover ~90%** vs **74–84%** for non-honest trees) at a **7–22% MSE cost**; needs unconfoundedness + overlap or a design; implemented in `grf`. **(e)** CIs are *pointwise* (multiplicity across many x), and are wide in the sparse tail; forests extrapolate poorly where overlap is thin (the extreme top). — Athey, S., & Imbens, G. (2016), *PNAS* 113(27):7353–7360, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1510489113; Wager, S., & Athey, S. (2018), *JASA* 113(523):1228–1242, DOI 10.1080/01621459.2017.1319839; Athey, S., Tibshirani, J., & Wager, S. (2019), *Annals of Statistics* 47(2):1148–1178, DOI 10.1214/18-AOS1709 [all VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**9. Orthogonal / quasi-oracle CATE: Double ML + R-learner (Robinson decomposition).** **(a)** Two-stage methods that "partial out" nuisance functions so the CATE estimate is first-order insensitive to nuisance errors. **(b)** **Robinson decomposition:** `Y − m(X) = (W − e(X))·τ(X) + ε`, with `m(x)=E[Y|X=x]`, `e(x)=P(W=1|X=x)`. **R-learner:** `τ̂ = argmin_τ (1/n)Σᵢ[(Yᵢ − m̂(Xᵢ)) − (Wᵢ − ê(Xᵢ))·τ(Xᵢ)]² + Λ(τ)` (the "R-loss"). **DML:** estimate the target via a Neyman-orthogonal moment + K-fold **cross-fitting** to remove regularization/overfitting bias → `√n`-consistent, asymptotically normal. **(c)** *Quantifies who-benefits robustly when confounders/nuisances are complex — the real GT-admissions situation (many correlated background variables).* **(d)** Quasi-oracle property: the R-learner attains oracle error bounds if `m̂,ê` converge faster than `n^{−1/4}`; always cross-fit; any ML method may be used for the nuisances. **(e)** Still requires overlap + unconfoundedness (or a design); thin tail overlap inflates variance through the `(W−ê)` weights; orthogonality cannot fix omitted confounders. — Nie, X., & Wager, S. (2021), *Biometrika* 108(2):299–319, DOI 10.1093/biomet/asaa076; Chernozhukov, V., Chetverikov, D., Demirer, M., Duflo, E., Hansen, C., Newey, W., & Robins, J. (2018), *The Econometrics Journal* 21(1):C1–C68, DOI 10.1111/ectj.12097 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**10. Bayesian Causal Forest (Hahn–Murray–Carvalho) — regularize the effect separately + defeat regularization-induced confounding.** **(a)** A BART-based model purpose-built for the GT-tail regime: small effects, real heterogeneity, and strong confounding by observables. **(b)** `Yᵢ = μ(xᵢ, π̂(xᵢ)) + τ(xᵢ)·zᵢ + εᵢ` — a **prognostic** function `μ` and a **treatment-effect** function `τ` each get their own BART prior; the estimated propensity `π̂(x)` enters `μ` as a covariate to remove **regularization-induced confounding (RIC)**, and `τ`'s prior can be tightened to "shrink to homogeneity." **(c)** *Quantifies who-benefits while guarding the two failure modes most dangerous for a gifted CATE: confounding-driven false heterogeneity, and over-fit effect curves that manufacture spurious "who benefits" structure.* **(d)** Include `π̂` as a covariate as routine practice (their explicit recommendation); set a tighter prior on `τ` than on `μ`; report posterior intervals for `τ(x)` and for subgroup ATEs. **(e)** Bayesian-model-dependent; still needs ignorability + overlap; posterior intervals are not design-based guarantees. — Hahn, P. R., Murray, J. S., & Carvalho, C. M. (2020), *Bayesian Analysis* 15(3):965–1056, DOI 10.1214/19-BA1195 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**11. From who-benefits to who-to-admit: policy learning / optimal treatment rules with a capacity constraint.** **(a)** Skips estimating every `τ(x)` and directly learns the assignment rule `π:X→{0,1}` that maximizes welfare inside a feasible, capacity-limited policy class — the actual gifted-seat allocation problem. **(b)** Policy value `V(π)=E[Y(π(X))]`; gain over never-treat `= E[π(X)·τ(X)]`. **EWM** (Kitagawa–Tetenov): `π̂ = argmax_{π∈Π} (1/n)Σᵢ π(Xᵢ)·Γ̂ᵢ^{IPW}`. **Athey–Wager doubly-robust score:** `Γ̂ᵢ = τ̂(Xᵢ) + (Wᵢ−êᵢ)/(êᵢ(1−êᵢ))·(Yᵢ − m̂_{Wᵢ}(Xᵢ))`; `π̂ = argmax (1/n)Σ(2π(Xᵢ)−1)Γ̂ᵢ`; **regret** `V(π*)−V(π̂) ≲ √(VC(Π)/n)` with cross-fitting. **Capacity:** `Σᵢ π(Xᵢ) ≤ B` (B = seats). **(c)** *Quantifies the best implementable admit-rule for a FIXED number of seats, ranking on benefit inside an explicit capacity- and fairness-constrained class — the decision-theoretic endpoint of a "who benefits" test.* **(d)** Bake capacity into the learning (never learn an unconstrained rule then truncate); cross-fit nuisances; evaluate on untouched data; promote only if the held-out policy-value lower bound beats a lottery by a pre-registered margin. JTPA illustration: linear EWM treated **69%** of the population vs a predictive plug-in's **86%** — targeting on benefit ≠ targeting on predicted level. **(e)** Valid only after identified outcomes exist (A9); double-robustness does **not** repair unmeasured confounding, selective labels, attrition, or population drift; in-sample welfare overstates value; a benefit-max rule can conflict with desert/fairness (Finding 16). — Kitagawa, T., & Tetenov, A. (2018), *Econometrica* 86(2):591–616, DOI 10.3982/ECTA13288; Athey, S., & Wager, S. (2021), *Econometrica* 89(1):133–161, DOI 10.3982/ECTA15732 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

**12. Evaluating a who-benefits score: uplift / Qini curves & RATE.** **(a)** How to score the *ranking* quality of any CATE/uplift model when the true individual effect is unobservable — via group-wise incremental outcomes across the ranked population. **(b)** Order units by `τ̂` descending; **Qini curve** `Q(φ) = n_{t,1}(φ) − n_{c,1}(φ)·(N_t/N_c)` vs targeted fraction `φ`; **Qini coefficient** = area between the Qini curve and the random-targeting diagonal (uplift analog of Gini). Modern academic version: **RATE / TOC** (rank-weighted ATE; area under the Targeting Operator Characteristic), with valid CIs, in `grf`. **(c)** *Quantifies whether the test/score actually orders children by program benefit — does treating the top-ranked few yield more total gain than treating a random few?* **(d)** Requires randomized/identified treatment AND control spanning the ranking; report Qini/AUTOC with CIs; a flat curve ⇒ no useful heterogeneity ⇒ fall back to a lottery. Cross-ref A8 (ranking metrics, calibration). **(e)** Qini is sensitive to the treated/control split and base rate and is noisy in small tail samples; ranking quality ≠ calibrated magnitude (Finding 13). — Radcliffe, N. J. (2007), *Direct Marketing Analytics Journal* (DMA), 14–21 [SECONDARY/industry]; Gutierrez, P., & Gérardy, J.-Y. (2017), "Causal Inference and Uplift Modelling," *PMLR* 67:1–13 [VERIFIED existence]; `grf` RATE/TOC documentation [SECONDARY]. **Established (framework); Contested (which metric).**

**13. Validating & calibrating the CATE: BLP / GATES / CLAN + honest splitting.** **(a)** Post-hoc inference that tests whether an estimated who-benefits score reflects *real* heterogeneity or noise, with honest sample-splitting and multiplicity control. **(b)** Build an ML proxy `S(X)≈τ(X)` on a training split; on a held-out split, using the AIPW/doubly-robust score: **BLP** — regress the score on S: `Γ̂ = β₀ + β₁(S − E[S]) + …`; `β₁ = 1` ⇒ well-calibrated slope, `β₁ ≈ 0` ⇒ no detectable heterogeneity. **GATES** — group by S-quantiles and estimate group ATEs `γ₁ ≤ … ≤ γ_K`; monotone-increasing ⇒ real sorting by benefit. **CLAN** — compare covariate means of most- vs least-affected groups. **Calibration of the CATE:** `E[τ(X) | τ̂(X)=t] = t` (treatment-effect analog of A8 probability calibration). **(c)** *Quantifies "is our who-benefits score trustworthy enough to allocate seats?"* **(d)** Pre-register; use repeated splits with quantile aggregation; report multiplicity-adjusted intervals; require GATES monotonicity + a BLP `β₁` CI excluding 0 before acting. **(e)** Heterogeneity discovery is prone to false positives; power is low at the tail (Finding 5); a passed test is still not an individual causal guarantee. — Chernozhukov, V., Demirer, M., Duflo, E., & Fernández-Val, I., "Fisher–Schultz Lecture: Generic Machine Learning Inference on Heterogeneous Treatment Effects in Randomized Experiments," *Econometrica* (2025), DOI 10.3982/ECTA19303 (widely cited working paper arXiv:1712.04802) [VERIFIED]. **Established (methods); Contested (finite-sample behavior).**

**14. Local (tail) predictive validity — validity AT the cut, not on average.** **(a)** The formal complement to A1/A10 measurement-at-the-cut: does the SCORE predict the OUTCOME specifically in the near-cut band, rather than an omnibus `r` that mixes the whole range? **(b)** Options: (i) **banded validity** `r_local = corr(S, Y | S ∈ [θ_cut ± h])` — heavily range-restricted, so usually small; interpret with A4/A9 corrections; (ii) **local regression slope** `β_local = dE[Y|S=s]/ds |_{s=θ_cut}` from a local-linear fit; (iii) **RD-augmented** — pair `β_local` with the fuzzy-RD LATE at `θ_cut` (A9); (iv) **local discrimination** — AUC / partial-AUC computed only in the tail band (A8/A10). **(c)** *Quantifies whether the test still "works" exactly where the decision is made — a headline validity of .5 can hide a near-zero predictive slope at the top-1–2% cut (or the reverse).* **(d)** Report validity as a curve / at-`θ_cut` value, never a single omnibus `r`; correct the banded `r` for direct/indirect restriction (A4/A9); do not *assume* linearity — Coward & Sackett found ability–performance ≈ linear across the range (so the average slope is a reasonable prior), but with acknowledged low power to detect tail departures, so verify locally. **(e)** Tiny near-cut N ⇒ unstable local estimates; local validity is a level/prediction claim, still distinct from benefit (Finding 15) and from program impact (A9). — Coward, W. M., & Sackett, P. R. (1990), *Journal of Applied Psychology* 75(3):297–300, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.75.3.297 [VERIFIED]; composition of A1/A4/A9/A10 [INFERENCE]. **Established (concept); Contested (tail linearity).**

**15. Benefit as the validity TARGET — "does a higher screen score predict a larger program effect?" (the null-at-margin precedent).** **(a)** The deepest validity test: regress the *identified program benefit* (not achievement level) on the screen score and ask whether higher-scoring children gain MORE. **(b)** Take `τ(x)` from an RD/lottery LATE (A9); test `Cov(S, τ) > 0`, or that `E[τ(X) | S=s]` increases in s, or GATES monotonic in S (Finding 13). A benefit-valid screen has a positive slope near the cut; a benefit-invalid one is flat or negative there. **(c)** *Quantifies the property GT actually needs — that the test selects children who benefit — logically independent of selecting children who score high (A1/A4) or who will achieve high (A4).* **(d)** Requires an identified benefit estimate + adequate near-cut power (Finding 5); report the slope with CIs; a null slope is decision-relevant (favor a lottery or universal service). **(e)** **Verified precedent that the near-cut benefit slope can be ZERO or mis-aligned with the ability screen:** Bui–Craig–Imberman's fuzzy-RD gifted-eligibility effects were statistically indistinguishable from zero (math −0.037 SD, SE 0.074; reading +0.049 SD, SE 0.068; ~4,000 obs) and their magnet-lottery science LATE ≈ +0.28 SD had attrition bounds including zero; Card–Giuliano found **+0.27–0.34 SD** for high-achiever-*rank* compliers (Black/Hispanic +0.4–0.5 SD) **yet an essentially NULL IQ-threshold RD** — the formal IQ/gifted cut did NOT mark who benefits while an achievement-rank cut did. Selecting on measured ability ≠ selecting on benefit. Cross-ref A9 §15. — Bui, S. A., Craig, S. G., & Imberman, S. A. (2014), *AEJ: Economic Policy* 6(3):30–62, DOI 10.1257/pol.6.3.30; Card, D., & Giuliano, L. (2016), *American Economic Review* 106(10):2783–2816, DOI 10.1257/aer.20150484 [VERIFIED]. **Established (precedent); Contested (generalizability to a specific GT program).**

### Wave 6 · A12 — Item-bank & adaptive-test operational measurement quality at the tail (2026-07-22)

*Scope:* whether an adaptive test can actually **deliver** concentrated θ_cut information — fairly, securely, reproducibly — at the top 1–2%, cataloging the operational levers (pool audit/blueprint, estimator choice, exposure/security, stopping rules, MST, real-pool certification) that can either realize or silently erase the nominal "2×" tail-information advantage from A1/A7/A10. Cross-refs: A1 (TIF/CSEM/Fisher–KL info, first flag of bank-info audit + estimator + parameter uncertainty), A2 (classification accuracy/consistency), A3 (reliability/G-theory), A5 (fairness/DIF), A6 (simulation/relative efficiency), A7 (θ_cut CSEM standard-setting), A10 (relative efficiency & "2×").

**Framing note carried through every finding:** nominal test/bank information `I(θ_cut)` sets a *ceiling*, not the delivered precision. An examinee sees only `L` items; exposure ceilings forbid always using the most informative tail items; Bayesian priors shrink high θ inward; and calibration error inflates *apparent* information. So a "2× information at θ_cut" claim requires (i) a bank that physically holds enough high-`a`, high-`b` items near θ_cut, (ii) an estimator that does not trade the gain away, (iii) exposure/security controls the tail bank can survive, and (iv) item parameters known well enough that the information is real, not capitalized. Do **not** assert CogAT's actual pool size/item counts — [UNVERIFIED] and out of scope here.

1. **Bank (pool) information function & tail-gap audit.** (a) The aggregate of item information over the whole pool, evaluated across θ. (b) `I_pool(θ) = Σ_{i∈pool} I_i(θ)`, with 2PL `I_i(θ)=a_i² P_i(θ)Q_i(θ)` and 3PL `I_i(θ)=a_i²·(Q_i/P_i)·[(P_i−c_i)/(1−c_i)]²`; plot `I_pool(θ)` and the count of items with `I_i(θ)≥τ` on a fine θ grid; "tail gap" = region near θ_cut where either `I_pool` or the *count of usable high-info items* falls below the target set by the desired CSEM. (c) *Quantifies the physical ceiling and the redundancy/depth of the pool at θ_cut.* (d) Compute `I_pool(θ_cut)`; the pool cannot deliver test information above this even before exposure limits; audit both the height of the curve and the *number* of near-optimal items (depth). (e) Bank info overstates deliverable precision (ignores that only `L` items are administered and that exposure control removes the best items from most test-takers); inflated if `a_i` are calibration-overestimates (#15/#16). — Reckase (2010), *Psychological Test and Assessment Modeling* 52(2):127–141 [VERIFIED; open-access PTAM, no CrossRef DOI]; Belov & Armstrong (2005), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 29(4):239–261, doi:10.1177/0146621605275413 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

2. **Target information & required bank depth near θ_cut ("how many high-a, high-b items?").** (a) Translating a target tail CSEM into a count of tail items and pool depth. (b) `I_target(θ_cut)=1/CSEM_target²`; items per form near the cut `k ≈ I_target / ī(θ_cut)` (`ī` = mean info of near-optimal items at θ_cut); to serve `N` examinees under max-exposure `r_max`, required pool depth `d(θ_cut) ≳ k / r_max`. (c) *Quantifies the tail-bank construction burden implied by any precision target.* (d) [INFERENCE] top-2% cut (θ_cut≈+2.05), 3PL tail items `ī≈0.40`: CSEM 0.30 → `I_target=11.1` → `k≈28` → with `r_max=0.20`, `d≈140` distinct high-quality items in the θ_cut band; CSEM 0.25 → `k≈40` → `d≈200`. Illustrative, not GT-specific. (e) Assumes every tail item peaks near θ_cut; guessing/content constraints lower realized `ī`, inflating required depth; if the real bank can't reach `d`, "2×" is not deliverable regardless of nominal info. — Reckase (2010) [VERIFIED]; worked numbers [INFERENCE] on A1 (`CSEM=1/√I`). **Established (method); worked numbers Inference.**

3. **Reckase *p*-optimality bin blueprint (item-pool design).** (a) A Monte-Carlo/heuristic method for the *statistical* blueprint of an optimal pool. (b) Simulate a target ability distribution, run the CAT, record item properties demanded at each θ; sort into difficulty bins; a pool is `p`-optimal (e.g., **0.95**) if for every θ an available item supplies ≥ `p×` the max possible information; under Rasch, 0.95-optimality ⇒ item within ≈ **0.35 logit** of θ; bin counts inflated for exposure/parallel forms. (c) *Quantifies the ideal number and (a,b,c) profile of items per θ bin, including the θ_cut tail bin.* (d) Report the target-information function, bin width, per-bin counts; θ_cut-bin count × exposure multiplier = the tail-writing target. (e) Assumes items can be written to spec at the tail (hardest exactly where most needed); 0.35-logit rule loosens under 2PL/3PL. — Reckase (2010) [VERIFIED]; Gu (2007) dissertation, Michigan State [SECONDARY]; MST extension Yang et al. (2020), *EPM* 80(4), doi:10.1177/0013164419901292 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

4. **Pool analysis & extension by optimal/stochastic assembly (which items to add at the tail).** (a) Diagnosing a *real* pool's weaknesses and prescribing new items. (b) **Belov–Armstrong Monte-Carlo test assembly**: uniform random sampling of feasible forms exposes how many parallel forms a pool supports, the most restrictive constraint(s), and the attributes to add; complementary **LP/MIP optimal assembly** (van der Linden; Veldkamp) maximizes `Σ I_i(θ_cut) x_i` s.t. content/enemy/exposure, `x_i∈{0,1}`. (c) *Quantifies whether a real bank can produce enough distinct, constraint-satisfying tail forms and pinpoints the missing tail items.* (d) If the binding constraint is "too few high-`a` items at θ_cut," the diagnosis names the writing target; certify parallel-form supply before promising precision. (e) Assumes accurate parameters; Monte-Carlo search is compute-heavy; says nothing about *delivered* precision until simulated with estimator + exposure (#16). — Belov & Armstrong (2005), doi:10.1177/0146621605275413 [VERIFIED]; Veldkamp, "Item Pool Design and Maintenance for MST," in Yan, von Davier & Lewis (2014), *Computerized Multistage Testing*, CRC Press, doi:10.1201/b16858 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

5. **MLE as a tail lever: outward bias & non-finiteness → over-selection / false positives at θ_cut.** (a) The score-equation estimator and its tail pathologies. (b) `θ̂_MLE` solves `S(θ)=∂lnL/∂θ = Σ_i [P_i'(θ)/(P_i Q_i)]·(u_i−P_i)=0`; Lord's first-order bias is **outward** (high scorers pushed higher) and `θ̂_MLE` is **non-finite (+∞)** for all-correct, **−∞** for all-incorrect. (c) *Quantifies a systematic upward tilt at the top* — near θ_cut this manufactures false positives and gives no finite estimate for the perfect-response patterns gifted examinees most often produce on a short adaptive test. (d) Never use raw MLE for a top-1–2% classification without a finite-scoring fallback; the outward bias means uncorrected MLE over-selects at the cut, and that over-selection is subgroup-dependent (cross-ref A5). (e) Undefined at boundaries; unstable with few items; bias grows where the test is least informative — often just above θ_cut. — Warm (1989), *Psychometrika* 54(3):427–450, doi:10.1007/BF02294627 [VERIFIED]; boundary behavior via Lord (1980) [SECONDARY]. **Established.**

6. **Warm's Weighted Likelihood Estimator (WLE): first-order bias removal & finite tail estimates.** (a) A weighted-score estimator canceling the leading MLE bias term. (b) `θ̂_WLE` solves `S(θ) + J(θ)/[2 I(θ)] = 0`, `J(θ)=Σ_i [P_i'(θ) P_i''(θ)]/[P_i(θ) Q_i(θ)]`; same asymptotic variance/normality as MLE but removes O(1/n) bias and yields **finite** estimates at perfect/zero patterns. (c) *Quantifies a bias-corrected tail location* — reduces MLE over-selection at θ_cut without the inward pull of a Bayesian prior. (d) Preferred default individual-scoring estimator for a tail CAT; report conditional bias/RMSE at θ_cut. (e) Weighted equation can have multiple roots; bias reduction is first-order only (residual tail bias with very short tests); assumes known item parameters. — Warm (1989), doi:10.1007/BF02294627 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

7. **EAP/MAP prior shrinkage: inward bias → false negatives at θ_cut; and plausible values.** (a) Bayesian point estimators and their tail behavior. (b) `θ̂_EAP = [Σ_q X_q L(X_q) g(X_q)]/[Σ_q L(X_q) g(X_q)]`, posterior SD `PSD` as SE; `θ̂_MAP` = posterior mode. With a `N(0,σ²)` prior the posterior mean **regresses toward the prior mean with slope ≈ reliability (<1)**, so high θ is **underestimated** (inward shrinkage). **Plausible values** (Mislevy) draw `m` values per examinee for *population/subgroup* inference, not individual decisions. (c) *Quantifies a systematic downward pull at the tail (opposite sign to MLE)* — near θ_cut EAP/MAP manufacture false negatives, heavier for subgroups whose prior/collateral mean is lower (a fairness choice, cross-ref A5). (d) Justify the prior and report its tail bias; a diffuse prior reduces shrinkage but reintroduces MLE-like instability; use plausible values for subgroup tail *rates*, never to classify an individual. (e) Bock–Mislevy showed near-linear EAP regression only within ±2.5 SD — the tail sits at that edge; MAP shares the shrinkage. — Bock & Mislevy (1982), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 6(4):431–444, doi:10.1177/014662168200600405 [VERIFIED]; Mislevy (1991), *Psychometrika* 56(2):177–196, doi:10.1007/BF02294457 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

8. **Exposure-control family: Sympson–Hetter, randesque, Stocking–Lewis *conditional* (why the tail bank erodes fastest).** (a) Probabilistic controls limiting how often high-info items are administered. (b) **Sympson–Hetter:** control parameter `K_i∈[0,1]`; administer-if-selected with prob `K_i` so marginal `P(admin_i)=P(selected_i)·K_i ≤ r_max`. **Randesque:** randomly administer one of the top-`k` most-informative. **Stocking–Lewis conditional:** control `P(admin_i | θ) ≤ r_max` *at each θ*. (c) *Quantifies the enforced trade of information for security* — every unit of exposure protection removes some of the very tail items that create the θ_cut advantage. (d) Use `r_max` (e.g., 0.20) and a **conditional** control near θ_cut: marginal SH can pass while an item is still shown to ~100% of high-θ examinees — so conditional control is mandatory at a gifted cut. (e) SH parameters are population-specific and drift; randesque controls overlap not the overall rate; conditional control needs adequate tail sample. — Sympson & Hetter (1985), *Proc. 27th Military Testing Association*, 973–977 [SECONDARY, no DOI]; Kingsbury & Zara (1989), *Applied Measurement in Education* 2(4):359–375, doi:10.1207/s15324818ame0204_6 [VERIFIED]; Stocking & Lewis (1998), *JEBS* 23(1):57–75, doi:10.3102/10769986023001057 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

9. **a-stratification (Chang–Ying): exposure balance *and* robustness to calibration error at the tail.** (a) Stratify the bank by discrimination `a`; spend low-`a` items early, high-`a` late. (b) Partition into `K` strata by `a`; at stage `k` select only from stratum `k` (low→high), optionally b-blocking so each stratum spans difficulty. (c) *Quantifies a way to lower average exposure/overlap while preserving tail efficiency and keeping observed ≈ true information.* (d) Lower max-exposure than MFI+SH in Chang–Ying simulations; keeps *observed* info close to *true* under calibration error (whereas MFI capitalizes on spuriously large `â` — link to #16), an equity-relevant property at the sparse tail (cross-ref A5). (e) Because it withholds the highest-`a` items early, pure a-stratification can *reduce* peak information at θ_cut for a short classification test — the opposite of "concentrate at θ_cut"; hybrids (STR-SH) or MFI-with-conditional-exposure may be preferable for a top-1–2% decision. — Chang & Ying (1999), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 23(3):211–222, doi:10.1177/01466219922031338 [VERIFIED]; b-blocking (2001), *APM* 25(3), doi:10.1177/01466210122032181 [VERIFIED]. **Established (exposure benefit); Contested (help/hurt a narrow θ_cut decision).**

10. **Exposure, overlap & utilization metrics (formulas + security thresholds).** (a) Audit statistics for security/efficiency of a live pool. (b) **exposure rate** `e_i = n_i/N`, constraint `e_i ≤ r_max`; identity `Σ_i e_i = L` ⇒ mean exposure `= L/m` (`m`=pool size); **utilization** = fraction with `e_i>0`; **test-overlap** (Chang–Zhang, random selection) expected common items `= L²/m`, overlap rate `= L/m`; general `T̄ = (m·σ²_e)/L + L/m`. (c) *Quantifies how concentrated usage is on scarce tail items and the compromise risk.* (d) High-stakes targets often set `r_max≈0.20` and overlap below a program threshold; the `L/m` floor shows a thin tail bank forces high overlap no matter the algorithm — a hard security ceiling on tail precision. (e) `L/m` benchmark assumes *uniform* selection (not tail-concentrated); real overlap at the cut is far above the floor; metrics are population/time-window-specific. — Chang & Zhang (2002), *Psychometrika* 67(3):387–398, doi:10.1007/BF02294991 [VERIFIED]; variance decomposition [INFERENCE]. **Established (benchmark); decomposition Inference.**

11. **Stopping-rule taxonomy: fixed-length vs fixed-precision vs classification.** (a) Three termination philosophies with different tail implications. (b) **Fixed-length:** stop at `n=L`. **Fixed-precision:** stop when `SE(θ̂) ≤ τ` i.e. `I_accumulated(θ̂) ≥ 1/τ²`. **Classification:** stop when the θ-vs-θ_cut decision is resolved (#12–13). (c) *Quantifies what "done" means and how much tail bank each consumes* — fixed-precision at the tail can be unreachable (SE floor from scarce info). (d) For a top-1–2% *decision*, a classification rule is the correct target (spends items only until the cut decision is confident, minimizing tail-item consumption); a fixed-precision rule referenced to θ_cut is the fallback when a defensible score is also required. (e) Fixed-precision can't beat the pool's `1/√I_pool(θ)` floor; fixed-length wastes items on easy classifications and starves hard ones; variable-length complicates exposure accounting. — Bock & Mislevy (1982), doi:10.1177/014662168200600405 [VERIFIED]; classification per #12–13. **Established.**

12. **Classification CAT I — SPRT (Wald) with cut-focused / KLI item selection.** (a) A sequential hypothesis test for "above vs below θ_cut." (b) Indifference region `θ_1=θ_cut−δ`, `θ_2=θ_cut+δ`; `LR_n = Π_j [P_j(θ_2)^{u_j}Q_j(θ_2)^{1−u_j}]/[P_j(θ_1)^{u_j}Q_j(θ_1)^{1−u_j}]`; continue if `B<LR_n<A`, classify "gifted" if `LR_n≥A=(1−β)/α`, "not" if `≤B=β/(1−α)`. Select items maximizing **Fisher info at θ_cut** or **KL info** `KL_i(θ_2‖θ_1)`. (c) *Quantifies the minimal evidence to make the top-1–2% decision at a controlled error rate* — the most item-efficient way to spend the scarce tail bank. (d) SPRT needed fewer items than sequential-Bayes at matched errors (Spray–Reckase); KLI ≥ Fisher-info selection for classification (Eggen); choose δ from the θ_cut CSEM (A7). (e) Wald bounds are approximations; very small δ inflates length; needs items informative at the cut (re-links to #2) — a security-vs-decision tension (#10). — Wald (1947), *Sequential Analysis* [SECONDARY]; Spray & Reckase (1996), *JEBS* 21(4):405–414, doi:10.3102/10769986021004405 [VERIFIED]; Eggen (1999), *APM* 23(3):249–261, doi:10.1177/01466219922031365 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

13. **Classification CAT II — ability-confidence-interval (Kingsbury–Weiss) & Generalized Likelihood Ratio (Thompson); multi-category.** (a) Two alternatives to fixed-point SPRT. (b) **Ability-CI / adaptive mastery:** classify when `θ̂ ± z_{1−α/2}·SE(θ̂)` lies entirely above/below θ_cut; else continue. **GLR:** for `H0: θ≤θ_cut` vs `H1: θ≥θ_cut`, `GLR = L(θ̂)/L(θ_cut)`, terminate at a bound. (c) *Quantifies decision confidence using the estimate itself (CI/GLR) rather than a pre-set indifference region* — often more efficient/transparent. (d) SPRT is generally more item-efficient at matched errors, but Kingsbury–Weiss found the CI approach gave *more valid and balanced* error rates; GLR (Thompson) avoids arbitrary δ; Eggen–Straetmans extend to 3 categories (not-gifted/borderline/gifted) with ≥22% length savings. (e) CI method inherits estimator bias (MLE outward vs EAP inward, #5/#7 flip the errors); GLR thresholds need calibration; multi-category cuts multiply tail-bank demand. — Kingsbury & Weiss (1983), in Weiss (Ed.), *New Horizons in Testing*, 257–283 [SECONDARY]; Thompson (2009), *EPM* 69(5):778–793, doi:10.1177/0013164408324460 [VERIFIED]; Eggen & Straetmans (2000), *EPM* 60(5):713–734, doi:10.1177/00131640021970862 [VERIFIED]. **Established (efficiency ordering); Contested (validity-vs-efficiency at a gifted cut).**

14. **Shadow-test approach & constrained CAT (content balancing without losing tail optimality).** (a) Re-assemble a full-length optimal test at each step so every administered item is both maximally informative and constraint-feasible. (b) Before item `k`, solve the MIP: maximize `Σ_i I_i(θ̂_{k−1}) x_i` s.t. all content/enemy/length constraints with administered items fixed at `x_i=1`; administer the most-informative *free* item; embed exposure via ineligibility constraints (α-shadow). (c) *Quantifies how to hold content/fairness constraints while concentrating info at θ_cut.* (d) 400+ constraints cost ~no efficiency in the LSAT study; solver runtimes real-time-feasible. (e) Assumes accurate parameters; feasibility can fail on a thin tail pool (#4); optimality conditional on the current (possibly biased) `θ̂`. — van der Linden & Reese (1998), *APM* 22(3):259–270, doi:10.1177/01466216980223006 [VERIFIED]; van der Linden & Veldkamp (2004), *JEBS* 29(3):273–291, doi:10.3102/10769986029003273 [VERIFIED]; Kingsbury & Zara (1989), doi:10.1207/s15324818ame0204_6 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

15. **Multistage testing (MST): panels/modules/routing as a tail-friendly, controllable alternative.** (a) Pre-assembled *modules* grouped into *panels*; examinees routed by interim `θ̂`. (b) Build modules with targeted info (a hard third-stage module peaking near θ_cut); after each stage route (approximate-max-information or number-correct/DPI); assemble by ATA. (c) *Quantifies a design where the tail-info concentration is pre-built and reviewable* — you can QA the exact hard module that decides the gifted cut and control its exposure at module level. (d) Retains most CAT efficiency with developer control over content/exposure/review; a "1-2-2"/"1-3" panel with a deliberately hard top module is a natural top-1–2% instrument. (e) Coarser adaptation ⇒ slightly less peak info at θ_cut than item-level CAT; stage-1 routing errors can misroute a gifted student away from the hard module (a tail false-negative with subgroup implications, cross-ref A5). — Luecht & Nungester (1998), *Journal of Educational Measurement* 35(3):229–249, doi:10.1111/j.1745-3984.1998.tb00537.x [VERIFIED]; Yan, von Davier & Lewis (2014), doi:10.1201/b16858 [VERIFIED]. **Established.**

16. **Item-parameter uncertainty at the tail: capitalization on calibration error, drift, calibration-N needs, real-pool certification.** (a) Why *nominal* info overstates *delivered* precision, and how to certify a real pool at θ_cut. (b) **Capitalization on chance (van der Linden–Glas):** max-information selection preferentially picks items whose `â` is *overestimated*, so realized estimates are worse than the (spuriously low) reported SE — worst with small calibration samples and high pool/test-length ratios; **drift (Bock–Muraki–Pfeiffenberger):** item location parameters drift over years (content-linked `b`-drift over 10 yrs; no significant `a`-drift), needing detection/re-linking; **certification:** run Monte-Carlo CAT simulation on the *actual* calibrated pool (with the chosen estimator, exposure control, stopping rule) and report *conditional* `SE(θ_cut)`, bias, classification accuracy/consistency (A2), exposure/overlap (#10) — using parameter *estimates* + a calibration-error perturbation. (c) *Quantifies the gap between "2× information" on paper and the precision a real, imperfectly-calibrated, exposure-controlled pool delivers at θ_cut.* (d) The simulated conditional SE at θ_cut must meet the A7 target *after* exposure control and *with* calibration error injected; if `a`-estimates rest on a thin tail calibration sample, deflate the claimed information (or use a-stratification / non-MFI selection, #9) and enlarge the tail calibration `N`; treat any residual "2×" as an *estimate-conditional* claim. (e) The deflation size is program-specific; drift monitoring needs ongoing data; you cannot certify a *nonexistent* tail bank — the bottleneck returns to #1–#4. — van der Linden & Glas (2000), *Applied Measurement in Education* 13(1):35–53, doi:10.1207/s15324818ame1301_2 [VERIFIED]; Bock, Muraki & Pfeiffenberger (1988), *JEM* 25(4):275–285, doi:10.1111/j.1745-3984.1988.tb00308.x [VERIFIED]; certification-by-simulation per Bock & Mislevy (1982) & Belov & Armstrong (2005) [VERIFIED]. **Established (capitalization & drift); exact deflation program-specific.**
