### Category 1: Remote proctoring modalities and whether the evidence says they work

*General idea: "Proctoring" is not one intervention but a family of them — a live human watching through a webcam, a recording reviewed after the fact, an algorithm flagging behaviour for later human adjudication, a browser that locks the machine down, and the null condition of nobody watching at all — and these differ enormously in what they are physically capable of observing. This category assembles what has actually been measured about that family: how much scores move when supervision is added or removed, how accurate automated flagging is when anyone independent tests it, whether the deterrent effect of *being watched* is separable from the detection effect of *being caught*, and what being surveilled while tested does to performance. The evidence is unusually contentious. Vendor-published efficacy narratives and independent peer-reviewed evaluations reach almost opposite conclusions about the same products, the one meta-analytic estimate of the proctoring effect is far smaller than practitioner intuition suggests, and the literature is close to silent on whether any of it transfers to young children tested at home.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: The modality taxonomy and the measured size of the proctoring effect on scores**
  - **Source:** Harerimana, Mchunu & Mtshali (2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The reviewers separate proctoring modalities by "their dependence on human versus machine oversight and in their monitoring formats, which can be synchronous or asynchronous," yielding live online proctoring, automated/algorithmic proctoring, record-and-review, biometric identity verification, lockdown browsers, and hybrid automated-plus-human designs. *(Harerimana, Mchunu & Mtshali, 2026, *International Journal for Educational Integrity* 22, article 19, DOI 10.1007/s40979-026-00229-7.)* `T2`
      - A systematic search of six databases for 2015–2025 returned 795 records but only six studies met inclusion criteria for remote proctoring in nursing-student assessment, and all six were published between 2021 and 2024. *(Harerimana, Mchunu & Mtshali, 2026, DOI 10.1007/s40979-026-00229-7.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The taxonomy is stable across reviews and turns on two axes: who or what does the observing, and whether observation is live or after the fact. The thinner finding is the evidence base — a six-database decade-wide search surfaced six eligible studies in one professional field, none pre-2021, which is the size of literature that supports confident vendor claims very poorly.
    - **Link to source:** [Harerimana, Mchunu & Mtshali (2026), scoping review of remote proctoring modalities](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40979-026-00229-7)
  - **Source:** Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A three-level random-effects meta-analysis of 109 effect sizes from 49 studies (total N = 100,434) found unproctored ability-test scores higher than proctored ones by a pooled Δ = 0.20, 95% CI [0.10, 0.31]. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, *European Journal of Psychological Assessment* 36(1):174–184, DOI 10.1027/1015-5759/a000494.)* `T2`
      - Moderator analysis found significantly smaller unproctored-score inflation for measures whose content is "difficult to research on the Internet." *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, DOI 10.1027/1015-5759/a000494.)* `T2`
      - The moderator test for implemented countermeasures against cheating — honesty contracts, follow-up verification testing, and similar measures written into testing guidelines — returned no significant effect. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, DOI 10.1027/1015-5759/a000494; author summary at ulrich-schroeders.de.)* `T2`
      - A small subsample of 5 studies contributing 15 effect sizes (total N = 1,280) indicated considerable rank-order change between proctored and unproctored administration, ρ = .58, 95% CI [.38, .78]. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, preprint version, DOI 10.31234/osf.io/wxj4y — **this rank-order figure comes from the preprint abstract and rests on only 5 studies; treat as weakly estimated.**)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The single best-powered estimate of the proctoring effect on ability-test scores is one fifth of a standard deviation, not the dramatic gap the framing "unproctored tests are worthless" implies. Two moderators matter more than the headline: inflation shrinks when the content cannot be looked up, and the standard countermeasures the industry recommends showed no measurable effect at all. The rank-order result, though thinly estimated, is the more alarming one — a modest mean shift can still reshuffle who ends up at the top.
    - **Link to source:** [Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs (2020), meta-analysis of proctored vs. unproctored ability scores](https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759/a000494)
  - **Source:** Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across two field experiments, students whose exams were not proctored scored "over 11% higher on average" than those whose exams were proctored. *(Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri, 2021, *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics* 90:101653, DOI 10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653.)* `T2`
      - The score gap was "much smaller when a web-based proctoring tool was used instead of an in-class live proctor," making the modality — not the presence of proctoring per se — the dominant variable. *(Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri, 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653.)* `T2`
      - The authors present evidence that peer collaboration, not test anxiety, is the primary mechanism behind the proctored/unproctored difference, while stating their data do not rule out anxiety. *(Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri, 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the cleanest published demonstration that modalities are not interchangeable: a live in-person proctor moved scores far more than a web-based proctoring tool in the same research programme. Their reading of the mechanism is collaboration between test-takers rather than surveillance-induced anxiety, which locates the leak in *other people being present or reachable* rather than in the internet. **A later working-paper treatment by an overlapping author team frames anxiety as explaining part of a 7.8% proctor-induced score reduction; that version is a RedNIE working paper and is not peer-reviewed.**
    - **Link to source:** [Vazquez, Chiang & Sarmiento-Barbieri (2021), field experiment on proctoring modality](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socec.2020.101653) · [Non-peer-reviewed working-paper version emphasising anxiety (RedNIE WP 320, 2024)](https://ideas.repec.org/p/aoz/wpaper/320.html)
  - **Source:** Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a webcam-proctored versus unproctored experiment, proctored participants scored lower only on the test whose answers were searchable online and not on the logical-reasoning test whose answers were not, and the authors characterise the decrease as very small. *(Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend, 2014, *Journal of Business and Psychology* 29(4):555–572, DOI 10.1007/s10869-014-9343-z.)* `T2`
      - Proctored participants reported higher perceived pressure and greater privacy concerns, and more of them withdrew from testing than in the unproctored condition. *(Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend, 2014, DOI 10.1007/s10869-014-9343-z.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The searchable/non-searchable contrast within a single study is the sharpest available evidence that the proctoring effect is content-dependent rather than a general property of supervision — it converges with the meta-analytic moderator from an entirely different design. The neglected result is attrition: proctoring did not only change scores, it changed *who finished*, which is a selection problem rather than a measurement one.
    - **Link to source:** [Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend (2014), cheating, reactions, and performance under remote proctoring](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-014-9343-z)

- **Subcategory 1.2: Detection performance — what automated flagging actually catches, and who adjudicates it**
  - **Source:** Bergmans, Bouali, Luttikhuis & Rensink (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a controlled trial of 30 computer-science students where 6 were instructed to cheat, the automated analysis flagged none of the 6 cheaters and an independent human reviewer caught 1 of 6. *(Bergmans, Bouali, Luttikhuis & Rensink, 2021, *Proceedings of CSEDU 2021*, pp. 279–290, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
      - False positives were 0% for the automated detection and 4% for the human review, which the authors describe as "relatively high specificity … which, however, is quite useless in the light of the disastrous sensitivity." *(Bergmans et al., 2021, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
      - The authors conclude the system's sensitivity "should therefore be put at very close to zero" and that using online proctoring "is best compared to taking a placebo: it has some positive influence, not because it works but because people believe that it works." *(Bergmans et al., 2021, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
      - Despite the near-zero measured sensitivity, a clear majority of the same students believed that use of the system would prevent cheating. *(Bergmans et al., 2021, DOI 10.5220/0010399602790290.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the only published controlled sensitivity test of a named commercial automated proctoring product, and it found sensitivity near zero against technically capable cheaters while specificity was perfect. **Its limits are real and should be stated: n = 30, six cheaters, a single product, and a computer-science cohort whose technical ability the authors describe as far above average.** The finding that matters beyond the sample size is the internal contradiction — the same students who defeated the system believed it would stop cheating, which is the mechanism a deterrence argument depends on and an efficacy argument cannot use.
    - **Link to source:** [Bergmans, Bouali, Luttikhuis & Rensink (2021), controlled trial of automated proctoring efficacy](https://doi.org/10.5220/0010399602790290) · [Open author copy](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2021/103996/103996.pdf)
  - **Source:** Burgess, Ginsberg, Felten & Cohney (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Reverse-engineering four proctoring suites used in 93% of U.S. law schools and 100% of remote state bar exams, the authors report that "despite promises of high-security, all their anti-cheating measures can be trivially bypassed and can pose significant user security risks." *(Burgess, Ginsberg, Felten & Cohney, 2022, *31st USENIX Security Symposium*.)* `T2`
      - The facial-recognition classifier used by the market-leading suite, the publicly available pre-trained `face-api.js` model, showed an average false non-match rate of 11.47% on datasets the authors selected to resemble live exam capture conditions. *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - False non-match rates varied by the subject's labelled race, with subjects labelled 'Black' showing higher rates than those labelled 'White' on the two largest datasets for all classifiers except one. *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - The authors state that vendors can pick classifiers with the lowest false non-match rate but that those classifiers "all present much higher FMRs, suggesting they provide little more than security theater." *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - They identify a structural limit rather than an implementation bug: because the software runs on hardware the test-taker administers, "the student has full administrative access and will always be able to bypass security features and monitoring schemes given enough time." *(Burgess et al., 2022, USENIX Security '22.)* `T2`
      - **Note on a citation error in this paper:** it attributes the Δ = 0.20 proctored/unproctored meta-analytic result to "Teclehaimanot et al."; that finding is Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs (2020), and the misattribution should not be propagated. `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The security-research verdict is that remote proctoring on test-taker-owned hardware is not a hard control and cannot be made into one by adding features, because the adversary owns the machine. Separately, the identity-verification layer carries an 11.47% average false non-match rate that varies by race — a gate that wrongly blocks or flags roughly one in nine attempts is a fairness problem before it is an integrity one. Their phrase for the trade-off between false-match and false-non-match tuning is "security theater."
    - **Link to source:** [Burgess, Ginsberg, Felten & Cohney (2022), bias and vulnerability in remote proctoring software](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-burgess.pdf)
  - **Source:** Simko, Hutchinson, Isaac, Fries, Sherr & Aviv (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Qualitative analysis of 137 YouTube and TikTok videos and 4,297 comments documented a community sharing both non-technical evasion methods such as sticky notes and "deeply technical (e.g., custom virtual machines) methods of evading proctoring." *(Simko, Hutchinson, Isaac, Fries, Sherr & Aviv, 2024, *CCS '24*, pp. 2681–2695, DOI 10.1145/3658644.3691638.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Evasion is not a theoretical capability held by security researchers; it is documented, socially distributed instructional content at consumer-platform scale. This changes the threat model from "a determined individual might defeat this" to "the defeat is a tutorial away," which is the assumption a deterrence argument based on perceived detection risk has to survive.
    - **Link to source:** [Simko et al. (2024), community-developed proctoring evasion techniques](https://doi.org/10.1145/3658644.3691638)
  - **Source:** Heinrich (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Because rule violations are not easily identifiable — the review's example is whether a student "look[ed] up in contemplation or did they look at someone providing information" — "the raising of flags needs to be overcautious, necessitating a large proportion of false positives." *(Heinrich, 2025, *Open Praxis* 17(3), DOI 10.55982/openpraxis.17.3.836.)* `T2`
      - Large-scale study of online proctoring accuracy is described as "something currently not possible" because flag data are not reported in any standardised, aggregatable form. *(Heinrich, 2025, DOI 10.55982/openpraxis.17.3.836, citing Dawson 2023.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** High false-positive rates in automated proctoring are not an accident of immature models but a design consequence: a flag is a referral for human judgement, so the system is deliberately tuned to over-refer. The more consequential claim is epistemic — nobody outside the vendors can currently audit flag accuracy at scale, which explains why the independent evidence base consists of small controlled trials rather than population statistics.
    - **Link to source:** [Heinrich (2025), systematic-narrative review of online proctoring systems](https://doi.org/10.55982/openpraxis.17.3.836)
  - **Source:** Dawson & Sutherland-Smith (2017, 2019)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Seven experienced markers blind-marking 20 assignments of which 6 were purchased detected the purchased work 62% of the time and correctly identified genuine student work 96% of the time. *(Dawson & Sutherland-Smith, 2017, *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 43(2):286–293, DOI 10.1080/02602938.2017.1336746.)* `T2`
      - In a pre-post training study across four disciplines, marker sensitivity rose from 58% to 82% (statistically significant) while specificity rose from 83% to 87% (not significant). *(Dawson & Sutherland-Smith, 2019, *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 44(5):715–725, DOI 10.1080/02602938.2018.1531109.)* `T2`
      - The authors describe the 2017 result as a pilot whose findings "should be taken with caution as they are from one course unit in one discipline." *(Dawson & Sutherland-Smith, 2017, DOI 10.1080/02602938.2017.1336746.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The only well-quantified human detection sensitivities in the integrity literature come from a different cheating channel — outsourced written work, not live exam surveillance — and they are 58–82%, well above the near-zero automated sensitivity measured against live technical cheating. Trained human judgement moved sensitivity by 24 points without costing specificity, which is the opposite pattern from tuning an automated flagger. **These figures are not transferable to webcam proctoring and should not be quoted as such.**
    - **Link to source:** [Dawson & Sutherland-Smith (2017), can markers detect contract cheating](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2017.1336746) · [Dawson & Sutherland-Smith (2019), training and detection accuracy](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2018.1531109)
  - **Source:** Vendor and industry claims — Proctorio; Proctor360; Eklavvya; CSUN institutional evaluation
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Vendor-sourced, not peer-reviewed; underlying study unverified.** Proctorio's corporate blog states that once proctoring was introduced there was "no difference between scores from face-to-face exams and online exams," attributing this to work by an accounting professor named Jenelle Conaway. *(Proctorio, "Setting the Record Straight: On the Necessity of (Online) Proctoring," proctorio.com — **no independent peer-reviewed publication of this result was located.**)*
      - **Vendor-sourced, not peer-reviewed.** A proctoring vendor's blog asserts that "some pure AI detection tools can generate false positives at rates as high as 30% to 50%" while advanced hybrid systems "consistently achieve false positive rates below 5%." *(Proctor360, "AI Proctoring False Positives: Hybrid Solution," proctor360.com.)*
      - **Vendor-sourced, not peer-reviewed, and internally inconsistent with independent findings.** A second vendor guide claims AI proctoring accuracy of 90–95% versus 75–85% for human proctors, and that "most institutions clear 60-70% of AI flags after human review." *(Eklavvya, "AI Proctoring Guide," eklavvya.com — **the 60–70% clearance figure is attributed only to unnamed "industry studies."**)*
      - **Institutional report, not peer-reviewed.** A university comparison of three proctoring services recorded that one student "successfully cheated on the exams by hiding a smartphone under the keyboard," and that students judged none of the three services sufficient to prevent cheating. *(California State University Northridge, "A comparison of three online proctoring services," csun.edu.)*
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Vendor-published performance figures cluster in the 90–98% accuracy and sub-5% false-positive range, are stated without accessible methods, datasets, or sample sizes, and in one case rest on an attributed finding that could not be located in the peer-reviewed record. They are directly contradicted by the one independent controlled sensitivity test available. The one honest number in this group comes from a non-commercial institutional evaluation: a phone under the keyboard defeated all three services.
    - **Link to source:** [Proctorio corporate blog (vendor claim)](https://proctorio.com/about/blog/setting-record-straight-proctoring) · [Proctor360 blog (vendor claim)](https://proctor360.com/blog/ai-proctoring-false-positives-hybrid-solution) · [Eklavvya guide (vendor claim)](https://www.eklavvya.com/blog/ai-proctoring-guide/) · [CSUN institutional comparison report](http://www.csun.edu/exlinfo/dl/pdf/report-proctoringservices_final.pdf)

- **Subcategory 1.3: Deterrence without detection, the surveillance cost to measurement, and the silence on young children**
  - **Source:** Corrigan-Gibbs, Gupta, Northcutt, Cutrell & Thies (2015a, 2015b)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a 409-student online course with cheating measured directly via a honeypot rather than by self-report, a pre-task warning produced a significant decrease in cheating while an honour code produced a smaller, non-significant effect. *(Corrigan-Gibbs, Gupta, Northcutt, Cutrell & Thies, 2015a, *Learning at Scale '15*, pp. 223–228, DOI 10.1145/2724660.2728663.)* `T2`
      - Across a pair of online exams spanning 632 students in India and a Mechanical Turk task with 2,378 participants, a stern warning spelling out the consequences of cheating produced an approximately twofold reduction in cheating, consistent across both experiments and both geographies. *(Corrigan-Gibbs, Gupta, Northcutt, Cutrell & Thies, 2015b, *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction*, DOI 10.1145/2810239.)* `T2`
      - The same two studies confirm that honour codes, "despite frequent use in massive open online courses, lead to only a small and insignificant reduction in online cheating behaviors." *(Corrigan-Gibbs et al., 2015b, DOI 10.1145/2810239.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Pure text — a warning shown before the task, with no monitoring behind it — roughly halved directly measured cheating across four samples on two continents. That is a larger measured effect on cheating behaviour than the independently measured detection sensitivity of automated proctoring, and it costs nothing and surveils no one. The companion negative result is that the traditional honour code, the intervention most widely deployed, did not work in any of these experiments.
    - **Link to source:** [Corrigan-Gibbs et al. (2015a), honour codes vs. warnings in an online course](https://doi.org/10.1145/2724660.2728663) · [Corrigan-Gibbs et al. (2015b), deterring cheating in online environments](https://doi.org/10.1145/2810239)
  - **Source:** Mukherjee, Distler, Lenzini & Koenig (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a between-subjects experiment with N = 997 in a realistic online test simulation, an honour-code reminder cut the odds of cheating by 50% (p = 0.006) and a warning message by 44% (p = 0.016). *(Mukherjee, Distler, Lenzini & Koenig, 2023, *Computers & Education*, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
      - A *monitoring* message — telling test-takers they were being watched — produced a 32% reduction in cheating odds that was not statistically significant (p = 0.103). *(Mukherjee et al., 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
      - Wald tests showed no significant differences between the three interventions, and none of them significantly affected user experience or test-taking self-efficacy. *(Mukherjee et al., 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
      - The decision to cheat was influenced by how strongly an intervention conveyed that cheating is wrong and by "test-takers' perception of getting caught if they cheated." *(Mukherjee et al., 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This study reverses the previous ranking: here the honour-code reminder was the strongest intervention and the announced-monitoring message was the only one that failed to reach significance. **The two findings are in direct conflict on which nudge works, and the Wald tests within this study cannot distinguish the interventions from each other, so the honest reading is that some pre-test moral or consequence prime reduces cheating and the literature does not yet identify which one.** The mechanism both agree on is perceived probability of being caught, which is a belief, not a detection capability.
    - **Link to source:** [Mukherjee, Distler, Lenzini & Koenig (2023), privacy-non-invasive anti-cheating interventions](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104925)
  - **Source:** Woldeab & Brothen (2019); Alguacil, Herranz-Zarzoso, Pernías & Sabater-Grande (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across 631 undergraduates, trait test anxiety measured on the Westside scale correlated negatively with exam performance at r = −.167 (p < .001), and this relationship was substantially stronger, r = −.443 (p = .003), among students in the online-proctored group. *(Woldeab & Brothen, 2019, *International Journal of E-Learning & Distance Education* 34(1), ISSN 2292-8588.)* `T2`
      - Within the proctored group the performance penalty was concentrated in students above the median anxiety score (high-anxiety M = 144.62, SD = 24.83; low-anxiety M = 162.25, SD = 24.63; t = 2.52, p = .015). *(Woldeab & Brothen, 2019, IJEDE 34(1).)* `T2`
      - In a randomized field experiment on a closed-book online exam, webcam-monitored students scored about one third of a standard deviation lower than unmonitored controls. *(Alguacil, Herranz-Zarzoso, Pernías & Sabater-Grande, 2024, *Journal of Computing in Higher Education* 36:835–851, DOI 10.1007/s12528-023-09378-x.)* `T2`
      - The same experiment reports that "the monitored students did not suffer higher levels of anxiety," with no significant group differences in completion time, completion rate (84% in both groups), or questions skipped, and attributes the score gap to reduced dishonesty instead. *(Alguacil et al., 2024, DOI 10.1007/s12528-023-09378-x.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Both studies observe lower scores under surveillance and attribute them to opposite causes, and the disagreement is substantive rather than semantic. Woldeab and Brothen find the anxiety–performance relationship roughly 2.7 times stronger under proctoring, concentrated in already-anxious students, which makes proctoring a source of construct-irrelevant variance that falls unevenly across test-takers. Alguacil and colleagues tested the anxiety mechanism through behavioural proxies — time used, completion, skipping — found no differences, and concluded the gap was cheating being suppressed. **Note the asymmetry in what each measured: one measured self-reported trait anxiety directly, the other inferred state anxiety from time-use behaviour, so they may not be measuring the same thing.**
    - **Link to source:** [Woldeab & Brothen (2019), online proctoring, test anxiety, and performance](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1227595.pdf) · [Alguacil, Herranz-Zarzoso, Pernías & Sabater-Grande (2024), randomized field experiment on monitoring](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12528-023-09378-x)
  - **Source:** Walker, Kirkham, Stotesbury, Dimitriou & Hood (2023); Hamner, Salorio, Kalb & Jacobson (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A systematic review of 21 pediatric tele-neuropsychology studies (ages 0–22, PROSPERO CRD42021248969) assessed feasibility, reliability, and acceptability, and reported that most studies found no significant in-person versus remote difference for most cognitive domains including IQ. *(Walker, Kirkham, Stotesbury, Dimitriou & Hood, 2023, *Journal of Pediatric Neuropsychology* 9:113–126, DOI 10.1007/s40817-023-00144-6.)* `T2`
      - That review's stated aims are feasibility, patient/family acceptability, reliability, and literature quality — **assessment security, cheat detection, and flag accuracy are not among them, and no such analysis appears in the review.** *(Walker et al., 2023, DOI 10.1007/s40817-023-00144-6.)* `T2`
      - The review notes prior concern "about the role of caregivers who are often needed to guide the patient's attention during assessments but whose presence may pose additional challenges (e.g., prompting)" — the caregiver-influence risk is named as a concern, not quantified. *(Walker et al., 2023, DOI 10.1007/s40817-023-00144-6, citing Hewitt et al. 2020.)* `T2`
      - In a study of clinically referred children and adolescents, WISC-V and KTEA-3 subtests administered by teletesting were equivalent to in-person administration, and the authors note this was achieved without the on-site "proctor" or "assistant" that earlier remote WISC-V work had required. *(Hamner, Salorio, Kalb & Jacobson, 2022, *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society* 28(8):835–844, DOI 10.1017/S1355617721001053.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** **The literature on remotely administered cognitive testing of children is real, peer-reviewed, and about something else entirely: whether scores obtained by a trained examiner over video match scores obtained in a clinic room.** In every one of these designs an adult professional is watching and controlling the session, so the integrity question — can a child at home be given unearned help, and would any monitoring modality detect it — is never posed, let alone answered. The nearest thing to an engagement with it is a passing acknowledgement that caregivers are sometimes needed to direct a child's attention and that their presence "may pose additional challenges (e.g., prompting)." A targeted search for peer-reviewed evidence on proctoring efficacy or parental assistance in at-home high-stakes testing of young children returned only vendor, tutoring-service, and school-marketing material; **no empirical study on this question was located, and that absence should be reported as an absence rather than filled by extrapolation from undergraduate samples.**
    - **Link to source:** [Walker, Kirkham, Stotesbury, Dimitriou & Hood (2023), pediatric tele-neuropsychology systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40817-023-00144-6) · [same paper, open-access full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10231293/) · [Hamner, Salorio, Kalb & Jacobson (2022), WISC-V and KTEA-3 remote equivalency](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1355617721001053) · [same paper, open-access full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9026666/)

## Candidate tensions

- Bergmans et al. measure automated detection sensitivity at essentially zero against technically capable cheaters, while vendor blogs advertise 90–98% accuracy and sub-5% false-positive rates without published methods.
- Corrigan-Gibbs et al. find honour codes have a small non-significant effect and warnings roughly halve cheating; Mukherjee et al. find the honour-code reminder strongest and the announced-monitoring message non-significant.
- Woldeab & Brothen attribute lower proctored scores substantially to amplified test anxiety; Alguacil et al. test for an anxiety mechanism, find none, and attribute the same direction of effect to suppressed cheating.
- Steger et al.'s pooled Δ = 0.20 is far smaller than the >11% raw gap reported by Vazquez et al., raising an unresolved question about how much of the "cheating effect" is a live-proctor-versus-webcam artefact rather than a proctoring effect.
- Steger et al. find no measurable effect of standard countermeasures such as honesty contracts, while a large experimental literature reports significant reductions in cheating from pre-test honour codes and warnings.
- Independent evidence reports automated flagging as necessarily over-inclusive and false-positive-heavy by design, while the one controlled trial of a commercial product found a 0% false-positive rate — the two cannot both describe typical operating points.
- Dawson & Sutherland-Smith show trained humans reaching 82% detection sensitivity, whereas the human reviewer in Bergmans et al. caught 1 of 6 — leaving open whether human review is a genuine safeguard or a rubber stamp depending on training and channel.
- Burgess et al. argue remote proctoring on test-taker-owned hardware cannot be secured in principle, while vendors and adopting institutions treat improved feature sets as closing the gap.
- Vazquez et al. find live in-person proctors move scores far more than web-based proctoring, implying the modalities are not substitutes, while most policy discussion and most reviews treat "proctored" as a single condition.
- Karim et al. report increased withdrawal from proctored testing, which no efficacy comparison in this set accounts for — if surveillance changes who completes, proctored and unproctored score distributions are not drawn from the same population.
- The pediatric remote-assessment literature reports equivalence with an examiner present and supervising, while the integrity literature reports substantial score inflation when supervision is absent — nothing published connects these two bodies of evidence for young children at home.
