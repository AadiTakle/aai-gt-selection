### Category 4: The fairness, privacy, and legal costs of proctoring — and how they change when the examinee is a minor

*General idea: Automated proctoring imposes costs that are measurable, unevenly distributed, and legally live. The detection machinery has documented differential error by skin tone, sex, and age — including elevated error on children specifically — and it flags disability-related movement, gaze, and assistive-technology use as suspicion. The environmental preconditions it requires (private quiet room, reliable bandwidth, camera-capable hardware, tolerance of a stranger's view into the home) are themselves distributed by income. Courts and data-protection regulators have now actually decided several of these questions, and they have not decided them the same way: a US federal court held a university's room scan an unreasonable Fourth Amendment search, an Italian regulator fined a university for processing exam-proctoring biometrics without a lawful basis, and a Dutch court upheld the same category of software as lawful while expressly finding no biometric data was involved. Children's data regimes add a further layer — EU law prohibits inferring emotions in education outright, and children's-privacy rules require parental consent from the very adult who, in a home administration, is also the most plausible source of improper assistance. That last point is where the literature is thinnest, and the thinness is itself a finding.*

- **Subcategory 4.1: Differential error by skin tone, sex, age, and body — the measured record**
  - **Source:** Grother, Ngan & Hanaoka (NIST, 2019); Buolamwini & Gebru (2018); Yoder-Himes et al. (2022); Srinivas et al. (2019); Satheesan analysis reported by Motherboard (2021); Proctorio company response (n.d.); Brown / CDT (2020, 2022); National Disabled Law Students Association (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across the 189 algorithms NIST evaluated, false positive rates varied across demographic groups by factors of 10 to beyond 100, while false negative differentials usually varied by factors below 3. *(Grother, Ngan & Hanaoka, 2019, NIST Interagency Report 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST found false positives 2 to 5 times higher in women than men, with the multiple varying by algorithm, country of origin, and age. *(NIST IR 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST found elevated false positives in both the elderly and in children, largest in the oldest adults and youngest children and smallest in middle-aged adults, consistently across country-of-birth, datasets, and algorithms. *(NIST IR 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST states that children are "disadvantaged in many automated border control systems either by being excluded by policy, or by encountering higher false negatives," even without significant time lapse between two photographs. *(NIST IR 8280, DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2`
      - NIST explicitly declined to relate its measured recognition errors to skin tone or any other phenotype, noting that estimating skin tone would itself require an algorithm that may exhibit demographic differentials. *(NIST IR 8280, §"What we did not do," DOI 10.6028/NIST.IR.8280.)* `T2` `[scope limit: NIST measured by sex, age, race and country of birth — not skin tone]`
      - NIST attributes elevated false negatives partly to image capture, giving "inadequate lighting or under-exposure of dark-skinned individuals" as an example of a photography condition that induces a demographic effect. *(NIST, Face Recognition Technology Evaluation demographics page, updated 2025-03-05.)* `T2`
      - Testing three commercial gender-classification systems on a Fitzpatrick-balanced dataset, Buolamwini and Gebru found darker-skinned females were the most misclassified group at error rates up to 34.7%, against a maximum error rate of 0.8% for lighter-skinned males. *(Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018, PMLR 81:77–91.)* `T2`
      - In that study all three classifiers performed better on lighter than darker faces by an 11.8%–19.2% error-rate difference, and worst on darker female faces at 20.8%–34.7% error. *(Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018, PMLR 81:77–91.)* `T2` `[scope limit: this measured gender classification, not face verification or face detection]`
      - Examining automated proctoring output for approximately 357 students at one institution, Yoder-Himes et al. found students with darker skin tones averaged 6.07 flags per assessment versus 1.91 for medium and 1.19 for lighter skin tones. *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, Frontiers in Education 7:881449, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - In the same dataset the software reported average facial detection of 78%, 87%, and 92% of assessment time for students with darker, medium, and lighter skin tones respectively (p < 0.001). *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - Students with darker skin tones were 2.5 and 5.6 times more likely to receive a "low facial detection" flag than medium- and lighter-skinned students respectively (p < 0.05 and p < 0.001). *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - Black students were flagged for a mean of 2.80 minutes per assessment versus 0.85 minutes for White students, with the overall racial-group difference significant at p = 0.0037. *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - The authors tested and rejected connection quality as the explanation: video frame-rate-reduction flags did not differ significantly by skin tone or race, and internet-interruption flags did not differ by race. *(Yoder-Himes et al., 2022, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2022.881449.)* `T2`
      - Across eight face recognition systems — five commercial, two government, one open source — Srinivas et al. found a negative bias, meaning considerably degraded performance, for children compared with adults on every algorithm tested. *(Srinivas, Ricanek, Michalski, Bolme & King, 2019, IEEE/CVF CVPR Workshops, pp. 2269–2277, DOI 10.1109/CVPRW.2019.00280.)* `T2`
      - A student researcher reverse-engineered a widely deployed proctoring tool's browser extension, found its face-detection files identical to published OpenCV models, and measured those models failing to detect faces in images labelled as Black 57% of the time across nearly 11,000 FairFace images. *(Satheesan analysis reported in Feathers, Motherboard/VICE, 8 April 2021; the outlet states a second security researcher validated and recreated the analysis.)* `[independent but not peer reviewed; outlets report the researcher's first name inconsistently]`
      - In the same testing the highest detection hit rate for any ethnicity was under 75%. *(Satheesan analysis as reported in The Verge, 8 April 2021.)* `[journalism reporting an independent analysis]`
      - The vendor publicly confirms it uses OpenCV for HAAR-based features and Canny edge detection to track the presence of a face, and responded that the analysis concerned face *detection* rather than identity recognition. *(Proctorio, "Setting the Record Straight: OpenCV," company blog.)* `[company statement]`
      - A disability-rights policy analysis argues that automated proctoring flags disability-linked behaviour as suspicious — motor tics, pacing, reading aloud, screen reader and dictation use, longer bathroom breaks — and can out a student as disabled to anyone with access to the recording. *(Brown, "How Automated Test Proctoring Software Discriminates Against Disabled Students," Center for Democracy & Technology, 16 November 2020; expanded in CDT, *Ableism and Disability Discrimination in New Surveillance Technologies*, May 2022.)* `[advocacy analysis, not empirical measurement]`
      - In a self-selected survey of 281 respondents, of whom 260 were registered for a remotely proctored 2020 bar examination and 113 identified as disabled, ambient noise was the most cited concern (114 respondents), followed by internet stability (68) and motion restrictions (62). *(National Disabled Law Students Association, *Report on Concerns Regarding Online Administration of Bar Exams*, August 2020.)* `[convenience sample drawn from the organisation's own networks; measures anticipated concerns, not observed flagging outcomes]`
      - Respondents in that survey specifically anticipated being flagged for fidgeting, stretching, stimming, gaze aversion associated with learning disabilities, and inability to hold gaze on-screen for an entire section. *(NDLSA, August 2020.)* `[reported concerns, not adjudicated findings]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Three different things have been measured and should not be merged: NIST measured face *recognition* error by sex, age, race and country of birth and found false-positive differentials of 10–100× plus elevated error at both age extremes including children; Gender Shades measured *gender classification* by Fitzpatrick skin type and found a 0.8%-to-34.7% spread; and one peer-reviewed audit measured an actual proctoring product's *face-detection* output and found darker-skinned students flagged roughly five times as often, with connection quality tested and ruled out. The vendor-specific 57% figure is an independent reverse-engineering effort corroborated by a second researcher and a journalist, not a peer-reviewed evaluation, and the vendor confirms the underlying OpenCV dependency while disputing the framing. The disability side of the record is asymmetric: the mechanism is well-articulated and the anticipated harms are documented in a large self-selected survey, but there is no equivalent to the Yoder-Himes audit measuring flag rates against disability status.

- **Subcategory 4.2: What has actually been decided — courts, regulators, and children's data law**
  - **Source:** *Ogletree v. Cleveland State University* (N.D. Reg. 16918 (2025); ICO Age Appropriate Design Code
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A US federal district court held that a state university's practice of conducting warrantless room scans of students' homes before remote tests is unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, concluding the student's privacy interest in his home outweighed the university's interest in scanning it. *(Ogletree v. Cleveland State Univ., No. 1:21-cv-00500 (N.D. Ohio, Opinion and Order, 22 August 2022), Judge J. Philip Calabrese.)* `T2`
      - The court reasoned in part that room scans are not necessary to preserve test integrity because there are numerous ways students could cheat regardless of whether a scan is performed. *(Ogletree, Amended Opinion and Order, 20 December 2022.)* `T2`
      - In an amended opinion the court issued a declaratory judgment and permanently enjoined the university from subjecting that plaintiff to a room scan "administered without offering a reasonable alternative or, alternatively, without his express consent." *(Ogletree, Amended Opinion and Order, 20 December 2022.)* `T2` `[single-plaintiff injunction from one district court; the Fourth Amendment reaches state actors only]`

      - Italy's data protection authority found that a university using proctoring software for written exams processed students' facial biometric data with no lawful basis under GDPR Article 9, and imposed a €200,000 fine plus a processing limitation. *(Garante, Provvedimento n. 317 of 16 September 2021, docweb 9703988; summarised by the EDPB, decision date 16 September 2021.)* `T2`
      - That authority held the students' consent could not be relied on both because the processing served the issuing of legally recognised diplomas and because consent could not be considered freely given given the imbalance between student and controller. *(Garante, Provvedimento n. 317/2021; EDPB summary.)* `T2`

      - A US federal court dismissed Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act claims against a proctoring vendor on the ground that a contractual choice-of-law provision required Alabama law, under which the claims were not actionable. *(Thakkar v.       - A putative BIPA class action against another proctoring vendor was dismissed with prejudice by joint stipulation in 2022, the plaintiff stating that after extensive discovery he found no evidence the vendor collects or possesses information sufficient to identify a test-taker by name, location, or contact information. *(Stipulation of dismissal, Hines v.     - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Four things are genuinely decided and they do not point the same way. - **Subcategory 4.3: What the examinee pays — environment, bandwidth, anxiety — and the unstudied case of a child proctored at home**
  - **Source:** Conijn, Kleingeld, Matzat & Snijders (2022); Woldeab & Brothen (2019); Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend (2014); Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs (2020); ITC/ATP *Guidelines for Technology-Based Assessment* (2022); Pew Research Center (2024, 2026); Viñas-Guasch et al. (2023); Kansas and Oklahoma state assessment remote-testing manuals (2025–2026)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Across four survey waves covering 1,760 students in 105 courses at one Dutch faculty, multi-level analysis found proctoring had no effect on temptation to cheat, perceived exam difficulty, or performance, but students reported significantly higher test anxiety. *(Conijn, Kleingeld, Matzat & Snijders, 2022, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 38(6):1521–1534, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12651.)* `T2`
      - In the same study, access to reliable technology and to a dedicated study space, along with gender and financial stress, were among the factors affecting test anxiety during online exams. *(Conijn et al., 2022, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12651.)* `T2`
      - In a study of 631 students, high trait test anxiety predicted lower exam scores, and the effect was stronger for students taking exams under live webcam proctoring than in a proctored campus testing centre. *(Woldeab & Brothen, 2019, *International Journal of E-Learning & Distance Education* 34(1), ERIC EJ1227595.)* `T2`
      - The authors characterise this as a person–situation interaction in which having a live proctor watching was most upsetting to high-anxiety students and interfered with their exam performance, disadvantaging some students through a common feature of online test monitoring. *(Woldeab & Brothen, 2019, ERIC EJ1227595.)* `T2`
      - In a randomised experiment with monetary incentive, webcam-proctored participants scored worse on a test whose answers were searchable online but not on a non-searchable logical reasoning test, indicating a small reduction in cheating, while reporting greater pressure and privacy concern and withdrawing at higher rates. *(Karim, Kaminsky & Behrend, 2014, *Journal of Business and Psychology* 29(4):555–572, DOI 10.1007/s10869-014-9343-z.)* `T2` `[direction and study design verified; the specific withdrawal and effect-size characterisation was read from a published review of the paywalled article, not from the original]`
      - A three-level random-effects meta-analysis of 109 effect sizes from 49 studies (total N = 100,434) found scores Δ = 0.20 higher in unproctored than proctored ability assessments, 95% CI [0.10, 0.31], with significantly smaller effects for measures hard to research on the internet. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, *European Journal of Psychological Assessment* 36(1):174–184, DOI 10.1027/1015-5759/a000494.)* `T2`
      - A smaller subsample of five studies providing 15 effect sizes (N = 1,280) in the same meta-analysis indicated considerable rank-order change between contexts, ρ = .58, 95% CI [.38, .78]. *(Steger, Schroeders & Gnambs, 2020, preprint record, DOI 10.17605/osf.io/wxj4y; the published version reports the mean-difference result.)* `T2`
      - The joint professional guidelines for technology-based assessment state that testing procedures should be monitored to ensure security is maintained, "e.g., through in-person proctoring or supervision, or by using remote monitoring through cameras if the stakes of the assessment warrant." *(Association of Test Publishers & International Test Commission, *Guidelines for Technology-Based Assessment*, 2022, Guideline 3.16.)* `[co-authored by a trade association of test publishers; not independent of the vendors whose products it governs]`
      - The same guidelines require that testing be conducted under appropriate environmental conditions with guidance on testing workspace, lack of distractions, internet capability and computer specifications, and contemplate unproctored self-administered testing only "in a low-stakes environment." *(ATP & ITC, 2022, Guideline 3.14.)* `[as above]`
      - Survey research finds 57% of US adults in households earning under $30,000 per year subscribe to home broadband, against 95% of adults in households earning at least $100,000. *(Pew Research Center, *Americans' Use of Mobile Technology and Home Broadband*, 31 January 2024.)* `[nationally representative survey research; not peer reviewed]`

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The measured cost to the examinee is anxiety rather than score: the largest study finds proctoring raises test anxiety while leaving performance, perceived difficulty, and cheating temptation unchanged, and a separate study finds the anxiety cost concentrated on exactly the students who are already most anxious. Against that sits the strongest quantitative case for proctoring — a 100,000-participant meta-analysis showing unproctored ability scores inflated by Δ = 0.20 and, in a smaller subsample, substantial rank-order instability, which for a ranked selection decision matters more than the mean shift. The access preconditions are not incidental: professional guidelines require a workspace, quiet, and specified connectivity, one state program specifies 100 Mb and two devices, and roughly half of the lowest-income US households do not subscribe to home broadband at all. On the child-at-home question the literature is one-sided rather than absent — pediatric tele-assessment names the parent's presence as an observer effect and possible active help, but treats the adult as a facilitator to be trained, while state assessment programs resolve the same problem by contract and by ejecting the adult from the room, and no located study measures whether either works.

## Candidate tensions

1. **Two regulators reached opposite conclusions on the same legal question about comparable software:** the Italian authority found exam-proctoring facial processing to be Article 9 biometric data processed without a lawful basis, while the Amsterdam court expressly found no Article 9 biometric data was involved.
2. **Consent is either the answer or is structurally unavailable:** the Amsterdam court accepted public-task processing precisely because a public university cannot rely on legitimate interest, while the Italian authority held student consent could not be freely given at all given the controller–student power imbalance.
3. **A room scan is either a Fourth Amendment search or an ordinary term of enrolment:** one US district court held it unreasonable and enjoined it, and its reasoning — that students can cheat regardless, so the scan is not necessary — is the same empirical premise the test-security literature disputes.
4. **Unproctored ability testing is measurably inflated, and proctoring measurably does not change scores:** the meta-analytic Δ = 0.20 for unproctored assessment and the null performance effect of proctoring in a 1,760-student field study are both credible and are usually cited by opposing camps.
5. **Rank-order instability versus mean shift:** the same meta-analysis reporting a modest mean inflation also reports ρ = .58 between contexts in a small subsample, which matters far more for a ranked selection decision than the mean does — and is the finding most often omitted when the effect is called "small."
6. **The strongest measured harm and the strongest measured benefit are in different currencies:** proctoring's documented cost is anxiety concentrated on already-anxious examinees, and its documented benefit is score integrity; nothing in the located literature converts between them.
7. **Face detection is defended as less invasive than face recognition, and is where the demographic differential was actually measured:** the vendor response distinguishing detection from identity recognition answers the privacy objection while leaving the differential-error objection untouched.
8. **NIST is the most authoritative source on demographic differential and explicitly refuses to attribute it to skin tone**, yet it is routinely cited for exactly that claim — and the study that did measure by skin tone measured gender classification, not detection or verification.
9. **Disability harm has a strong mechanism and a weak measurement base:** the flagging pathway is well-articulated and widely reported by disabled examinees, but no located study measures flag rates against disability status the way one study measured them against skin tone.
10. **Children are the group with the worst measured biometric performance and the strongest legal protection, and the least research attention** — every one of eight recognition systems degraded on children's faces, NIST found elevated false positives at the youngest ages, and no proctoring-specific evaluation on child examinees was located.
11. **EU law prohibits inferring emotions in education while classifying test-behaviour monitoring as merely high-risk**, so a proctoring system's legality can turn on whether an internal signal is characterised as behavioural or affective — a line the vendor draws.
12. **The consenting adult is the adversary:** children's data law requires parental consent, home administration requires parental setup and device provision, and the pediatric tele-assessment literature names the same adult as a source of possible active help — resolved in practice by asking that adult to sign a promise and leave the room.
13. **Requiring a private quiet room is a security control and a means test:** the same environmental specification that makes remote proctoring defensible is one that roughly half of the lowest-income households cannot meet, and at least one state program's stated remedy is to send those students to test in person.
14. **The professional guidance most often cited to justify remote proctoring is co-authored by the vendors' trade association**, which does not make it wrong but means it is not independent in the sense the fairness literature is.
