# Dynamic Assessment: how the field actually scores "learning"

DOK 2 research notes. Sources below were retrieved and verified during this session unless
explicitly flagged as **UNVERIFIED**. Several primary papers are paywalled (SAGE, ScienceDirect

## Sources

1. **Caffrey, E., Fuchs, D., & Fuchs, L. S. (2008).** "The Predictive Validity of Dynamic
   Assessment: A Review." *The Journal of Special Education*, 41(4), 254–270.
   https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022466907310366 (DOI 10.1177/0022466907310366)
   — The closest thing the field has to a predictive-validity meta-review: 24 studies, 15 pooled

   — Establishes the Leipzig *Lerntest* split between **long-term learning tests**

   — The standard meta-analytic reference: 30 studies, 170 effect sizes. Critically, it supplies
   the field's **operational definition**: DA "determines whether substantive changes occur in
   examinee behavior **when feedback is provided** across increasingly complex or challenging
   tasks," explicitly contrasted with "traditional assessment models in which the examiner gives
   no feedback on student performance." Learning potential is defined as "the distance or change
   from unassisted performance to assisted performance."
   *Mean effect sizes NOT retrieved — full text paywalled (JSTOR). Do not cite a number.*

10. **Budoff, M. and colleagues (1967–1976), "Studies in Learning Potential."** Freely available
    ERIC reports, e.g. https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED085962.pdf ("Measuring Learning

    indexed at https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED085963
    — Establishes the **test–train–retest** paradigm and the strongest early psychometric result
    in the whole literature: in n = 627 EMR adolescents, immediate and one-month-delayed posttests

    what is stable here: **the posttest score**, not a gain or slope.

13. **Overall, J. E., & Woodward, J. A. (1975).** "Unreliability of difference scores: A paradox for
    measurement of change." *Psychological Bulletin*; with Cronbach & Furby (1970) and the modern
    rebuttals (Rogosa; Zimmerman & Williams, 1998; Thomas & Zumbo, 2012,
    https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0013164411409929).
    — Establishes the gain-score reliability critique *and* its limits: differencing strips stable
    between-person variance, so change scores are unreliable **precisely when true individual
    differences in change are small** (Rogosa). *Retrieved via secondary synthesis, not primaries.*

14. **Hedge, C., Powell, G., & Sumner, P. (2018).** "The reliability paradox: Why robust cognitive
    tasks do not produce reliable individual differences." *Behavior Research Methods*, 50.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5990556/ (PMC5990556)
    — Establishes the general mechanism: across well-established cognitive tasks reliabilities
    ranged 0 to .82, low because of **small between-person variance**, not large measurement
    error. Homogeneous samples depress reliability at constant error.

## Findings

### 1. What quantity does the field actually score? (Almost never a slope.)

Across all five traditions, the scored quantity is one of four things — and a fitted growth slope
through unaided responses is not among them:

| Tradition | Scored quantity | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| Campione & Brown (graduated prompts) | **Count of standardised hints to criterion**, plus a separate transfer score | Most psychometrically defensible; standardised prompt ladder is what buys the defensibility |
| Budoff (learning potential) | **Post-training score** (test–train–retest); also a gainer/non-gainer/high-scorer *classification* | Empirically the best-supported: stable across a one-month delay, training added 23% unique variance (n = 627) |
| Guthke (Leipzig *Lerntest*) | **Posttest score** after an explicit training phase (long-term) or after in-test assistance (short-term) | Grigorenko explicitly recommends the posttest over gain, citing Guthke (1982) |
| Feuerstein (LPAD) | **Qualitative clinical profile** of deficient cognitive functions + mediation needed | Weakest. Deliberately non-standardised; Frisby & Braden (1992) found the evidence "not compelling"; criticised even from *inside* the DA camp for construct vagueness |
| Sternberg & Grigorenko (dynamic testing) | Pretest/posttest under a transparent, repeatable procedure | Splits the field: "testing" (defensible) vs "assessment" (clinical) |

Two things follow. First, **the field's own consensus is to avoid the difference score.** Grigorenko
(2009) states flatly that "there is no preferred or recommended approach to quantifying change
within DT/A," calls gain scores methodologically troublesome, and recommends *posttest performance*
as the learning-potential indicator instead. Second, **where reliability is acceptable, it attaches
to a level, not to a rate**: Budoff's stability result is a correlation between two posttests, and
Campione's hint count is a count of a standardised stimulus consumed, not a fitted parameter.

The weak end is not ambiguous. The LPAD is the tradition that explicitly abandons standardisation,
and it is the tradition with the least defensible quantification — Lidz's much-quoted line about
that end of the spectrum being "a clinician's dream and a psychometrician's nightmare" captures the
field's own self-assessment.

### 2. Is mediation/feedback definitional? Yes — but there is one real complication.

On the definitional question the field is close to unanimous. Swanson & Lussier's meta-analysis
defines DA as determining "whether substantive changes occur in examinee behavior **when feedback
is provided**," and defines learning potential as "the distance or change from **unassisted**
performance to **assisted** performance" — explicitly contrasting DA with "traditional assessment
models in which the examiner gives no feedback." Guthke's taxonomy is a taxonomy *of where the
intervention goes* (between tests, or inside the test), not of whether there is one. Campione's
score is literally a count of mediation events. Sternberg & Grigorenko's narrower "dynamic testing"
category relaxes the *clinical individualisation*, not the intervention itself.

So on the field's own definitions, a block with no feedback, no hints, no training phase and no
mediation is **not a dynamic assessment**. It is a static test administered above level. Whatever
its merits, it does not inherit DA's evidence base.

**The complication.** Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs (2008) found *superior* predictive validity for DA
"when feedback is not contingent on student response." This is easy to misread as support for a
no-feedback block. It is not: non-contingent feedback still means feedback is *given* — on a fixed
schedule rather than triggered by the individual child's errors. The finding is an argument for
**standardising** the mediation (everyone gets the same input, so between-child variance is in
response-to-input rather than in examiner behaviour), which is the same logic that makes graduated
prompts more defensible than the LPAD. It is not an argument for removing the input. Zero feedback
is not the limiting case of non-contingent feedback; it is a different design with nothing to
respond to.

### 3. Predictive validity: modest, additive, and not established at the gifted tail.

Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs (2008) is the closest thing to a meta-analytic answer (24 studies, 15 pooled
via Pearson r). Its findings are more sobering than DA advocacy usually implies:

- DA and traditional static tests predicted future academic achievement **similarly**. DA did not
  beat static testing on raw prediction.
- DA's contribution is **incremental** — it "may add unique variance," positioning it as a
  supplement, not a replacement.
- Predictive validity was **higher for students with disabilities** than for at-risk or typically
  achieving students.

That last point runs directly against the gifted use case. The population where DA's predictive
validity is best evidenced is the disability tail; the population where it is weakest in this review
is *typically achieving* students — and a top-1–2% sample is further from the disability tail still.
I found **no meta-analytic or large-sample predictive-validity evidence at the gifted tail.** The
gifted-DA literature is position pieces (Lidz & Elliott, 2006; Bolig & Day, 1993 — the latter
**UNVERIFIED**, seen only in secondary citation) and single-site identification studies. Calero
et al. (2011) is the one study directly on high-IQ children and I could not retrieve it (403).

### 4. The equity claim: directionally consistent, evidentially thin.

The claim is real and repeatedly made, but the evidence is weaker than its citation frequency
suggests.

**What holds up best** is Budoff's, and it is not the claim usually advanced. In n = 627 EMR
adolescents, the *training procedure did not elicit systematic differences by race or social class* —
i.e. the intervention itself was not differentially advantaging. That is a fairness property of the
procedure, not a demonstration that DA finds children static tests miss.

**The identification claim** rests on Lidz & Macrine (2001): in a 473-pupil school that was 60%
immigrant/minority, a DA-inclusive procedure identified 5% as gifted whose backgrounds *paralleled*
the school's composition — proportionality achieved. But this is one school, one cohort, no control
condition and no longitudinal outcome follow-up. Proportional identification is a demographic
result, not evidence that the identified children would have thrived.

**The rest is thinner still.** Secondary reviews list Borland & Wright (1994), Kirschenbaum (1998),
Calvo (2004), Sarouphim (1999/2000), Matthews & Foster (2005), and Chaffey et al. (2015, Coolabah
Dynamic Assessment with Indigenous Australian students) as the corpus, and one review I retrieved
concedes the base is "scarce." I did not retrieve any of these primaries; they are **UNVERIFIED**
here. Critically, none of the equity studies I found reports the *counterfactual that matters*:
whether the DA-identified children outperformed demographically matched children who were
DA-identified but not gifted-flagged. The equity claim as it stands is a proportionality claim.

A quiet catch: because DA scores respond to instruction, the equity mechanism is partly that DA
measures something *less* saturated by prior schooling. A no-mediation novel-item block removes the
mechanism while keeping the novelty. Whether novelty alone carries the equity effect is, as far as
I can find, untested.

### 5. Why it never went high-stakes: five converging reasons.

Grigorenko (2009) is unusually blunt, and the reasons are structural rather than fixable-by-effort:

1. **No agreed change metric.** "There is no preferred or recommended approach to quantifying change
   within DT/A." Fifty years in, the field cannot agree what number to report.
2. **Standardisation.** DA is "an umbrella term used to describe a heterogeneous range of
   approaches" with "construct fuzziness"; sandwich-format interventions are "standardized loosely,
   if at all." And standardising trades one problem for another — you replace standardised *testing*
   with standardised *instruction* (Gerber, 2005), which may not suit the child in front of you.
3. **Examiner effects.** Individualisation "requires expert-level skill"; "clinical intuition,
   skills, and expertise mean a lot." LPAD training burden is likened to the TAT's. Practitioner
   surveys cite insufficient skill/knowledge as a top barrier.
4. **Gain-score unreliability.** The Cronbach & Furby / Overall & Woodward critique, plus DA's own
   wrinkle: DA *aims* to change functioning mid-test, which by design "produc[es] unreliability
   among test items," so internal-consistency logic is arguably misapplied and reliability gets
   reported as interrater agreement instead. Rogosa's rebuttal matters but cuts a specific way:
   change scores are unreliable *when true individual differences in change are small* — so the
   rebuttal only helps you if real variance in learning rate exists and is large.
5. **Scale and time.** "No DT/A approaches have ever aimed to be upscaled to systemic levels."
   Roughly one third of familiar practitioners reported using DA, and only rarely, citing time.
   Net verdict: DA is "potentially but not actually useful"; use is "incidental."

## What this means for a 30-item, no-feedback, single-session block

Mapping the design onto the literature, point by point:

**It is not a dynamic assessment under any of the five traditions' definitions.** No hints (so no
Campione hint count), no training phase (so no Budoff or Guthke posttest-after-training), no
mediation (so no LPAD profile), no assisted condition at all (so no "distance from unassisted to
assisted performance," which is Swanson & Lussier's definition of learning potential). It is a
short, above-level, novel-content static test. That is not a criticism of the block's usefulness —
above-level static testing has its own strong track record at the gifted tail (the SMPY/talent-search
tradition) — but it means the block cannot borrow DA's evidence base, and the word "learning rate"
is doing work the design does not support.

**The reported quantity is the one the field abandoned.** A fitted logistic slope is a change
parameter — closer to a gain score than to anything with acceptable reliability in this literature.
Grigorenko's explicit recommendation is to report *posttest level* rather than change. Budoff's
stability finding is about a level. Campione's defensible score is a count of standardised input.
The design has chosen to report the least-defensible family of quantity and, unlike DA, has no
standardised input whose consumption it could count instead.

**Two independent mechanisms predict the observed r = 0.33 at 30 trials.** The gain-score critique
(Cronbach & Furby; Overall & Woodward) says differencing strips stable between-person variance. The
reliability paradox (Hedge et al. 2018) says reliability collapses when *between-person variance is
small relative to error*, and a top-1–2% cohort is by construction a homogeneous sample. These
compound: a change parameter estimated on a restricted-range sample from 30 binary trials is close
to a worst case for individual-differences measurement. Rogosa's defence of gain scores does not
rescue it, because that defence is conditional on real, substantial individual differences in
change — which is exactly what a no-mediation block gives learners no lever to express.

**The positive-slope-from-nothing artefact has a named analogue.** A cohort that learned nothing
still fitting a positive slope is a *measurement* artefact, not a DA-specific problem — but it is
why the field reports counts and levels rather than fitted rates. Nothing in the retrieved
literature offers a validated correction for it.

**The equity rationale does not transfer.** DA's equity mechanism is that responsiveness to
mediation is less saturated by prior instruction than static performance is. Remove the mediation
and the mechanism goes with it; what remains is novelty of content, whose independent equity effect
I found no evidence for either way.

**One thing the design does buy.** The block sidesteps reasons 2, 3 and 5 of the non-adoption list
entirely: it is fully standardised, has zero examiner effects, and costs one session at scale. That
is a genuine engineering advance over examiner-mediated DA — but it purchases those by deleting the
intervention, which is what reasons 1 and 4 were about. The design has solved the tractable problems
and kept the intractable one.

## Productive tension

Genuine unresolved conflicts in the retrieved material — not rhetorical framing:

1. **Standardisation vs. sensitivity.** Every step toward psychometric respectability (fixed prompt
   ladders, computerised delivery, non-contingent feedback) removes the clinical individualisation
   that DA's proponents claim is the source of its unique signal. Feuerstein refused the trade
   outright. Sternberg & Grigorenko resolve it by *renaming* the two halves rather than reconciling
   them. Gerber's point sharpens it: standardising DA swaps standardised testing for standardised
   instruction, so you have not escaped standardisation, only relocated it. **No one in the
   retrieved literature has both.**

2. **Caffrey's non-contingent-feedback finding cuts both ways.** If less individualised feedback
   predicts better, the field's core claim (that responsiveness to *tailored* mediation is the
   signal) is under strain. But the finding also stops short of zero feedback, so it cannot be read
   as vindicating a no-mediation block. It sits awkwardly for both positions.

3. **Rogosa vs. Cronbach & Furby, unresolved and consequential.** Whether the low reliability of
   change scores is a fatal measurement flaw or an artefact of unrealistic assumptions is still
   argued (Thomas & Zumbo, 2012; the Psychometrika multilevel review argues the pessimism should be
   "renounced convincingly"). This matters directly: if Rogosa is right, r = 0.33 might be evidence
   that true individual differences in learning rate are small in this population, rather than
   evidence that the instrument is bad. Those are very different diagnoses with different fixes.

4. **DA's best evidence is at the tail opposite the one being served.** Caffrey found predictive
   validity strongest for students with disabilities and weakest for typically achieving students.
   The gifted application is an extrapolation across the entire ability range, on a literature that
   is position pieces and single sites. Meanwhile the equity argument — DA's most persuasive
   rhetorical asset — is exactly where the empirical base is thinnest.

5. **Reliability may be the wrong criterion, and no one has supplied the right one.** DA aims to
   change functioning mid-test, deliberately violating the consistency assumption that reliability
   coefficients rest on; the field's workaround (interrater agreement) measures examiner consistency,
   not score precision. Embretson's (1991) multidimensional latent trait model for measuring change
   is repeatedly cited as the principled alternative. **I did not retrieve it** — it is the most
   important gap in these notes.

## Starter hypotheses (NOT conclusions)

Each is testable and currently unsupported. None is an insight; they are prompts for the owner.

- **H1 (renaming).** If the block reported *above-level accuracy* rather than a slope, its
  predictive validity would be no worse and its reliability substantially better. Testable directly
  against existing data: correlate total-correct and fitted-slope against the same outcome.
- **H2 (add the input).** Inserting a standardised, non-contingent prompt ladder into the block —
  Campione's design, delivered by software, so zero examiner effects — would produce a *hint count*,
  a quantity with better standing than a slope, at similar session cost. This is the one change the
  literature most directly recommends.
- **H3 (restriction of range, not instrument failure).** The r = 0.33 recovery is driven more by
  homogeneity of a top-1–2% cohort than by trial count. Distinguishable by simulating recovery at
  the same trial count on an unrestricted ability range.
- **H4 (level beats rate at this tail).** In a gifted-tail population, terminal/posttest performance
  on the novel material carries more of whatever signal exists than the fitted rate does — following
  Grigorenko's and Guthke's explicit preference for posttest over change.
- **H5 (novelty ≠ mediation for equity).** Novel content alone does not reproduce DA's demographic
  proportionality effect, because the retrieved equity mechanism runs through responsiveness to
  mediation. Testable by comparing subgroup identification rates for the no-feedback block vs. a
  prompted variant.
- **H6 (the trial-count question is prior).** Before any of the above: how many trials would a
  no-feedback block need for slope recovery adequate for a high-stakes cut? The retrieved literature
  does not answer this, and nothing here suggests 30 is close.

## What this does NOT support

Explicit limits on the above:

- **Not** a claim that the block is useless. Above-level static testing at the gifted tail has its
  own separate evidence base (talent-search / SMPY tradition) which this research did **not**
  examine. The finding is narrower: the block cannot claim DA's warrant.
- **Not** a claim that dynamic assessment works and should be adopted. The retrieved literature says
  the opposite about high-stakes use — Grigorenko's own summary is "potentially but not actually
  useful." Nothing here recommends DA as a replacement screener.
- **Not** a quantitative claim about DA's effect sizes. I could not retrieve Swanson & Lussier's mean
  effect sizes or Caffrey's specific r values (both paywalled). **Do not cite numbers from those two
  papers on the strength of these notes.**
- **Not** evidence about DA at the gifted tail. There is no meta-analysis there. Calero et al. (2011),
  the one study directly on high-IQ children, was not retrievable.
- **Not** verified on several secondary citations. Grigorenko & Sternberg (1998) page range and
  wording, Bolig & Day (1993), Kirschenbaum (1998), Chaffey et al. (2015), Büchel & Scharnhorst
  (1993), Murphy (2011), Embretson (1991), Lidz (1997)'s "psychometrician's nightmare" quote, and
  the Campione & Brown primaries were all seen only through secondary sources or abstracts. Verify
  before any external use.
- **Not** a resolution of the gain-score debate, the standardisation trade-off, or whether r = 0.33
  reflects the instrument or the population. Those are live and belong to the owner.
- **No DOK-3 insight or DOK-4 SPOV is offered or implied here.** This is retrieved evidence plus
  mapping only.
