# BrainLift: Which Measured Signals **Provably** Improve Accuracy and Learning-Rate Estimation

## Owners

- Aadi Takle

## Purpose

### Purpose

A cognitive test can record far more than whether each answer was right: how long the response took, which wrong option was chosen, how confident the person felt, how they moved through the task, and whether they improved across trials. Every one of these is *collectable*, and each is routinely promoted as a richer window into ability. This BrainLift asks a narrower and harder question: for which of these signals does published evidence actually demonstrate **incremental validity** — a measurable gain in precision, validity, or decision accuracy **over correctness alone** — and for which is the evidence absent, thin, or non-replicated? The organising discipline is that "correlates with ability" and "adds information beyond accuracy" are different claims with very different evidence bases, and the literature conflates them constantly. It argues from external evidence and general principles only, and is written to be read with no knowledge of any specific product or codebase. *(DOK 1–2 are AI-assisted and citation-verified; DOK 3–4 are the author's to own. Companion: `source-register-annotated.md`.)*

### In Scope

Measured signals available from a computer-delivered reasoning assessment, each judged against the incremental-validity standard:

- **Response time as measurement information** — joint speed–accuracy modelling, the speed–accuracy tradeoff, evidence-accumulation decomposition, and what timing adds to an ability estimate once modelled properly.
- **Disengagement and effort detection** — rapid-guessing and response-time effort, effort-moderated scoring, and the class of disengagement that latency-based methods structurally cannot see.
- **Process, log, and interactive-task telemetry** — action sequences and behavioural traces, evidence-centred design as the precondition for interpreting them, and the replication and generalisability record.
- **Information in the wrong answer** — distractor-level and polytomous scoring, diagnostic classification, and the formal criterion for when a subscore earns the right to be reported separately.
- **Metacognition and confidence** — calibration and metacognitive sensitivity, the historical verdict on confidence-weighted scoring, and confidence as a fairness hazard.
- **Learning rate and modifiability** — dynamic testing and graduated prompts, the reliability of change and gain scores, individual learning-curve estimation, and practice effects as a contaminant.
- **Statistical power and measurability** throughout: how many trials or occasions a signal needs before an individual-level estimate means anything.

### Out of Scope

- **The structure and sequencing of the test experience** — adaptive routing, item ordering, session design, retake policy, and the cognitive-load grounding of the delivered experience are owned by the companion `test-structure` BrainLift. This BrainLift asks what a signal is *worth*, not where in a test it should be collected.
- **Instrument-level measurement validity and tail-quality metrics** — owned by the companion `gifted-assessment-quality` BrainLift.
- **The statistical methodology for validating a whole instrument** against an established test — reliability machinery, classification and decision accuracy, agreement and linking, DIF and invariance — is owned by the companion `test-evaluation` BrainLift. This BrainLift evaluates *signals*; that one evaluates *instruments*.
- **Causal identification** — separating a program's effect from selection into it is owned by the companion `gt-school-counterfactual` BrainLift.

### Category 1: Response time as measurement information — joint RT–accuracy models

*General idea: Response time is the cheapest process signal to collect during any digital assessment, and a mature psychometric literature exists for modelling it jointly with correctness. The question here is not whether response time correlates with performance — it plainly does — but how much additional measurement information about a person's ability it supplies once correctness has already been used, under what modelling assumptions that gain survives, and what it costs in bias, validity, and estimation burden.*

- **Subcategory 1.1: The joint (hierarchical) framework — what it actually models, and what it assumes**
  - **Source:** van der Linden (2006); van der Linden (2007); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A lognormal model for item response times was specified with a parameter structure analogous to two-parameter logistic item response models: one *speed* parameter per person, plus *time intensity* and *time discrimination* parameters per item. Fitted by Gibbs sampling to response times from an adaptive form of a vocational aptitude battery, the lognormal specification fit well while a normal model could not accommodate the characteristic right skew of the response-time distributions. *(van der Linden, 2006, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 31(2):181–204, DOI 10.3102/10769986031002181.)* `T2`
      - A two-level "plug-and-play" hierarchical framework was proposed in which a measurement model for accuracy (e.g., normal ogive) and a measurement model for response time (e.g., lognormal) are specified separately at the first level and joined at the second level only through correlations among their person parameters and among their item parameters. The paper explicitly criticises earlier response-time models for building a speed–accuracy tradeoff into the parameter structure and for being unclear about which level of parameterisation they represent. *(van der Linden, 2007, *Psychometrika* 72(3):287–308, DOI 10.1007/s11336-006-1478-z.)* `T2`
      - The standard hierarchical model makes five distinct conditional-independence assumptions: local independence of the accuracies; conditional independence of the response times given latent speed; conditional independence of response time and accuracy given both latent variables; conditional independence of response time and ability given speed; and conditional independence of accuracy and speed given ability. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, in *Essays on Contemporary Psychometrics*, Springer, pp. 307–325, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - The same review states that conditional independence of response time and accuracy "often appears to be violated in practice," listing five published lines of evidence, and that both positive and negative conditional dependence can occur on different items *within the same test*, so dependence must be examined item by item. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - The same review notes that no formal empirical study of one of the five assumptions — residual dependence between accuracy and latent speed — had been conducted, and that no test had been developed to detect it. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The dominant approach does not treat response time as a covariate bolted onto a scoring rule. It treats speed as a *separate latent trait* with its own item parameters, and links it to ability only through a population-level correlation. That architecture is what makes the model interpretable, but it also loads the entire claim of added information onto a set of independence assumptions, at least one of which is known to fail routinely and another of which has apparently never been empirically tested at all.
    - **Link to source:** [Hierarchical framework](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-006-1478-z), [Lognormal RT model](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986031002181), [Limitations and risks review](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16)

- **Subcategory 1.2: How much precision does response time actually add? (the incremental-validity core)**
  - **Source:** van der Linden, Klein Entink & Fox (2010); Ranger (2013); Bolsinova & Tijmstra (2018); Reis Costa, Bolsinova, Tijmstra & Andersson (2021); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Within the hierarchical framework, response times were shown to act as *collateral information* for estimating response-model parameters through two channels: the second-level parameter estimates, and the posterior distribution of response parameters given the response times, which functions as an empirical prior. In an empirical study, both the accuracy *and* the bias of item and person parameter estimates improved — unlike conventional hierarchical item response modelling, where accuracy gains are usually purchased with increased bias. *(van der Linden, Klein Entink & Fox, 2010, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 34(5):327–347, DOI 10.1177/0146621609349800.)* `T2` `[COI: the lead author's listed affiliation on this article is CTB/McGraw-Hill, a commercial test publisher]`
      - Two later academic sources independently attribute to Ranger (2013) the result that the size of the precision gain for ability is bounded by the magnitude of the latent speed–ability correlation, and that the marginal gain from additional items decreases rapidly once speed itself is estimated with reasonable precision — so the largest relative gains occur on *short* tests. *(Ranger, 2013, *Psychometrika* 78(3):538–544, DOI 10.1007/s11336-013-9324-6 — bibliographic record verified; substantive content read here only through the two citing sources named next: Reis Costa et al., 2021, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128, and Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - An extension adding cross-loadings between ability and individual response times was proposed, on the argument that the standard model's assumption that *only* overall speed carries ability information ignores collateral information in the residual response times. Gains over both an accuracy-only model and the standard hierarchical model were evaluated by simulation and then applied to a high-stakes educational test. *(Bolsinova & Tijmstra, 2018, *British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology* 71(1):13–38, DOI 10.1111/bmsp.12104; Crossref records online-first 2017-06-21.)* `T2`
      - In the largest reported application — 10 released mathematics items from an international computer-based assessment — expected-a-posteriori reliability was 0.73 for an accuracy-only model, 0.77 for the simple-structure hierarchical model, and 0.80 for the cross-loading model; corresponding average standard errors were 0.51, 0.49, and 0.45. The authors' own characterisation of the difference between models was that "there was no substantial difference," while still describing the precision increase as notable. *(Reis Costa, Bolsinova, Tijmstra & Andersson, 2021, *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:579128, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128.)* `T2`
      - In the same study's country-level analyses, the *additional* reliability increase from adding cross-loadings on top of the simple-structure hierarchical model was "generally between 0.02 and 0.03," and was generally *less* sizable than the increase obtained from moving from the accuracy-only model to the simple hierarchical model; the authors conclude "the biggest gain in precision was already obtained by using a simple-structure hierarchical model." They also warn that the extra information captured "may not reflect the testing objective." *(Reis Costa et al., 2021, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128.)* `T2`
      - A review by two of that study's co-authors states the opposite theoretical expectation for the cross-loading extension: because its collateral information "increases linearly with every additional item," whereas the standard hierarchical model "can never do better than the inclusion of a single perfectly estimated covariate," the cross-loading gain "may exceed the original gain" and this is "especially likely for larger tests." *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the only subcategory that speaks directly to incremental validity, and the honest reading is that the effect is real, replicated, and modest. On a short mathematics test, using response times moved a reliability coefficient from 0.73 to 0.80 and shrank the average standard error from 0.51 to 0.45 — and the authors who produced those numbers described the between-model difference as not substantial. The gain has a theoretical ceiling set by how strongly speed and ability correlate in the population, and the marginal contribution shrinks as more items are administered, which means the signal is most useful precisely where there is least accuracy data. The elaborated cross-loading model is theoretically expected to scale better with test length, but in the one large empirical application reported here it added only 0.02–0.03 on top of the simple model.
    - **Link to source:** [Collateral information](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621609349800), [Bound on the gain](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-013-9324-6), [Cross-loading extension](https://doi.org/10.1111/bmsp.12104), [Large-scale application](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.579128)

- **Subcategory 1.3: The speed–accuracy tradeoff — why "faster = smarter" is a category error**
  - **Source:** Heitz (2014); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023); Goldhammer (2015); Goldhammer & Kroehne (2014); Goldhammer, Naumann, Stelter, Tóth, Rölke & Klieme (2014); Naumann & Goldhammer (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The speed–accuracy tradeoff — that increasing the speed at which a cognitive task is executed generally decreases accuracy — is documented as a *within-person* phenomenon with its own history, physiology, and methodology. *(Heitz, 2014, *Frontiers in Neuroscience* 8:150, DOI 10.3389/fnins.2014.00150.)* `T2`
      - A methodological review states explicitly that the between-person speed–ability correlation estimated in joint models "should not be confused with" the within-person speed–accuracy tradeoff, that the tradeoff is only *one* of the factors contributing to the between-person association, and that a more competent person can be both faster and more accurate — which is why the between-person correlation can be positive even though the within-person tradeoff pushes it negative. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - The same review reports that published estimates of the between-person speed–ability correlation include positive values, negative values, and values close to zero, citing separate empirical studies for each case. It further states that no inference about what would happen to a person's performance if they responded faster or slower can be drawn from this correlation, because that is a counterfactual within-person question the model cannot address. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - Separating ability from speed was argued to require *experimental control* of response speed rather than statistical modelling alone, in a target article devoted to the problem of whether a test measures ability, speed, or both. *(Goldhammer, 2015, *Measurement: Interdisciplinary Research and Perspectives* 13(3–4):133–164, DOI 10.1080/15366367.2015.1100020.)* `T2`
      - A method for experimentally controlling the time individuals spend on task in speeded performance measures was developed and reported. *(Goldhammer & Kroehne, 2014, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 38(4):255–267, DOI 10.1177/0146621613517164.)* `T2`
      - In 1,020 adults aged 16–65 completing computer-based reading and problem-solving tasks, the time-on-task effect *reversed sign by task type*: positive in problem solving (fixed effect β₁ = 0.56, z = 2.30, p = .02, at average task difficulty) and negative in reading. In problem solving the positive effect increased with task difficulty and decreased with skill; in reading the negative effect was stronger for easier tasks and increased with skill. The authors conclude time on task "has no uniform interpretation." *(Goldhammer, Naumann, Stelter, Tóth, Rölke & Klieme, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(3):608–626, DOI 10.1037/a0034716.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up study reported that time-on-task effects in digital reading are *non-linear* and moderated by both person skills and task demands. *(Naumann & Goldhammer, 2017, *Learning and Individual Differences* 53:1–16, DOI 10.1016/j.lindif.2016.10.002.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Speed and ability are separate latent quantities, and the sign of their relationship is not a fact of nature — it is an empirical property of a particular test, task type, difficulty level, and examinee skill level. The same behaviour (spending longer) predicts better outcomes on tasks requiring controlled processing and worse outcomes on tasks that reward fluent routine processing, and the effect is non-linear on top of that. Crucially, even a well-estimated between-person speed–ability correlation licenses no statement at all about what would happen if a given individual sped up or slowed down; that within-person question requires an experimental manipulation of speed, not a richer model.
    - **Link to source:** [Speed–accuracy tradeoff review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00150), [Time-on-task sign reversal](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034716), [Non-linearity](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2016.10.002), [Ability, speed, or both](https://doi.org/10.1080/15366367.2015.1100020)

- **Subcategory 1.4: Evidence-accumulation decomposition — drift rate as an ability-like parameter**
  - **Source:** Ratcliff, Schmiedek & McKoon (2008); Schmiedek, Oberauer, Wilhelm, Süß & Wittmann (2007); Lerche, von Krause, Voss, Frischkorn, Schubert & Hagemann (2020); Dutilh, Vandekerckhove, Ly, Matzke, Pedroni, Frey, Rieskamp & Wagenmakers (2017); Schubert (2019); van der Maas, Molenaar, Maris, Kievit & Borsboom (2011)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Simulations showed that the diffusion model reproduces the "worst performance rule" — stronger correlations between intelligence and slow response-time quantiles than fast ones — under *either* of two different assumptions: that higher-ability people have higher drift rates, *or* that they have lower boundary separation. The two accounts are distinguishable only because the model also predicts accuracy. The pattern emerged only when other components of processing also varied across subjects. *(Ratcliff, Schmiedek & McKoon, 2008, *Intelligence* 36(1):10–17, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2006.12.002.)* `T2`
      - Individual differences in components of response-time distributions were related to working memory and intelligence; this study is repeatedly cited in later peer-reviewed work as having shown that working-memory capacity can be predicted from drift rate across a range of response-time tasks. *(Schmiedek, Oberauer, Wilhelm, Süß & Wittmann, 2007, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 136(3):414–429, DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.136.3.414 — bibliographic record verified; the specific characterisation above is taken from citing sources, not from the article text, and no effect size is asserted here.)* `T2`
      - In structural equation models, a domain-general drift-rate factor showed a strong relationship with general intelligence, and domain-specific drift factors related to matching domain scores: figural intelligence with figural drift r = .90 — which the authors themselves caution against overinterpreting because the residual variance of figural drift did not differ significantly from zero — numeric r = .74, and verbal r = .50. A latent psychometric-speed factor correlated most highly with latent drift rate (r = .59), less with threshold separation (r = .42), and essentially not at all with nondecision time (r = .04). The authors also report that models of *mean response time* did not reproduce the domain-general/domain-specific pattern that drift rates did. *(Lerche, von Krause, Voss, Frischkorn, Schubert & Hagemann, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(12):2207–2249, DOI 10.1037/xge0000774.)* `T2`
      - In a preregistered, analyst-blinded study of 916 participants, the classical worst performance rule was *rejected* for both easy and difficult perceptual stimuli; after exploratory release of the sign constraints on the correlations, the evidence still favoured absence of the effect (Bayes factors in favour of the null of 6.29 for easy and 19.9 for hard stimuli), and controlling for response caution by splitting into ten homogeneous-caution subgroups did not change the conclusion. In the *same* data, the predicted positive correlation between working-memory capacity and diffusion drift rate was "strongly confirmed" for both difficulty levels, although the authors note the drift–capacity correlations were moderate relative to comparable published studies and raise selective reporting and publication bias as a possible explanation for the apparent robustness of the worst performance rule in the wider literature. *(Dutilh, Vandekerckhove, Ly, Matzke, Pedroni, Frey, Rieskamp & Wagenmakers, 2017, *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics* 79(3):713–725, DOI 10.3758/s13414-017-1304-y.)* `T2`
      - A meta-analysis across 19 studies containing 23 datasets and 3,767 participants found robust evidence for the worst performance rule, but with a *logarithmic* rather than linear increase across response-time quantiles: r = −0.18 for fast responses, −0.28 for average responses, and −0.33 for slow responses. The largest change was between the fastest and average responses, not between average and slowest — leading the author to propose renaming it the "not-best performance rule." The effect was *attenuated* in less intelligent samples and was *larger* when response times were correlated with speed tests than with intelligence or memory tests. *(Schubert, 2019, *Intelligence* 73:88–100, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2019.02.003.)* `T1`
      - An explicit theoretical link was argued between process models for decision making and latent variable models for individual differences. *(van der Maas, Molenaar, Maris, Kievit & Borsboom, 2011, *Psychological Review* 118(2):339–356, DOI 10.1037/a0022749 — title- and bibliographic-level verification only in this pass; no specific finding is claimed here.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The evidence-accumulation decomposition is the strongest theoretical case for response time carrying ability information, because it explains *why* time and correctness are two views of one process, and because drift rate — not raw or mean response time — is the parameter that behaves like an ability. Latent drift-rate factors relate substantially to intelligence factors and outperform mean response time in reproducing the structure of intelligence. But the headline empirical regularity built on raw quantiles is shakier than its reputation: a large preregistered blinded test failed to find the worst performance rule at all while simultaneously confirming the drift-rate mechanism said to cause it, and the meta-analytic pattern is compressed into the fast-versus-average contrast rather than the average-versus-slow contrast the rule emphasises. Two of the meta-analytic moderator results also cut against a strong ability interpretation: the effect is weaker in lower-ability samples and stronger against speed tests than against intelligence tests.
    - **Link to source:** [Diffusion account of the worst performance rule](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2006.12.002), [Drift rates and intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000774), [Preregistered blinded test](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-017-1304-y), [Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2019.02.003)

- **Subcategory 1.5: Estimation requirements — what it costs to get a usable response-time-derived parameter**
  - **Source:** Lerche, Voss & Nagler (2017); Lerche & Voss (2017); Enkavi, Eisenberg, Bissett, Mazza, MacKinnon, Marsch & Poldrack (2019); Kang, De Boeck & Ratcliff (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In simulation studies spanning trial numbers from 24 to 5,000 and three optimisation criteria, maximum likelihood was superior for uncontaminated data while Kolmogorov–Smirnov outperformed the alternatives in the presence of fast contaminants, and chi-square was generally least precise. The authors report that "under certain conditions even small numbers of trials (N < 100) are sufficient for robust parameter estimation," with parsimonious three- and four-parameter models achievable at fewer than 100 trials and six- and seven-parameter models requiring more. The associated doctoral work states that increasing trials beyond 500 improves precision only marginally, and that fixing the intertrial variabilities of drift rate and starting point yields more reliable estimates of the four main parameters. *(Lerche, Voss & Nagler, 2017, *Behavior Research Methods* 49(2):513–537, DOI 10.3758/s13428-016-0740-2; Crossref records online-first 2016-06-10. Supporting statements: Lerche, 2016 dissertation, DOI 10.11588/heidok.00022279.)* `T2` and `T3` respectively
      - In two studies with sessions one week apart — 105 participants on a lexical decision and a recognition memory task, and 128 participants on an associative priming task — retest reliability of the main diffusion parameters was reported as satisfying, with r > 0.70 for drift rate and threshold separation in all three tasks. *(Lerche & Voss, 2017, *Psychological Research* 81(3):629–652, DOI 10.1007/s00426-016-0770-5; Crossref records online-first 2016-04-23.)* `T2`
      - A large retest study reported that self-report survey measures showed high test–retest reliability while measures derived from behavioural tasks did not, both in a systematic literature review and in a new sample, and attributed the difference to between-subject variability rather than to measurement noise alone. The authors also compared model-derived parameters against raw response times. *(Enkavi, Eisenberg, Bissett, Mazza, MacKinnon, Marsch & Poldrack, 2019, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 116(12):5472–5477, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1818430116. This study concerns self-regulation task batteries, not ability tests; specific reliability coefficients are not asserted here.)* `T2`
      - A diffusion-based item response model was published specifically to represent conditional dependence between response accuracy and response time. *(Kang, De Boeck & Ratcliff, 2022, *Psychometrika* 87(2):725–748, DOI 10.1007/s11336-021-09819-5 — title- and bibliographic-level verification only; no finding claimed.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The trial-count objection to evidence-accumulation modelling is weaker than commonly assumed, but only for deliberately simplified models: parsimonious specifications can recover parameters from under a hundred trials of a *binary* task, and the drift-rate estimates so obtained show week-to-week retest correlations above 0.70. Two constraints matter more than trial count. First, these results come from repeated homogeneous two-choice trials, not from heterogeneous curriculum items. Second, the broader literature on individual differences from behavioural tasks reports systematically weak retest reliability relative to self-report, which is a warning about the general enterprise of deriving stable person parameters from task behaviour rather than a specific indictment of diffusion modelling.
    - **Link to source:** [Trial-count requirements](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-016-0740-2), [Retest reliability of diffusion parameters](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-016-0770-5), [Task-measure reliability](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818430116)

- **Subcategory 1.6: Response time in adaptive testing, item selection, and time-limit management**
  - **Source:** van der Linden, Scrams & Schnipke (1999); van der Linden (2009); Fan, Wang, Chang & Douglas (2012); Choe, Kern & Chang (2018); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023); Wise & Kong (2005); Wise (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Response-time constraints were proposed as a mechanism for controlling *differential speededness* in computerised adaptive testing — the problem that an adaptive test can function as a speed test for some examinees and a power test for others. *(van der Linden, Scrams & Schnipke, 1999, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 23(3):195–210, DOI 10.1177/01466219922031329.)* `T2`
      - A predictive-control method for managing speededness during adaptive testing was subsequently published. *(van der Linden, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 33(1):25–41, DOI 10.1177/0146621607314042; Crossref records online-first 2008-03-19 with issue volume 33(1), commonly cited as 2009.)* `T2` `[COI: at around this period the author's listed affiliation on related work was a commercial test publisher]`
      - Two item-selection criteria using a lognormal response-time model were proposed: maximising information per unit of time, and an inverse-time-weighted stratification variant intended to give more balanced item exposure than the purely information-based approach. The stated motivation is that the most informative item at the current ability estimate may be very time-consuming and therefore not the most efficient item to administer. *(Fan, Wang, Chang & Douglas, 2012, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 37(5):655–670, DOI 10.3102/1076998611422912.)* `T2`
      - A follow-up study characterises the information-per-unit-time criterion as effectively reducing average completion time for a fixed-length test "with minimal decrease in the accuracy of ability estimation," but also as producing "extremely unbalanced exposure of items"; and it reports that the recommended exposure-control remedy comes "at substantial costs of attenuating the reduction in average testing time, increasing the variance of testing times, and further decreasing estimation accuracy." *(Choe, Kern & Chang, 2018, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 43(2):135–158, DOI 10.3102/1076998617723642; Crossref records online-first 2017-08-18.)* `T2`
      - A methodological review warns that once speed influences the ability estimate, a well-informed examinee can optimise speed to maximise the estimate — responding as slowly as the time limit permits when the speed–ability association is negative — and concludes that using the hierarchical model to improve precision of ability estimates in high-stakes settings "may be ill-advised," while being more defensible in low-stakes settings. The same review notes that in low-stakes settings the risk of heterogeneous and disengaged responding is *higher*, that disengaged responses will bias the estimated speed–ability correlation, and that "it is unlikely that any method will succeed in detecting disengaged responses with such a degree of accuracy that their presence no longer biases" that correlation. *(Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10370-4_16.)* `T2`
      - A response-time-based index of examinee effort ("response time effort") was introduced as a measure of motivation in computer-based tests, and rapid-guessing behaviour was later reviewed as an identifiable, interpretable phenomenon with measurement consequences. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2; Wise, 2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 36(4):52–61, DOI 10.1111/emip.12165.)* `T2` `[COI: the 2017 article lists the author's affiliation as NWEA, a commercial assessment provider; the 2005 article's affiliation was not verified in this pass]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** In adaptive delivery, the best-evidenced uses of response time are *operational* rather than psychometric: equalising how speeded a test feels across examinees, and buying back testing time by preferring items that yield information per second rather than information per item. Even that trade is not free — the time savings are purchased with unbalanced item exposure, and the standard fix for exposure gives back much of the time saving while further degrading estimation accuracy. There is also a direct incentive argument against letting response time enter a consequential score: if speed shifts the estimate, speed becomes gameable, and the review literature says so explicitly. Separately, response time has a well-established use in flagging non-effortful responding, which is information about *engagement*, not about ability.

- **Subcategory 1.7: Where response-time information is confounded — position, fatigue, reading load, motor speed, and age**
  - **Source:** Kail (1991); Ratcliff, Love, Thompson & Opfer (2012); Thompson, Ratcliff & McKoon (2016); Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023); Debeer & Janssen (2013); Fox & Marianti (2016); Naumann & Goldhammer (2017); AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A review of 72 published studies yielding 1,826 paired response times (an adult condition mean paired with the corresponding younger-group mean) found that children's and adolescents' response times increase linearly as a function of adult response times in corresponding conditions, and that the amount of increase declines with age in a manner well described by an exponential function — consistent with a general, non-task-specific component of processing speed that changes rapidly in childhood and more slowly in adolescence. *(Kail, 1991, *Psychological Bulletin* 109(3):490–501, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.109.3.490; indexed as a review.)* `T1`
      - With 130 children (mean ages spanning 8.51–15.68 years) and 72 college-aged adults on numerosity discrimination and lexical decision, diffusion fits showed that *all* model components contributed to children's slower responses: children extracted lower-quality evidence (lower drift rate), boundary separation decreased significantly with age, and the nondecision component — which includes encoding, memory access, and response execution — also decreased significantly with age. Older adults, by contrast, extract evidence of similar quality to younger adults. *(Ratcliff, Love, Thompson & Opfer, 2012, *Child Development* 83(1):367–381, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8624.2011.01683.x.)* `T2`

      - Joint modelling of ability together with *differential speed* — that is, allowing the latent speed of an examinee to change over the course of a test rather than assuming it is stationary — has been developed. *(Fox & Marianti, 2016, *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 51(4):540–553, DOI 10.1080/00273171.2016.1171128 — title- and bibliographic-level verification only.)* `T2`

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Any measured latency is a sum of a decision component, an encoding component, and a motor-execution component, and for children the age-related change in the non-decision components and in decision caution is as large and as systematic as the change in evidence quality. That is the sharpest form of the confounding problem: two children of identical ability can differ in measured time because one is more cautious or slower to read and press, and in at least one developmental sample individual accuracy and response time were statistically unrelated because they are governed by different parameters. Layered on top of that are position and fatigue effects that break the stationarity assumption the standard joint model relies on, and reading demand that loads on time independently of the target construct. The professional standards framing matters here because the failure mode is not noise — a confounded time signal can raise apparent precision while moving the estimate *away* from the construct.

#### Tensions and contradictions

- **Does the cross-loading extension add a lot or a little?** The same two authors state in a review that the cross-loading gain "may exceed the original gain" from adding speed, "especially for larger tests" (Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023), while the large empirical application they co-authored found the additional gain was "generally between 0.02 and 0.03" and "generally less sizable" than the accuracy-only-to-hierarchical step, concluding that "the biggest gain in precision was already obtained by using a simple-structure hierarchical model" (Reis Costa et al., 2021). The theoretical argument and the one large empirical test point in opposite directions. Note that the empirical test used only 10 items, which is exactly the short-test regime where the simple model is expected to do best — so the tension may be a test-length artefact rather than a genuine disagreement, but no one in these sources resolves it.
- **Is the worst performance rule real?** A meta-analysis of 19 studies and 3,767 participants reports "robust evidence" for it (Schubert, 2019), while a preregistered, analyst-blinded study of 916 participants found evidence *for the null* (Bayes factors 6.29 and 19.9 favouring absence) and explicitly raised publication bias as an explanation for the meta-analytic picture (Dutilh et al., 2017). A meta-analysis inherits the selection biases of its input literature; a single preregistered study inherits the idiosyncrasies of one task and sample. Both authors independently converge on the same *mechanism* (drift rate), which makes this a disagreement about the raw-quantile phenomenon, not about evidence accumulation.
- **Does response time make the estimate better or just tighter?** van der Linden, Klein Entink & Fox (2010) report that response times improved *both* accuracy and bias — unusual for a hierarchical borrowing-strength method. Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023) argue the opposite risk: because ability now depends on speed, a misspecified speed–ability relationship introduces bias that an accuracy-only model would not have had, and the resulting increase in precision may come "at the cost of increasing the discrepancy" between the latent variable and the intended construct. Precision and validity are pulling apart, and the sources disagree about which direction dominates.
- **Do accuracy and time carry the same information or different information?** The diffusion framing says they are two projections of one process (Ratcliff, Schmiedek & McKoon, 2008), which is why drift rate can be recovered from their joint distribution. But in a developmental sample, individual accuracy and response time were *uncorrelated*, because accuracy tracked drift rate while time tracked boundary separation (Thompson, Ratcliff & McKoon, 2016). Both can be true simultaneously, but they imply very different expectations about how much a time measurement adds once accuracy is known.
- **Low stakes as the safe harbour, or the dangerous one?** Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023) argue that using speed to sharpen ability estimates is more defensible in low-stakes settings because a little bias is tolerable — and then, in the same section, that low-stakes settings carry the greatest risk of disengaged and heterogeneous responding, which is precisely what biases the speed–ability correlation, and that no detection method is likely to be accurate enough to eliminate the problem. The recommended safe harbour contains the recommended hazard.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each item below is a starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate, test, and either own or discard. None of these is a finding, and none of them is yet the author's position.*

1. **Starter hypothesis: the response-time precision gain may be worth roughly a modest increase in test length, and that is the honest unit to argue in.** The published figures are reliability 0.73 → 0.80 and average standard error 0.51 → 0.45 on a 10-item test (Reis Costa et al., 2021). *Author's own arithmetic, to be checked:* applying the Spearman–Brown relation, moving a reliability of 0.73 to 0.80 corresponds to roughly 1.5 times the test length — about five more items on a ten-item test. That framing makes the trade concrete and falsifiable. It also has two caveats the author must resolve before using it: expected-a-posteriori reliability is not classical reliability, and Spearman–Brown assumes parallel added items. If the arithmetic survives scrutiny, the interesting question is whether the modelling complexity, the bias risk, and the confound exposure documented in subcategories 1.6 and 1.7 are a better deal than simply asking a handful of additional questions.
2. **Starter hypothesis: the strongest evidence-based case for response time may be about *engagement and process*, not about *ability*.** The best-replicated operational uses in these sources are detecting non-effortful responding (Wise & Kong, 2005; Wise, 2017), equalising speededness across examinees (van der Linden, Scrams & Schnipke, 1999), and buying testing time (Fan et al., 2012), while the ability-precision gain is modest and bounded (Ranger, 2013, as reported by two citing sources; Reis Costa et al., 2021). If the author pursues this, the claim to test is that response time's incremental value is highest for *validity screening* — deciding whether a response should count at all — and lowest for *score sharpening*. The counter-evidence to hunt for is any study showing a large ability-precision gain that is not attributable to filtering out disengaged responses.
3. **Starter hypothesis: for children, the non-decision and caution components may dominate the ability component enough to invert the usual reasoning.** Children differ from adults on *every* diffusion component including boundary separation and nondecision time (Ratcliff, Love, Thompson & Opfer, 2012), overall developmental speed change is well described by a single general age function across many tasks (Kail, 1991), and in one developmental sample accuracy and time were uncorrelated because they were driven by different parameters (Thompson, Ratcliff & McKoon, 2016). The hypothesis to interrogate is that in a developing population a raw time signal is closer to a measure of caution, reading fluency, and motor execution than of ability — and therefore that any use of it must either model those components explicitly or be restricted to within-person comparisons where they are held roughly constant. The author should look specifically for studies that estimate how much of the between-child variance in latency is non-decision variance, since none of the sources here quantifies that share directly.
4. **Starter hypothesis: the "collateral information" argument and the "construct-irrelevant variance" argument may be the same mechanism viewed from two ends.** Response time helps sharpen the estimate exactly to the extent that speed correlates with whatever the score is tracking (Ranger, 2013, as reported by citing sources), but if part of what the score tracks is construct-irrelevant — test-taking experience, reading skill — then borrowing strength from speed imports more of that irrelevant part (Tijmstra & Bolsinova, 2023). The testable version: the size of the precision gain and the size of the construct-contamination risk should be *positively* related, not independent. If that holds, then a large observed precision gain is a warning sign rather than a success metric, which is close to the opposite of how such gains are usually reported.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **Nothing in this category addresses learning rate.** Every source located here targets a static ability or trait estimate. No evidence was found in this pass that response time adds incremental information about a *rate of change* or growth parameter. Searches for peer-reviewed evidence that response-time features improve student-model or knowledge-tracing predictions beyond correctness surfaced only low-quality or preprint material that did not meet the citation bar, so no claim — positive or negative — is recorded. This is the single largest gap relative to the organising question and should be treated as unresolved rather than as a null result.
- **The quantitative incremental-validity evidence rests on very few applications.** The specific numbers reported here (reliability 0.73/0.77/0.80; standard errors 0.51/0.49/0.45; cross-loading gains of 0.02–0.03) come from a *single* study on 10 items of one mathematics assessment (Reis Costa et al., 2021). No meta-analysis of incremental precision gains from joint response-time modelling was located. Any general claim about "how much response time adds" is currently a one-study claim.
- **Ranger (2013) was not read directly.** Its bibliographic record was verified against Crossref, but the substantive results attributed to it — that the gain is bounded by the speed–ability correlation and that marginal gains decay with test length — were read through two academic citing sources (one journal article and one edited-volume chapter). Both cite it consistently, but the original derivation and any conditions attached to it were not inspected.
- **Effect sizes for the drift-rate/intelligence link vary widely and one is unstable.** The reported figural drift–figural intelligence correlation of r = .90 is disclaimed by its own authors because the residual variance was not significantly different from zero (Lerche et al., 2020). The domain-specific values (.74 numeric, .50 verbal) and the psychometric-speed-to-drift value (.59) are the ones to rely on. Independent replication of the domain-general/domain-specific pattern was not located in this pass.
- **The most relevant paradigm mismatch is unmeasured.** All diffusion estimation guidance located here (Lerche, Voss & Nagler, 2017; Lerche & Voss, 2017) comes from many repeated trials of homogeneous *binary* tasks. No source was found establishing whether drift rate can be recovered with comparable reliability from a short sequence of heterogeneous curriculum items with more than two response options. Trial-count feasibility claims should not be transferred across that boundary without evidence.
- **One of the five conditional-independence assumptions has apparently never been tested.** Tijmstra & Bolsinova (2023) state that residual dependence between accuracy and latent speed has not been formally studied in real data and that no detection test exists. Model fit therefore cannot currently be claimed as complete for the standard joint framework.
- **Device, input modality, and mode confounds are asserted here only indirectly.** Targeted searches did not surface a peer-reviewed study isolating the effect of input device (touch versus mouse versus keyboard) on measured item response times in child assessment. The motor-and-encoding confound is supported here only through the diffusion nondecision-time findings (Ratcliff et al., 2012; Thompson et al., 2016), which show that the non-decision component varies systematically with age but do not decompose it by device. This should be flagged as a reasoned inference from adjacent evidence, not a documented finding.
- **The professional-standards citation is at the level of general principle.** The joint testing standards were cited for the requirement to validate score interpretations for their intended use and for the treatment of construct-irrelevant variance as a validity threat, but specific standard numbers, edition pagination, and ISBN were not verified against the source text in this pass.
- **Publication bias is an open question, raised by the sources themselves.** Dutilh et al. (2017) explicitly suggest that the apparent ubiquity of the worst performance rule may partly reflect selective reporting, and state that this can only be resolved by additional large-scale preregistered studies. No such follow-up replication was located here.
- **COI coverage is incomplete.** Commercial affiliations were confirmed via indexed metadata for two of the sources cited (a commercial test publisher for one modelling paper; a commercial assessment provider for the rapid-guessing work). ### Category 2: Disengagement and effort as a measured signal

*General idea: Effort signals are unusual among behavioural measures because their value comes from subtraction, not addition — they identify item responses that carry no information about ability so those responses can be excluded, rather than adding a new source of variance to the estimate. The evidence base is unusually strong on detection and unusually weak on the part that matters: response-time methods reliably flag fast disengagement, but the best available external validation suggests they capture only a fraction of the total score distortion caused by disengagement, and the act of filtering can itself introduce bias when disengagement is related to ability or group membership.*

- **Subcategory 2.3: Effort-moderated scoring and model-based treatment of non-effortful responding**
  - **Source:** Wise & DeMars (2006); Schnipke & Scrams (1997); Yamamoto (1995)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**

  - **Source:** Rios & Soland (2021a, 2021b); Rios & Deng (2025); Wise & Kingsbury (2016)

- **Subcategory 2.4: The detection ceiling — slow and passive disengagement that response times cannot see**
  - **Source:** Wise & Kuhfeld (2021) — retest-based external validation
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Using achievement test data from test takers who were quickly retested and showed differing degrees of disengagement between the two sittings, standard effort-moderated scoring accounted for roughly one-third of the score distortion attributable to differential disengagement. *(Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 58(1):130–149, DOI 10.1111/jedm.12275.)* `T2` `[COI: NWEA]`

      - The majority of these methods attempt to locate the boundary between engaged and disengaged response-time distributions, and that boundary is defined by a particular type of disengaged response — a rapid guess with insufficient time for an engaged interaction; disengaged interactions of longer duration such as mind-wandering and off-task behavior are possible but have received less research attention on identification. *(Lehman, Sparks & Steinberg, 2024, *ETS Research Report Series* 2024(1):1–15, DOI 10.1002/ets2.12384.)* `T3` `[COI: ETS]`

      - Slow responding in international large-scale assessments has received far less attention than rapid guessing; sequence analysis of log data from one complex problem-solving item identified two distinct clusters among slow responders, one with long pauses interspersed with repeated inefficient attempts and one with shorter pauses coupled with inefficient exploratory actions. *(Jerez, Mazzullo & Bulut, 2026, *Computers* 15(1):64, DOI 10.3390/computers15010064.)* `T2`
      - In an adaptive test, the item-selection consequences of a burst of rapid guessing persisted long after the disengagement itself had ceased, because the depressed provisional ability estimate caused subsequent items to be mistargeted; treating rapid guesses as missing when computing provisional estimates was proposed as the remedy. *(Wise, 2023, *Journal of Computerized Adaptive Testing* 10(2), DOI 10.7333/2305-1002022.)* `T3` `[COI: NWEA]`

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Effort is a within-session state, not a trait, and the strongest predictor of losing it is simply how long the person has already been working. Two convergent findings support a resource-depletion reading rather than a motivation-only reading: effort declines across the day even though the items are identical, and it declines with position even when position is experimentally varied. The mechanism offered for that jump is explicitly a folk belief in the source itself, so the age gradient is currently a documented regularity without an established cause. All of this evidence about the youngest test takers comes from group-administered school assessments; none of it addresses individually administered or shorter-session testing of young children.

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **The average distortion is small, but the practice is treated as essential.** The meta-analytic average effect of rapid guessing on aggregated scores is 0.13 SD (Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022), and the conceptual literature nonetheless argues that including rapid guesses in scoring "makes little sense" as a matter of principle (Wise, 2017). Both can be true — a 0.13 SD average with I² above 92% means some contexts are badly affected and most are barely affected — but the strength of the normative claim is not proportional to the average magnitude of the problem.

2. **Filtering can overcorrect by more than the error it removes.** Careless responding depressed a group mean by about 0.20 SD, while listwise deletion of unmotivated examinees inflated it by up to 0.42 SD when ability and disengagement were related (Rios, Guo, Mao & Liu, 2017). Since a negative relationship between rapid-guessing propensity and ability is documented in real testing contexts (Rios & Deng, 2025), the harmful condition is not a corner case.

3. **The model's key assumption is contradicted by the model's own literature.** Effort-moderated scoring assumes non-effortful responding is unrelated to ability (Rios & Soland, 2021). Independent work states that lower-ability examinees are more likely to rapid-guess in certain testing contexts (Rios & Deng, 2025), and a separate modelling study found that the sole significant examinee-level predictor of rapid guessing was an ability test score — though the direction of that association was not confirmed in the material reviewed here (Wise, Pastor & Kong, 2009). Under assumption violation the model's advantage over doing nothing disappears for mean ability estimates specifically.

4. **The method's own developers report that it recovers only a minority of the distortion.** Standard effort-moderated scoring accounted for roughly one-third of disengagement-driven score distortion in a retest design (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021), yet the same threshold family is described elsewhere as validated and operationally sufficient. The gap is closed only by loosening thresholds — which increases false-positive removal of fast, genuinely effortful responders (Wise, 2017; Şahin & Colvin, 2020).

5. **Prevalence estimates differ by more than an order of magnitude.** Rapid guessing was found on about 1% of item responses on one commercial low-stakes instrument (Setzer et al., 2013) versus 6.89% in the meta-analytic average (Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022). The threshold procedure used partly determines this (Rios & Deng, 2021), so prevalence is not cleanly separable from measurement decisions.

6. **Detecting a behaviour and improving an estimate are demonstrably different achievements.** Help-seeking feedback durably changed the targeted behaviour and produced no learning gain (Aleven et al., 2016). Among four measured off-task behaviours, three had no relationship with learning once prior knowledge was controlled (Baker et al., 2004). The engagement-detection literature is not entitled to assume that a validated detector implies a better ability estimate.

7. **The headline motivation effect is retracted, but the validity-threat framing built on it persists.** The incentive effect on ability scores fell from g = 0.64 to a publication-bias-corrected g = 0.16 whose interval includes zero (retraction notice, 2025). Meanwhile individual experiments report effects several times larger (Liu, Rios & Borden, 2015). Whether motivation moves measured ability a lot or barely at all is currently unsettled at the level of pooled evidence.

8. **Fairness cuts both ways and the literature does not resolve which way dominates.** Differential engagement can manufacture spurious differential item functioning in up to 18% of items when true item parameters are identical (DeMars & Wise, 2010) and bias latent mean comparisons by 0.18 SD at a 7% differential rate (Rios, 2021) — arguments for adjustment. Yet substantial subgroup differences in rapid guessing mostly failed to change achievement-gap interpretations in a large empirical study (Soland, 2018) — an argument that adjustment changes little. Filtering is itself a group-differential deletion process.

9. **A large body of the strongest applied evidence is produced by parties with a commercial interest in it.** The retest validation, the operational implementation, the proctor-notification evaluation, the grade and time-of-day patterns, and the achievement-gap analysis are all authored by researchers affiliated with commercial assessment providers. The findings include self-critical results, which is reassuring, but independent replication of the retest-based recovery estimate does not appear to exist.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each item below is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. Each is stated so that it could be shown wrong.*

1. **Starter hypothesis — "Effort filtering is better understood as a data-quality guarantee than as an accuracy improvement."** The evidence for filtering is strong where the claim is "these particular responses carry no information" and weak where the claim is "the resulting estimate is closer to the truth." Supporting strands: excluding rapid guesses reduces distortion but raises standard errors (Wise & Kingsbury, 2016) and risks non-convergence at higher rates (Rios & Deng, 2025); it recovers about a third of the distortion (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021); the meta-analytic average distortion is 0.13 SD (Rios, Deng & Ihlenfeldt, 2022); deletion can overcorrect by 0.42 SD (Rios, Guo, Mao & Liu, 2017). *What would falsify it:* a study showing filtering improves criterion-related validity or classification accuracy by more than it inflates uncertainty, in a design where the criterion is measured independently of engagement. The author should check whether the individual-level result of 0.15 SD bias and seven points of misclassification (Rios & Soland, 2021, *Applied Psychological Measurement*) already constitutes such a demonstration.

2. **Starter hypothesis — "Because response-time detection is definitionally restricted to fast disengagement, the residual it leaves behind is not noise but a second, separately estimable dimension."** Three independent lines converge: retest analysis attributes unexplained distortion to non-rapid, less-than-fully-engaged responses (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021); persistence modelled from declining solution probability across randomised positions explains disengagement beyond response times and correlates with self-reported effort decline (Nagy, Ulitzsch & Lindner, 2023); slow responders cluster into distinct interaction-pattern subtypes rather than forming one group (Jerez, Mazzullo & Bulut, 2026). *What would falsify it:* evidence that the residual is unsystematic, or that persistence estimates are simply ability or speed under another name. The author should also confirm whether persistence has ever been shown to improve an ability estimate as opposed to explaining variance in the response process.

3. **Starter hypothesis — "Cleaning and fairness are in direct tension, and the literature has not chosen between them."** Differential engagement both distorts group comparisons and, when corrected by deletion, produces group-differential missingness. Supporting strands: subgroup differences in rapid-guessing rates by sex and race (Soland, 2018; Wise, Ma, Kingsbury & Hauser, 2010) and by administration mode (Kröhne, Deribo & Goldhammer, 2020); spurious differential item functioning from differential guessing alone (DeMars & Wise, 2010); overcorrection when disengagement tracks ability (Rios, Guo, Mao & Liu, 2017). *What would falsify it:* a filtering or model-based approach demonstrated to reduce spurious bias signals without introducing ability-correlated missingness. The author should treat the divergence between Soland's mostly-null empirical gap result and Rios's non-trivial simulated bias as the specific question to resolve, since these are different designs answering slightly different questions.

4. **Starter hypothesis — "Disengagement is substantially a design outcome, which makes 'filter it out' a second-best response to it."** Rapid guessing is predicted by item text volume, item position, and elapsed testing time (Wise, Pastor & Kong, 2009; Lindner, Lüdtke & Nagy, 2019), by time of day (Wise, Ma, Kingsbury & Hauser, 2010), and by whether administration is proctored (Kröhne, Deribo & Goldhammer, 2020). If the signal is largely produced by measurable features of the encounter, prevention is available where correction is only partial. *What would falsify it:* the meta-analytic finding that interventions modifying assessment design had negligible impact on effort and performance, while external incentives had the largest effect (Rios, 2021) — which points the other way. The author needs to reconcile "design predicts disengagement" with "design changes did not reduce it," and should check whether the design interventions in that meta-analysis actually manipulated the features shown to be predictive.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **The one-third recovery figure has a single source.** The retest-based estimate of how much distortion effort-moderated scoring recovers (Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021) appears not to have been independently replicated, and it was produced by researchers affiliated with the provider whose operational method it evaluates. Everything downstream that treats response-time filtering as partial rather than sufficient rests on this one study.
- **No evidence located on whether filtering improves estimates of learning rate or growth.** All identified evidence concerns single-occasion ability or proficiency estimates, group means, item parameters, and classification accuracy. Whether removing disengaged responses improves the estimation of change across occasions — where a disengaged episode at one time point could distort a slope in either direction — was not found addressed in the sources reviewed.
- **Young children are largely absent.** The youngest group with published rapid-guessing evidence in these sources is grade 3, and the finding is that disengagement is *less* common there than in later grades (Svetina Valdivia et al., 2023; Wise, Ma, Kingsbury & Hauser, 2010). No evidence was located on rapid-guessing threshold validity below grade 3, on pre-readers, or on individually administered or short-session assessment of young children. Whether response-time thresholds derived from older test takers are even interpretable for young children is an open question, not a settled one.
- **The age gradient has no established mechanism.** The increase in rapid guessing across grade with a jump in early adolescence is documented, but the explanation offered in the source is explicitly a common belief about adolescent compliance rather than a tested account. Competing explanations — cumulative testing experience, changing perceptions of stakes, reading load of grade-appropriate items — were not distinguished in the sources reviewed.
- **Answer changes and navigation patterns are unsupported here.** Despite being commonly proposed as engagement indicators, no study was located in this search establishing that answer-change behaviour or within-test navigation adds incremental validity to an ability estimate. Item-level abandonment and minimal-action patterns are established as observable and as characteristic of disengagement (Şahin & Colvin, 2020) but not as validated ability-estimate improvements. Treat these as uninvestigated rather than as investigated and null.
- **Threshold conventions rest on convenience.** The RTE 0.90 cut-off for classifying a person as unmotivated originated in 2005 and propagated through applied research by adoption (Rios & Deng, 2021). No source located re-derives it against an external criterion.
- **Un-verified magnitudes, explicitly flagged.** Two numbers in this document could not be confirmed against the primary text: the specific mean-performance and criterion-correlation changes from motivation filtering in the founding RTE study (reported in a table that did not extract reliably), and the 61% / 0.34 SD / 0.38 SD proctor-notification magnitudes, which are taken from a later secondary account by one of the original authors rather than from the primary article. Both are marked at point of use.
- **Publication and grey-literature exposure.** Nearly half the studies in the threshold-comparison meta-analysis were unrefereed (Rios & Deng, 2021), and the collapse of the incentive meta-analysis under publication-bias correction (retraction notice, 2025) demonstrates that this literature's pooled estimates have proven fragile at least once.
- **Cross-cultural and language-minority evidence is thin in what was reviewed.** One study reports rapid guessing being consistently very low across demographic subpopulations (Setzer et al., 2013) and another reports rates differing across student groups (Kröhne, Deribo & Goldhammer, 2020), without a synthesis reconciling them for language-minority test takers specifically.

### Category 3: Process data, log files, and interactive-task telemetry

*General idea: Interactive assessment platforms emit a dense stream of behavioural exhaust — every click, pause, revision, navigation, and tool invocation — and a large literature argues that this stream reveals how a person arrived at an answer, not merely whether they got it right. The evidence, examined closely, supports a much narrower claim than the enthusiasm suggests: a small number of theoretically pre-specified, well-behaved indicators (most robustly, response-time-based disengagement detection) demonstrably improve score validity, while the large majority of "found" process features are task-specific, weakly reliable, rarely replicated, and — in the handful of studies that actually run the test — do not survive a controlled incremental-validity comparison against outcome scores plus an existing ability measure.*

      - The same paper notes that log data is "especially vulnerable to threats to validity because of its partiality, as it only captures log events from respondent interactions with the keyboard and mouse," and specifically does not capture idle time away from keyboard, reading behaviour between clicks, or off-screen activity such as use of pen and paper. *(Maddox, 2023, DOI 10.1787/5d9009ff-en.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** "Process data" is not a thing you collect; it is a thing you *construct* from a raw event stream by imposing a theory about which events matter and how they aggregate. The event stream itself is also systematically incomplete — it sees keyboard and mouse, and is blind to reading, thinking, scratch paper, and everything happening away from the input device. The most useful taxonomy in the literature is not about *what* is logged but about *how strongly the process is allowed to enter the inference*: from ignored, to auxiliary, to essential alongside the outcome, to being the outcome. Almost all of the interesting validity questions live at the boundary between "auxiliary" and "essential," and the field's own senior figures characterise its accumulated work as theory-poor and uncoordinated.
    - **Link to source:** [Process data in NAEP](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998618784700); [Log data framework](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41237-018-0063-y); [Quixotic quest](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-020-00092-z); [OECD process data](https://doi.org/10.1787/5d9009ff-en)

- **Subcategory 3.2: The strongest positive evidence — response-time indicators of disengagement, which demonstrably improve score validity**
  - **Source:** Wise & Kong (2005); Wise & DeMars (2006); Wise (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A response-time-based index (the proportion of test items on which an examinee exhibited "solution behaviour" rather than a rapid guess) computed on an 80-item university assessment showed very high internal consistency (α = .97). *(Wise & Kong, 2005, *Applied Measurement in Education* 18(2):163–183, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - The same index showed convergent validity via significant correlations with self-reported effort (r = .25) and with a person-fit statistic (r = −.42), and discriminant validity via near-zero correlations with a college-admissions test score. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - In the same study, filtering out examinees flagged as low-effort by the response-time index successively increased the convergent-validity correlation between test scores and an external criterion, while the criterion scores themselves remained virtually unchanged — the pattern expected if filtering removes noise rather than shifting the sample. Filtering by the response-time index produced modestly *more* favourable results than filtering by self-reported effort while deleting fewer examinees. *(Wise & Kong, 2005, DOI 10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2.)* `T2`
      - An IRT model that incorporates item response time into both proficiency estimation and item-parameter estimation ("effort-moderated" model) outperformed a standard three-parameter logistic model in a real-data study and a simulation study on four criteria: better model fit, more accurate item-parameter estimates, more accurate test-information estimates, and proficiency estimates with higher convergent validity against external variables. *(Wise & DeMars, 2006, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 43(1):19–38, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x.)* `T2`
      - A later review article treats rapid-guessing behaviour as an identifiable, interpretable, and consequential phenomenon in its own right, addressing its identification, interpretation, and implications for score use. *(Wise, 2017, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 36(4):52–61, DOI 10.1111/emip.12165.)* `T2`
      - Subsequent simulation work on effort-moderated IRT reports that rapid-guessing produces *differential* bias in ability estimates — greater bias for lower-scoring examinees than higher-scoring ones — and frames this differential bias explicitly as a fairness concern, not merely an accuracy concern. *(Analysis of two-step and multidimensional effort-moderated IRT variants, 2024, open-access methodological article; specific journal/volume not independently verified here.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The single clearest incremental-validity win in this entire literature is also the least glamorous: use response times to detect that a person was not really trying, and either down-weight or exclude those responses. This clears the bar the way the rest of the field does not — it improves model fit, improves parameter recovery, and raises the correlation between the score and an *external* criterion, which is the actual test of added validity rather than added correlation. It is worth noticing exactly why it works. The indicator is theoretically pre-specified (a threshold below which a response cannot reflect solution behaviour), it targets a *threat* to the outcome score rather than trying to measure a new construct, and its claim is negative and modest: "this response is uninformative." Almost every failed process-data programme differs from this one by attempting something far more ambitious.
    - **Link to source:** [Response time effort](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1802_2); [Effort-moderated IRT](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2006.00002.x); [Rapid guessing](https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12165)

- **Subcategory 3.3: Process indicators in interactive problem-solving tasks — strong correlations, and what happens when incremental validity is actually tested**
  - **Source:** Greiff, Wüstenberg & Avvisati (2015); Goldhammer et al. (2014); Stadler, Hofer & Greiff (2020); Teig, Scherer & Kjærnsli (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Log files from a single interactive problem-solving item in a large international assessment (N = 16,219 students, 44 countries/economies) were analysed for use of an optimal exploration strategy (vary-one-thing-at-a-time, VOTAT). The authors report that VOTAT application was strongly related both to performance on that same item and to overall problem-solving proficiency, at the individual and country level, and that distinct levels of non-mastery were identifiable — including students who applied VOTAT yet still failed the item. *(Greiff, Wüstenberg & Avvisati, 2015, *Computers & Education* 91:92–105, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2015.10.018.)* `T2` — *the specific coefficient magnitudes reported in the paper's tables could not be verified from the accessible abstract/landing pages; the relationship is therefore stated qualitatively here.*
      - In a national field trial of an adult skills assessment (N = 1,020, ages 16–65), linear mixed models showed that the time-on-task effect was *positive* in problem solving (which required controlled processing) and increased with task difficulty, but *negative* in reading (which required more routine processing) and more negative the easier the task. In problem solving the positive time-on-task effect *decreased* with increasing skill; in reading the negative time-on-task effect *increased* with increasing skill. The authors conclude time on task "has no uniform interpretation but is a function of task difficulty and individual skill." *(Goldhammer, Naumann, Stelter, Tóth, Rölke & Klieme, 2014, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(3):608–626, DOI 10.1037/a0034716.)* `T2`
      - In a sample of students who all achieved the *maximum* score on five complex problem-solving tasks (i.e. zero variance in the outcome score), significant variance remained in time-on-task (s² = 260.09, p = .005) and in number of interactions (s² = 0.381, p < .001). *(Stadler, Hofer & Greiff, 2020, *Computers in Human Behavior* 111:106442, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442.)* `T2`
      - In the same study, both process indicators significantly predicted GPA in a structural equation model — time-on-task β = 0.17, p = .015; number of interactions β = −0.17, p < .001. When an intelligence measure was added as a predictor of GPA (β = 0.55, p < .001), both process effects were reduced to negligible, non-significant levels: time-on-task β = −0.09, p = .053; number of interactions β = 0.09, p = .100. *(Stadler, Hofer & Greiff, 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442.)* `T2`
      - Note on the same result: the two process coefficients did not merely shrink toward zero when intelligence was added — both *reversed sign* (+0.17 → −0.09; −0.17 → +0.09). *(Derived directly from the coefficients reported in Stadler, Hofer & Greiff, 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442.)* `T2`
      - Log-file data from simulated scientific inquiry tasks in a large international assessment has been used to identify distinct latent profiles of inquiry performance that go beyond response correctness. *(Teig, Scherer & Kjærnsli, 2020, *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* 57(9):1400–1429, DOI 10.1002/tea.21657.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This subcategory contains the field's flagship demonstrations, and they establish two things cleanly and one thing not at all. Cleanly established: (a) behaviour during an interactive task is not redundant with the score — people with identical scores demonstrably behave differently, and those behavioural differences are not random; (b) the *meaning* of even the simplest process feature is not fixed — time on task flips sign depending on whether the task demands controlled or routine processing, and its slope depends on the examinee's own skill, so "spent longer" is not interpretable without knowing the task and the person. Not established: that these indicators add validity. The one study here that ran the decisive test — regressing an external criterion on process indicators with and without an established ability measure in the model — found the process effects collapsed to non-significance once ability entered. The sign reversal is a further warning: coefficients that flip when a correlated predictor enters are behaving like suppressors or unstable estimates, not like a stable independent signal. The authors nonetheless summarise their result as supporting process data "over and above test scores," which is defensible only against the *bare score*, not against the score plus an ability measure. That gap between what was shown and what was claimed is the characteristic move of this literature.
    - **Link to source:** [PISA log-file showcase](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2015.10.018); [Time on task](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034716); [First among equals](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2020.106442); [Inquiry log profiles](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.21657)

- **Subcategory 3.4: Evidence-Centered Design — why the claim must be built before the data is mined**
  - **Source:** Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond (2003); Mislevy & Haertel (2006); Goldhammer, Hahnel, Kroehne & Zehner (2021)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The foundational statement of evidence-centered design frames assessment as an evidentiary argument: "we observe what students say, do, or make in a few particular circumstances and attempt to infer what they know, can do, or have accomplished more generally," connected by "a web of inference." The framework makes explicit the interrelations among substantive arguments, assessment designs, and operational processes, and is organised around linked student, evidence, and task models. *(Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond, 2003, *Measurement: Interdisciplinary Research and Perspectives* 1(1):3–62, DOI 10.1207/s15366359mea0101_02.)* `T2` `[COI: two of three authors were employed by a commercial assessment organisation that funded the design programme]`
      - The stated motivation for the framework was the need to develop assessments incorporating "purposes, technologies, and psychological perspectives that are not well served by familiar forms of assessments"; a companion introduction from the same programme states the framework spans "a broad range of assessment types, from familiar standardized tests and classroom quizzes, to coached practice systems and simulation-based assessments, to portfolios and student-tutor interaction." *(Mislevy, Steinberg & Almond, 2003, DOI 10.1207/s15366359mea0101_02; Mislevy, Almond & Lukas, 2003, *ETS Research Report Series* 2003(1), DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.2003.tb01908.x.)* `T3` `[COI: as above]`
      - A subsequent treatment aimed at practitioners sets out implications of evidence-centered design for educational testing generally. *(Mislevy & Haertel, 2006, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 25(4):6–20, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00075.x.)* `T2`
      - A validity-focused paper argues process indicators should move "from byproduct to design factor," and that the *activity spaces within items need to be designed* so that observed behaviours constitute a representative sample of the universe of possible observations — otherwise the generalisation inference cannot be justified. *(Goldhammer, Hahnel, Kroehne & Zehner, 2021, *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9(1), Article 20, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5.)* `T2`
      - The same paper states that "there is a lack of theory or process models relating behavioral low-level features to attributes of the work process through evidence identification and accumulation," and that such comprehensive theories "may not be available for complex ability/competence domains encompassing various cognitive processes." It therefore concludes that both exploratory theory-building and data-driven evidence identification are needed, and that a *necessary condition* for cumulative progress is that indicator construction be transparent — that how derived actions and states link to log events be made explicit — and reproducible across studies. *(Goldhammer et al., 2021, DOI 10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Evidence-centered design is the answer to a question most process-data work never asks: what claim am I entitled to make, and what would count as evidence for it? Its discipline is that you specify the target competency, the observable features that constitute evidence about it, and the tasks engineered to elicit those observables — *in that order, before data exists*. This matters more for process data than for anything else, because a log file supports an effectively unbounded number of post-hoc features, so a post-hoc "finding" carries almost no evidential weight. Note also the honest self-assessment from within the design-oriented camp: the theories needed to link low-level behavioural features to work-process attributes largely do not exist for complex competencies. That admission cuts both ways — it justifies exploratory work, but it also means that a design-first framework is currently being applied without the substantive theory it presupposes, and its stated minimum condition (transparent, reproducible indicator construction) is a *precondition* for evidence, not evidence itself.
    - **Link to source:** [Structure of educational assessments](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15366359mea0101_02); [ECD implications](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00075.x); [Byproduct to design factor](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5)

- **Subcategory 3.5: The measurement critique — task-specificity, non-replication, overfitting, and explicit null incremental-validity results**
  - **Source:** Stadler, Brandl & Greiff (2023); Goldhammer et al. (2021); Zhang & Deane (2015); Provasnik (2021); AERA, APA & NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - Reviewing two decades of interactive tasks in large-scale assessments, the authors state: "the lack of generalizability of findings and a missing relation between data and theory strongly limit the replicability of research on sequence data from interactive tasks in educational large-scale assessments, and thus, its scientific value." *(Stadler, Brandl & Greiff, 2023, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 39(6):1852–1859, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12847.)* `T2`
      - The same paper distinguishes low-level features (time-on-task, number of interactions), which "can be more idiosyncratic and may not convey the same meaning across different tasks," from higher-level, theoretically coded features, which can support conceptual replication across differing items. *(Stadler, Brandl & Greiff, 2023, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12847.)* `T2`
      - The same paper characterises the existing corpus as "scarce, often restricted to single or a few selected items with little common theoretical underpinning and minimal attempts at replicating findings." *(Stadler, Brandl & Greiff, 2023, DOI 10.1111/jcal.12847.)* `T2`
      - A study of keystroke-logged writing-process features tested their incremental value over automatically extracted product (final-text) features for predicting human ratings. Adding a writing-fluency process factor produced a statistically significant but very small improvement in adjusted R², from .709 to .714, F(1, 500) = 7.84, p < .001. **None** of the other process factors had statistically significant incremental value, and **on cross-validation in a second sample, none of the factors added incrementally to the product features at a statistically significant level.** *(Zhang & Deane, 2015, ETS Research Report RR-15-27, *ETS Research Report Series* 2015(2):1–12, DOI 10.1002/ets2.12075.)* `T3` `[COI: both authors employed by a commercial assessment organisation; report published in that organisation's own series]`
      - The authors of that study attribute the null to a structural reason rather than a technical one: human raters "have no knowledge of the writing process leading to the final text," and the product features already measure the text quality specified in the scoring rubric. *(Zhang & Deane, 2015, DOI 10.1002/ets2.12075.)* `T3`
      - The 2014 professional testing standards list five sources of validity evidence, one of which is evidence based on response processes, defined as "evidence concerning the fit between the construct and the detailed nature of the performance or response actually engaged in by test takers," and note that "documentation of other aspects of performance, like eye movements or response times, may also be relevant to some constructs." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Chapter 1, Washington, DC: AERA.)* `T1`
      - The same standards state directly: "there are many other cases where claims about response processes are not part of the validity argument. In some cases, multiple response processes are available for solving the problems of interest, and the construct of interest is only concerned with whether the problem was solved correctly." *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Chapter 1.)* `T1`
      - The same standards also state that "each type of evidence … is not required in all settings. Rather, support is needed for each proposition that underlies a proposed test interpretation for a specified use" (p. 14). *(AERA, APA & NCME, 2014, Chapter 1.)* `T1`
      - A commissioned policy paper notes that because response processes are multi-dimensional, "any single method used for the collection of process data is vulnerable to threats to validity associated with methodological bias and partiality," and recommends triangulation with eye tracking, think-aloud, and video studies to validate interpretive use of log data. *(Maddox, 2023, OECD Education Working Papers No. 286, DOI 10.1787/5d9009ff-en.)* `T3`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The critique has four distinct legs and they compound. **Task-specificity:** low-level features do not mean the same thing on a different item, so a coefficient estimated on one task is not a property of the person. **Non-replication:** the literature is built on single items, small samples, and almost no replication attempts, by the assessment of researchers who work in it. **Overfitting:** a log file supports unbounded feature construction, so anything can be found; the writing-process study is the cleanest demonstration — a feature that reached significance in one sample failed to replicate incrementally in a second from the same design. **The standards do not require it:** the governing professional standards explicitly contemplate that when multiple response processes can produce a correct answer and the construct is about correctness, response-process evidence is simply not part of the validity argument. That last point is the one most often skipped. The burden of proof runs the other way from how it is usually assumed: process data is not presumptively informative and awaiting refutation; it is presumptively irrelevant to the score's validity argument unless a specific proposition in that argument requires it.
    - **Link to source:** [20 years of interactive tasks](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12847); [Writing process incremental value](https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12075); [Byproduct to design factor](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5); [OECD process data](https://doi.org/10.1787/5d9009ff-en)

- **Subcategory 3.6: Game-based versus gamified, and what gameplay telemetry actually measures**
  - **Source:** Landers & Sanchez (2022); Landers, Armstrong, Collmus, Mujcic & Blaik (2022); Auer, Mersy, Marin, Blaik & Landers (2022); Bipp, Wee, Walczok & Hansal (2024); Gomez, Ruipérez-Valiente & García Clemente (2023); Ramos-Villagrasa, Fernández-del-Río & Castro (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A definitional review distinguishes three things routinely conflated: *game-based assessment* is an assessment **method** in which the person participates in a core gameplay loop while trait information is inferred; *gameful design* is a **design strategy** in which assessment professionals use game mechanics to guide decisions when building a new assessment; *gamification* is a **redesign strategy** in which game mechanics are added to an assessment that already exists. Only the first is an assessment method; the other two are (re)design strategies. *(Landers & Sanchez, 2022, *International Journal of Selection and Assessment* 30(1):1–13, DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12376.)* `T2` `[COI: first author has commercial and advisory relationships with game-based assessment vendors, disclosed in related work]`
      - A theory-driven game-based assessment of general cognitive ability showed strong convergence with a latent general-intelligence factor (β = .97) in an academic sample (N = 633), and predicted GPA (r = .16) and, in a small organisational sample (N = 49), supervisory ratings of job performance (r = .29). *(Landers, Armstrong, Collmus, Mujcic & Blaik, 2022, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 107(10):1655–1677, DOI 10.1037/apl0000954.)* `T2` `[COI: study funded by a commercial game-based assessment vendor; the lead author became a compensated affiliate of that vendor; two co-authors were employed by it]`
      - **Directional null in the same study:** unit-weighted composites of the *traditional* test battery showed incremental prediction of GPA beyond the game-based battery, **but not the reverse**. *(Landers et al., 2022, DOI 10.1037/apl0000954.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - The same study found similar adverse impact for the traditional battery and the game-based assessment, and no differential prediction of criteria; reactions were more positive across all measures for the game-based assessment. *(Landers et al., 2022, DOI 10.1037/apl0000954.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - A secondary analysis engineered 65 features from the raw trace data of that same game-based assessment (N = 621) — counts of game objects interacted with, time spent doing so, and mouse-movement data across intervals — and applied a series of machine-learning models. The authors report "promising evidence for trace data modeling of cognitive ability, including incremental contribution to the prediction of a criterion grade point average (GPA), but less promising evidence for trace data modeling of conscientiousness." *(Auer, Mersy, Marin, Blaik & Landers, 2022, *International Journal of Selection and Assessment* 30(1):82–102, DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12363.)* `T2` `[COI: one co-author employed by the commercial vendor that produced the assessment]`
      - The same author team later characterised that result more soberly in a methodological paper: the trace-data models "were unable to predict general cognitive ability at a sufficiently high level to support measurement equivalence (R² = .434)," though they predicted the criterion slightly better than the cognitive-ability measure did — which the authors note could indicate either improved measurement *or* the reflection of additional constructs beyond cognitive ability. *(Landers, Auer, Mersy, Marin & Blaik, 2022, *International Journal of Testing* 22(3–4):243–263, DOI 10.1080/15305058.2022.2134394.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - The same methodological paper states that "trace data generally only provide weak signals in true score variance of target constructs from a vast set of behaviors," and lists error sources that must be isolated: working-memory load from screen size, lag from unreliable internet, erratic mouse hardware, low clicking precision from inexperience with computers and mice, and browser incompatibility. *(Landers et al., 2022, DOI 10.1080/15305058.2022.2134394.)* `T2`
      - A three-level meta-analysis of 52 samples from 44 papers (> 6,100 adult participants) found an overall observed correlation of r = 0.30, 95% CI [0.26, 0.34] between game-related assessment indicators and traditional cognitive-ability measures (corrected r = 0.45), with substantial heterogeneity, Q_E(806) = 3871.42, p < .001. *(Bipp, Wee, Walczok & Hansal, 2024, *Journal of Intelligence* 12(12):129, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - **The most damaging finding in the same meta-analysis:** game-related assessments *intended* to assess cognitive ability did **not** show stronger relationships with traditional cognitive-ability measures than game-related assessments **not** designed to measure cognitive ability. *(Bipp et al., 2024, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - The same meta-analysis reports that its reliability corrections produced correlations above 1.0 in seven cases — because the underlying studies reported reliabilities as low as 0.16–0.20 — which the authors describe as "raising further questions about how to adequately assess reliability in GRAs." *(Bipp et al., 2024, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - The same meta-analysis states plainly: "it remains unclear whether GRAs provide incremental predictive value for academic and work outcomes beyond traditional selection instruments," and notes that only a few studies have examined predictive validity for grades or work performance at all. *(Bipp et al., 2024, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence12120129.)* `T1`
      - The first systematic literature review of empirical game-based assessment studies (65 papers) concludes that the field's current limitations indicate future research "would benefit from the use of bigger data samples and more specialized algorithms," and identifies replication and validation problems as open challenges. *(Gomez, Ruipérez-Valiente & García Clemente, 2023, *IEEE Transactions on Learning Technologies* 16(4):500–515, DOI 10.1109/TLT.2022.3226661.)* `T1`
      - A PRISMA systematic review of 34 articles on game-related assessment in personnel selection concludes that such assessments "can be used" but that "the supposed advantages of GRA over conventional tests are fewer than imagined," and that claims of comparable accuracy with better reactions and reduced adverse impact and faking "still lack scientific support." *(Ramos-Villagrasa, Fernández-del-Río & Castro, 2022, *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:952002, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952002.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Take the strongest single case in this literature — a game-based assessment built theory-first, published in a top journal, converging at β = .97 with latent g. Even there, when the decisive comparison is run, the *traditional* battery adds incrementally over the game, and the game does not add over the traditional battery. Then widen to the meta-analytic view and the picture gets worse: the average observed correlation with conventional cognitive measures is r ≈ .30, heterogeneity is enormous, some underlying reliabilities are so low (0.16–0.20) that correction formulas break, and — the finding that should stop the conversation — games *designed* to measure cognitive ability correlate with cognitive tests no better than games that were never designed to measure anything. That last result is very hard to reconcile with a claim that gameplay telemetry is measuring the intended trait; it is much more consistent with a broad, weakly construct-specific "does well at digital tasks" factor. Note too the sequencing of the trace-data claim: presented as "promising evidence … including incremental contribution" in the applied journal, and as "unable to predict … at a sufficiently high level to support measurement equivalence" by the same authors in the methods journal. Both are true. Only the second is a validity statement.

- **Subcategory 3.7: Stealth assessment — the claim and the actual evidence base, characterised honestly**
  - **Source:** Shute, Wang, Greiff, Zhao & Moore (2016)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The canonical validation study of stealth assessment for problem solving embedded assessment in a lightly modified commercial video game, built a four-facet problem-solving competency model (analysing givens and constraints; planning a solution pathway; using tools effectively; monitoring and evaluating progress), identified in-game indicators for each facet, and implemented the evidence model as Bayesian networks — a unique network per game level, 42 levels in total. The approach is explicitly grounded in evidence-centered design. *(Shute, Wang, Greiff, Zhao & Moore, 2016, *Computers in Human Behavior* 63:106–117, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2` `[COI: authors acknowledge support from a game-based learning organisation and worked with a commercially published entertainment game]`

      - The in-game overall problem-solving estimate correlated significantly with both external measures: r = 0.40, p < .01 with a matrix-reasoning test, and r = 0.41, p < .01 with an external complex-problem-solving measure. *(Shute et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2016.05.047.)* `T2`

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Authors' conclusions frequently outrun their own numbers.** The clearest instance: a study finds process indicators predict an external criterion, then finds those effects drop to non-significance (and reverse sign) once an established ability measure enters the model, and concludes the study "supports the assumption of test-taking behavior actually representing differences in CPS ability — over and above test scores." That conclusion holds against a bare score and fails against a score-plus-ability baseline. The same pattern appears in trace-data work, where the same author team wrote "promising evidence … including incremental contribution" in an applied journal and "unable to predict … at a sufficiently high level to support measurement equivalence" in a methods journal.

2. **The design-first camp and the empirical record contradict each other about what is currently possible.** Evidence-centered design says the claim, the observables, and the tasks must be specified in advance. But the same design-oriented authors state that the theories linking low-level behavioural features to work-process attributes largely do not exist for complex competencies. So the methodology that would make process data interpretable presupposes theory that the field acknowledges it lacks — which means most current work is either doing post-hoc mining under a design-first banner, or is honestly exploratory and should not be cited as validity evidence.

3. **Enthusiasm scales with distance from the incremental-validity test.** Where the test is not run, the literature reports "significant relations," "promising evidence," and "great potential." In the small set of studies where it *is* run — writing process features against product features, game-based batteries against traditional batteries, process indicators against ability measures — the result is null, marginal, or directionally against the process data. This is not a coincidence of which studies happened to be run; it is what you would expect if process features are largely reflections of the same underlying ability that the outcome score already captures.

4. **The professional standards are more permissive of correctness-only measurement than the field's discourse admits.** The governing standards explicitly contemplate that when multiple response processes can produce a correct answer and the construct concerns correctness, response-process evidence is *not part of the validity argument*. Much process-data advocacy proceeds as if collecting process evidence were a validity obligation. It is a conditional obligation, triggered only by specific propositions in a specific interpretive argument.

5. **"Game-based" and "gamified" are formally distinguished in the definitional literature and routinely conflated in the empirical literature.** The meta-analytic corpus pools purpose-built cognitive game-based assessments with commercial entertainment games used opportunistically — and then finds no difference between them in relation to cognitive tests. Either the distinction does not matter empirically (which would undermine the design-theory case) or the pooling is masking real heterogeneity (which would undermine the meta-analytic estimate). Both readings are damaging.

6. **Reliability is treated as a solved problem in advocacy and as an open one in the methods.** A meta-analysis had to discard seven reliability-corrected estimates because they exceeded 1.0, driven by underlying reliabilities as low as .16–.20. A widely cited stealth-assessment validation reports α = .76 for a four-facet model in which the overall score correlates .98 with one facet. There is no settled answer to what "reliability" even means for a score built from performance across heterogeneous mini-games or game levels.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of these is a **starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own**, not a finished insight. They are cross-source connections that the sources do not themselves state.*

- **Seed A — "The successful process indicators are the ones making negative claims."** *Starter hypothesis.* The one process feature with a clean, replicated incremental-validity record (response-time-based disengagement detection) differs from the failures on a specific dimension: its claim is that a response is *uninformative*, not that a behaviour *measures something*. Detecting "this observation should not count" requires only a threshold and a theory of what a non-attempt looks like; measuring "this behaviour indicates planning ability" requires a validated construct-to-behaviour mapping that the field concedes does not exist. If this holds, the actionable rule is that process data earns its place first as a **filter on the evidence** and only much later as **evidence itself** — and a programme that skips the first use to reach for the second is inverting the difficulty ordering. The author should test this against counter-examples: are there any well-replicated *positive* process indicators, and if so what do they share?

- **Seed B — "Process features are mostly a noisy re-measurement of the same construct, and 'incremental' claims usually mean 'incremental over an artificially impoverished baseline.'"** *Starter hypothesis.* Three independent results point the same way: process effects on an external criterion vanish once ability enters the model; the traditional battery adds over the game-based battery but not vice versa; and games designed to measure cognitive ability correlate with cognitive tests no better than games never designed to measure anything. A single explanation covers all three — process features load on the same general factor the outcome score already loads on, plus construct-irrelevant medium-familiarity variance. If so, the diagnostic question for any incremental-validity claim is not "incremental over what?" but "incremental over the *best available conventional alternative*, in a held-out sample." The author should look for a documented case where a process feature survives that specific test, and treat its absence as informative.

- **Seed C — "Construct-irrelevant variance in telemetry is worse than construct-irrelevant variance in scores, because it corrupts predictor and criterion in opposite directions."** *Starter hypothesis.* Device-comparability research is reassuring about *scores* and largely silent about *process indicators*, which are far more sensitive to input latency, pointer precision, and screen real estate than correctness is. Meanwhile, medium familiarity has been found to raise game-based assessment performance while *lowering* the academic criterion. Combined, this suggests telemetry-based measurement may carry a fairness liability that score-based measurement does not, and that the standard device-comparability evidence base does not license extending "scores are comparable across devices" to "process features are comparable across devices." The author should check whether any published study has directly tested measurement invariance of *process indicators* (not scores) across device or input modality — and treat a null result on that search as a substantive finding.

- **Seed D — "The unit of replication in this field is the item, and that is why nothing replicates."** *Starter hypothesis.* Low-level features are acknowledged not to carry the same meaning across tasks; the corpus is built on single items; and the proposed fix — abstracting to higher-level, theory-coded features — currently lacks the theory it requires. This suggests that the field's replication crisis is not primarily a statistical-power or publication-bias problem but a **construct-definition** problem: there is no task-independent definition of the behaviour, so there is nothing for a second study to replicate. If true, the precondition for any process-data programme is not more data or better algorithms but an explicit, task-independent operational definition of the target behaviour written before any task exists — and any programme that cannot state one should expect its features not to transfer. The author should stress-test this by asking whether the response-time-effort case (Seed A) succeeded precisely because "rapid guess" *is* a task-independent definition.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **No verified numeric effect sizes for the flagship international log-file study.** The relationship between the vary-one-thing-at-a-time strategy indicator and problem-solving proficiency is reported qualitatively above because the paper's coefficient tables could not be reached from accessible abstract or repository pages. There is also a structural concern that could not be checked without the full text: the study relates strategy use on one item to *overall* proficiency, and that item plausibly contributes to the proficiency estimate — a part-whole dependency that would inflate the association. This needs the full text to resolve and should not be cited quantitatively until it is.
- **Very few studies run the decisive comparison at all.** Only a handful of the sources above test process features against an external criterion with a conventional measure already in the model. The literature is therefore not "mixed" on incremental validity — it is mostly *silent*, with a small number of results clustering on the null side. Absence of contrary evidence here reflects absence of testing, not confirmation.
- **No evidence located on measurement invariance of process indicators across device or input modality.** Device-comparability research addresses scores. Whether timing, click-count, and trajectory features are invariant across touch versus mouse, screen sizes, or connection quality appears not to have been directly tested at scale. This is a first-order gap given that these are exactly the features telemetry models consume.
- **No evidence located on socioeconomic gradients in telemetry features specifically.** Prior familiarity with the interaction medium is documented as affecting performance; its correlation with socioeconomic access to technology is a reasonable inference but was not verified against a study in this search.
- **Reliability of process-derived scores is unresolved as a methodological matter.** Internal consistency across heterogeneous mini-games or game levels, test–retest stability under practice effects, and generalisability-theory decompositions of process indicators are all identified as open by the meta-analytic and methodological sources, with no settled standard.
- **The literature on learning *rate* from process data was not located.** Everything above concerns estimating ability or proficiency at a point in time. Whether process indicators improve estimation of *change* or *learning rate* — a distinct and harder inferential target — did not surface in this search and should be treated as an open question rather than assumed to follow.
- **Publication bias is plausible and only partly addressed.** The one meta-analysis here identified just five unpublished studies and received no usable data from twenty contacted authors; it also excluded commercially held validation data on intellectual-property grounds. The direction of that second exclusion is unknown: vendor data could be better (proprietary refinement) or worse (unfavourable results never released).
- **Tier-3 sources are load-bearing in two places.** The strongest single negative incremental-validity result (writing process features failing to cross-validate) comes from a commercial assessment organisation's own report series, and the policy-level framing comes from a commissioned working paper by an author with a commercial affiliation. Both are used above because their claims are specific and self-critical, but neither has been through independent peer review.

### Category 4: Information in the wrong answer — distractor-level and polytomous scoring

*General idea: A half-century of psychometric work establishes that which wrong option a person selects is not noise — it carries recoverable information about the latent trait, and models exist to extract it. But the measured size of that gain in ability estimation is small (on the order of two to three points of reliability in reasoning tests), it is concentrated at the low end of the ability range, it depends on distractors that were deliberately built to be diagnostic, and it costs substantially more calibration data. The stronger claim — that error patterns support reliable *profile* or *per-skill* reporting — is where the evidence turns negative, and there is a precise, well-established statistical criterion for deciding when a subscore earns the right to be reported at all.*

---

- **Subcategory 4.1: The founding claim — unordered response categories carry trait information**
  - **Source:** Bock (1972); Thissen (1976); Thissen & Steinberg (1984); Thissen, Steinberg & Fitzpatrick (1989)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A multivariate logistic latent-trait model was proposed for items scored in two or more nominal (unordered) categories; it estimates two item parameters for *each* response alternative of a multiple-choice item and provides "recovery of information from 'wrong' responses when estimating latent ability." *(Bock, 1972, *Psychometrika* 37(1):29–51, DOI 10.1007/BF02291411.)* `T2`
      - In that paper's application to twenty vocabulary items, comparing item and test information curves under multiple-category versus dichotomous scoring, "multiple scoring proves substantially more precise for subjects of less than median ability, and about equally precise for subjects above the median." *(Bock, 1972, *Psychometrika* 37(1):29–51, DOI 10.1007/BF02291411.)* `T2`

      - In that replication the reliability gains from nested logit over the matched binary model were 0.018 (95% CI [0.017, 0.021], z = 17.765) for 2PL→2PNL, 0.019 (95% CI [0.018, 0.023], z = 15.265) for 3PL→3PNL, and 0.015 (95% CI [0.011, 0.020], z = 6.669) for 4PL→4PNL; the authors report that "the gain in reliability was especially obtained at low ability levels." *(Storme et al., 2019, *Journal of Intelligence* 7(3):17, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence7030017.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - A multidimensional extension of the nested logit model was developed specifically because of the concern "that distractor selection reflects a different trait/ability than that underlying the correct response," and was applied to a university testing centre's decision about whether to incorporate distractor selection into scoring. *(Bolt, Wollack & Suh, 2012, *Psychometrika* 77(2):339–357, DOI 10.1007/s11336-012-9257-5.)* `T2`
      - **Negative finding.** An empirical-plus-simulation study comparing polytomous (nominal response and multiple-choice model) against dichotomous (3PL) characterisations of the same test found apparently superior item and test information functions from the polytomous models, then concluded on closer inspection that "these outcomes are artifactual"; two simulation studies confirmed a "structural inadequacy of the nominal response model for multiple-choice items," and found that the multiple-choice model outperformed the 3PL "only when distractor endorsement frequencies vary non-monotonically with ability, although this feature is rarely observed in empirical data sets." *(García-Pérez, 2014, *The Spanish Journal of Psychology* 17:E88, DOI 10.1017/sjp.2014.95.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the best-quantified corner of the category, and the honest headline is *real but small*. Two independent studies on matrix/series reasoning, one low-stakes and one high-stakes, one small and one large, converge on a reliability gain of roughly 0.015 to 0.03 — about two to three points on a scale already sitting in the 0.84–0.87 range. That is a genuine, statistically robust effect and it is not nothing on a short test, but it is far from the transformative gain the phrase "recovering information from wrong answers" suggests. Both studies also agree the gain lives at the low-ability end, which is where the model has wrong answers to read. Against this sit two substantial cautions: a formal argument that apparent information gains from polytomous scoring of multiple-choice items can be an artifact of model misspecification and only become real under a distractor pattern that is empirically rare; and a demonstration that distractor choice may load on a *different* dimension than the correct response, meaning distractor scoring can quietly change what the test measures rather than measuring the same thing better.
    - **Link to source:** [Storme et al. 2019 replication with measured reliability gains](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence7030017)

- **Subcategory 4.4: Diagnostic classification models — attribute-level inference from error patterns, and whether those classifications are reliable enough to act on**
  - **Source:** de la Torre (2009); Templin & Bradshaw (2013); Johnson & Sinharay (2020); Sinharay & Haberman (2009)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A cognitive diagnosis model for multiple-choice options was proposed on the explicit premise that "the dichotomization approach to the analysis of multiple-choice data ignores the potential diagnostic information that can be found in the distractors and is therefore deemed diagnostically suboptimal"; the framework requires that options be *constructed* to be cognitively diagnostic (an extended Q-matrix mapping attributes to each option, not only the key), and a simulation study evaluated the model's viability. *(de la Torre, 2009, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 33(3):163–183, DOI 10.1177/0146621608320523.)* `T2`
      - A reliability measure for the categorical latent variables of diagnostic classification models was defined; using theory- and simulation-based results the authors showed that these models "uniformly provide greater examinee estimate reliability than IRT models for tests of the same length," and attributed this explicitly to "the smaller range of latent variable values examinee estimates can take" in a classification model. *(Templin & Bradshaw, 2013, *Journal of Classification* 30(2):251–275, DOI 10.1007/s00357-013-9129-4.)* `T2`
      - The same paper opens by noting that "despite their use, the reliability of examinee estimates in diagnostic classification model applications has seldom been reported." *(Templin & Bradshaw, 2013, *Journal of Classification* 30(2):251–275, DOI 10.1007/s00357-013-9129-4.)* `T2`
      - Three further measures of the reliability of posterior skill-mastery means were subsequently proposed, on the stated grounds that "as with any assessment, it is important to derive and report estimates of the reliability of the reported scores"; the authors recommended two of the measures for future use. *(Johnson & Sinharay, 2020, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 45(1):5–31, DOI 10.3102/1076998619864550.)* `T2` `[COI: one author affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - **Negative/critical finding.** A commentary in a peer-reviewed measurement journal responding to a major review of diagnostic classification models was titled, and framed around, the question "How much can we reliably know about what examinees know?" *(Sinharay & Haberman, 2009, *Measurement: Interdisciplinary Research and Perspectives* 7(1):46–49, DOI 10.1080/15366360802715486.)* `T2` `[COI: both authors affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - **Methodological caution on the reliability metric itself.** A review of diagnostic classification model practice reports the worked example that if 90% of a population possesses an attribute and the fitted model "provides absolutely no information about the attribute," a classification-accuracy index will nonetheless report accuracy for 90% of the population — i.e. raw classification accuracy is confounded with base rate. The same review notes that a widely used software routine for classification accuracy gave "substantially" different results from published indices and, at time of writing, lacked supporting peer-reviewed publication. *(Cambridge Assessment research report, "Cognitive Diagnostic Models and how they can be useful," summarising Sinharay & Johnson, 2019, pp. 369, 371.)* `T3` `[COI: published by an examination board]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Two things are true at once here and they are easy to conflate. First, the models work: given options deliberately engineered so that each distractor implies a specific attribute deficit, a diagnostic classification model can extract attribute-level classifications that a right/wrong model cannot, and it will do so with a *higher* nominal reliability than a continuous-trait model of the same test length. Second, that reliability advantage is partly an artifact of the question being easier — you are asked to sort people into two or a few boxes rather than place them on a continuum, so a coarser answer is naturally more repeatable. That is a real gain in decision consistency, not a gain in information. The field's own reliability metrics are contested; attribute-level classification accuracy is inflated by skewed base rates, and the leading authors concede that these reliabilities were rarely reported at all in the model's first decade of use. Crucially, the diagnostic payoff is contingent on distractors being *designed* against an attribute map beforehand — retrofitting a diagnostic model onto distractors written to be merely plausible is not the same procedure.
    - **Link to source:** [Templin & Bradshaw 2013 on DCM reliability](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00357-013-9129-4)

- **Subcategory 4.5: Subscores and profile reporting — a precise criterion for added value, and how often real tests fail it**
  - **Source:** Haberman (2008); Sinharay (2010); Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman (2011); Sinharay (2010, review); AERA/APA/NCME (2014)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The criterion, stated precisely: define the proportional reduction in mean squared error (PRMSE) in estimating the *true* subscore, relative to a trivial predictor. Let PRMSE<sub>s</sub> be the PRMSE of the observed subscore and PRMSE<sub>x</sub> that of the observed total score. A subscore "provides added value over the total score" **if and only if PRMSE<sub>s</sub> > PRMSE<sub>x</sub>** — that is, if and only if the observed subscore beats the observed total score at estimating the true subscore in mean squared error. PRMSE<sub>s</sub> is exactly equal to the reliability of the subscore. *(Haberman, 2008, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 33(2):204–229, DOI 10.3102/1076998607302636, as restated in Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman, 2011, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 30(3):29–40, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00208.x.)* `T2` / `T1` `[COI: all authors affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - The originating paper states the two joint conditions under which observed subscores have value: "observed subscores are most likely to have value if they have relatively high reliability by themselves and if the true subscore and true total score have only a moderate correlation. Both conditions are important." *(Haberman, 2008, *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics* 33(2):204–229, DOI 10.3102/1076998607302636.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - An augmented subscore — a weighted combination of subscore and total score — has PRMSE at least as large as either alone by construction, and is therefore recommended only when the increase over both is substantial. *(Haberman, 2008, DOI 10.3102/1076998607302636; operationalised as roughly a 10% increase over the better of the two in a companion survey.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - **Negative finding, operational scale.** Across data from 25 operational tests, 16 had *no* subscores with added value by this criterion, "even though several of them report subscores operationally." Among the remaining nine — all of which averaged at least 24 items per subscore — only *some* subscores had added value. *(Sinharay, 2010, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 47(2):150–174, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2010.00106.x, as summarised in Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman, 2011, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00208.x.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - The accompanying simulation study found that to have added value a subscore must (a) be based on a sufficient number of items, roughly 20, and (b) be sufficiently distinct — the disattenuated correlation between subscores must be less than about .85. *(Sinharay, 2010, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2010.00106.x.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - Subscores composed of 10 items were reported to have no added value "even for a realistically extreme (low) disattenuated correlation of 0.70." *(Sinharay, 2010, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2010.00106.x, as quoted in secondary summaries.)* `T2` `[COI: as above]`
      - **The incremental-validity bound.** Separately from mean-squared-error added value, there is a hard ceiling on how much a subscore can add to prediction of an external criterion: incremental validity, measured as the difference in coefficient of determination between predicting from total-plus-subscore versus total alone, "is no greater than the reliability of the residual from linear prediction of the subscore by the total score." *(Haberman, 2008, "Subscores and Validity," *ETS Research Report Series*, DOI 10.1002/j.2333-8504.2008.tb02150.x.)* `T3` `[COI: test-publisher technical report]`
      - **Professional standard.** Standard 1.14: "When interpretation of subscores, score differences, or profiles is suggested, the rationale and relevant evidence in support of such interpretation should be provided." Its comment: "When a test provides more than one score, the distinctiveness and reliability of the separate scores should be demonstrated, and the interrelationship of those scores should be shown to be consistent with the construct(s) being measured" (p. 27). Standard 2.3: "For each total score, subscore, or combination of scores that is to be interpreted, estimates of relevant indices of reliability/precision should be reported" (p. 43). *(American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association & National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, Washington, DC: AERA.)* `T1`
      - A critical review of diagnostic score reporting demonstrated "using results from operational and simulated data, that diagnostic scores have to be based on a sufficient number of items and have to be sufficiently distinct from each other to be worth reporting and that several operationally reported subscores are actually not worth reporting." *(Sinharay, 2010, *Multivariate Behavioral Research* 45(3):553–573, DOI 10.1080/00273171.2010.483382.)* `T2` `[COI: author affiliated with a large commercial testing organisation]`
      - An instructional module on the topic states plainly that "a psychometric model cannot extract something that is not there," and that when factor analysis reveals a single factor, no amount of modelling will produce defensible per-domain scores. *(Sinharay, Puhan & Haberman, 2011, *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice* 30(3):29–40, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00208.x.)* `T1` `[COI: as above]`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This subcategory contains the single most useful thing in the whole category, because it converts a vague design intuition into a decidable test. The question "should I report this breakdown?" has a formal answer: report it only if the sub-part predicts its own true value better than the whole test does. That test is cheap to compute and brutally strict. When it was applied to 25 real operational tests, roughly two-thirds had no defensible subscore at all — including tests that were shipping subscores to users. The practical thresholds that emerge are concrete: about 20 items minimum per reported sub-part, and disattenuated correlations among the parts below roughly .85. Ten-item sub-parts fail even under generous assumptions. Note the failure mode is not "the model can't estimate it" — the model estimates it fine. The failure is that the estimate is worse than just using the total score, which means reporting it actively misinforms. And there is a second, independent ceiling: however much a sub-part might *appear* to add to predicting anything external, it cannot exceed the reliability of the part of the sub-part that the total score does not already explain — which for correlated sub-parts is very little. The professional standards make demonstrating distinctness and reliability a *precondition* for reporting, not an afterthought.
    - **Link to source:** [Haberman 2008, "When can subscores have value?"](https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998607302636)

- **Subcategory 4.6: Error taxonomies in matrix and figural reasoning — is error type coherent, stable, and predictive?**
  - **Source:** Kunda, Soulières, Rozga & Goel (2016); Vodegel Matzen, van der Molen & Dudink (1994); Gonthier & Roulin (2020)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The published taxonomy of conceptual error types on matrix reasoning tests, synthesised from test manuals, has four categories: **Repetition** (the chosen distractor copies a matrix entry adjacent to the blank space), **Difference** (the distractor is qualitatively distinct from the alternatives, e.g. blank or containing elements found nowhere in the matrix), **Wrong Principle** (the distractor combines matrix elements according to an incorrect rule), and **Incomplete Correlate** (the distractor closely approximates the correct answer but does not fully account for problem details). *(Kunda, Soulières, Rozga & Goel, 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004, synthesising Raven et al., 2003 manuals.)* `T2` `[COI: the underlying error taxonomy originates in commercially published test manuals]`
      - **Coding reliability problem.** An earlier attempt to apply the taxonomy from one form of the test to sets C through E of another form found inter-rater reliability between two coders "to be only around 70%"; the authors concluded classification of those distractors was "problematic," as no explicit methodology for constructing the distractors was apparent in the test or its literature. *(Vodegel Matzen, van der Molen & Dudink, 1994, *Personality and Individual Differences* 16(3):433–445, DOI 10.1016/0191-8869(94)90070-1, as reported in Kunda et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2`
      - The 2016 paper's stated motivating problem was that "there is no established classification of conceptual error types" for the middle form of the test, and that distractors are frequently ambiguous between categories — one worked example is a distractor readable either as Repetition (it duplicates the entry above the blank) or as Incomplete Correlate (it differs from the key by a single missing row). The authors' response was to reorganise the four types into two *orthogonal* classifications rather than one four-way partition. *(Kunda et al., 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2`
      - The authors' review table of prior error-analysis studies records mixed and sometimes contradictory findings across studies, including at least one reporting that "patterns of error types [were] not related to total set score," and another reporting that the proportion of Incomplete Correlate errors correlated with score in lower-scoring groups but not in higher-scoring groups. *(Kunda et al., 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004, review table.)* `T2` — *these are secondhand summaries of primary studies not independently retrieved here.*
      - The same review table reports a study of 818 adults on the advanced form finding that "medium and high-ability participants make more Incomplete Correlate errors than other types" while "low-ability participants do not show an error type preference." *(Babcock, 2002, as summarised in Kunda et al., 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2` — *secondhand; the primary source was not retrieved or verified.*
      - The authors warn of a systematic artifact: "many previous analyses of error types did not correct for the baseline distributions of different error types in the test itself" — i.e. if a test simply contains more Repetition distractors, test-takers will make more Repetition errors regardless of strategy. *(Kunda et al., 2016, *Intelligence* 59:181–198, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004.)* `T2`
      - **Within-person instability.** A study of intraindividual strategy shifts in matrix reasoning found that individuals change solution strategy *within* a single test session, with the shift depending on working memory capacity and need for cognition. *(Gonthier & Roulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(3):564–579, DOI 10.1037/xge0000660.)* `T2`
      - Independent context, converging: across eye-movement, self-report, and latent-class strategy studies, "constructive matching" (building the correct solution) indicators correlate positively with cognitive ability, while "response elimination" indicators (time on options, back-and-forth gaze between matrix and options) correlate negatively with performance; both strategies can be used on the same item. *(Forthmann, Förster, Schütze, Hebbecker, Flessner, Peters & Souvignier, 2020, *Journal of Intelligence* 8(1):11, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011, reviewing Bethell-Fox et al. 1984; Vigneau et al. 2006; Hayes et al. 2011; Gonthier & Roulin 2019.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Error taxonomies for figural reasoning exist, come from the test manuals themselves, and have obvious face validity — a "nearly right" error is intuitively different from a "copied the box above" error. But the empirical foundation is much weaker than the taxonomy's confident four-category structure implies. Two trained coders agreed only about 70% of the time on which category a distractor belonged to, and the categories are not even logically disjoint: a single distractor can legitimately satisfy two definitions at once. Findings across studies conflict, and a large fraction of published error-pattern results are contaminated by a baseline artifact — nobody was correcting for how many distractors of each type the test happened to contain. Worse for the within-person stability question: people demonstrably switch strategies mid-test, and which strategy they use is itself ability-linked, so error type is not a stable trait-like signature but a moving, ability-dependent quantity. The one pattern with some cross-study consistency is directional rather than diagnostic: as ability rises, errors shift toward "close to correct" and away from "attending to the wrong thing." That is broadly the same information the nested-logit models are extracting numerically — which is reassuring for convergent validity, and also a reason to doubt that a hand-built error taxonomy adds much beyond what a fitted distractor model already captures.
    - **Link to source:** [Kunda et al. 2016 on matrix reasoning error patterns](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2016.09.004)

- **Subcategory 4.7: Costs — calibration data requirements, parameter instability, and the item-writing burden**
  - **Source:** DeMars (2003); Forthmann et al. (2020); Storme et al. (2019); Gierl, Bulut, Guo & Zhang (2017)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - In a study manipulating sample size, number of items, and number of categories as separate factors for nominal response model calibration: "Increasing the number of items had little effect on item parameter recovery, but increasing the number of categories increased the error variance of the parameter estimates. Error variance was also greater for more highly discriminating items and for skewed distributions of ability." *(DeMars, 2003, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 27(4):275–288, DOI 10.1177/0146621603027004003.)* `T2`
      - Prior work cited in that study had suggested "the ratio of the sample size to the total number of item parameters was a key factor" — a nominal model estimates two parameters per response alternative, so parameter count scales with options, not items. *(DeMars, 2003, DOI 10.1177/0146621603027004003, citing De Ayala & Sava-Bolesta, 1999.)* `T2`
      - A simulation study aimed at pilot-stage item selection concluded: "it is not generally recommended to use sample sizes of N = 100 for the detection of discriminatory distractors. Based on type-I-error findings, the sample size should be at least N = 200 when items include three distractors. When items include seven distractors, however, sample sizes below N = 500 cannot be recommended without further considerations, due to unacceptable type-I-error rates." *(Forthmann et al., 2020, *Journal of Intelligence* 8(1):11, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011.)* `T2`
      - The same authors state that "direct use of nested logit models with small sample sizes (e.g., N = 200) seems not feasible" given the models' parameter demands — the motivation for their proposing cheaper screening statistics instead. *(Forthmann et al., 2020, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011.)* `T2`
      - Distractors chosen by fewer than 5% of participants are conventionally treated as non-functioning; in that simulation the proportion of distractors falling below the 5% threshold *increased* as true distractor discrimination increased (9.62%, 10.34%, 15.56%, and 21.33% across four increasing levels of nominal-model discrimination), producing missing effect-size estimates. *(Forthmann et al., 2020, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence8010011.)* `T2`
      - **Observed instability in real data.** Even with 2,949 respondents, "the information matrix of the 4PNL model could not be inverted in order to compute the parameter standard errors, which may be a sign that the estimates were unstable"; the authors recommended the more parsimonious two- and three-parameter versions instead. *(Storme et al., 2019, *Journal of Intelligence* 7(3):17, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence7030017.)* `T2` `[COI: as in 4.3]`
      - A comprehensive review of the distractor literature concluded that "despite a vast body of literature on multiple-choice testing, the task of creating distractors has received much less attention," and identified distractor development and quality evaluation as areas where further research is needed. *(Gierl, Bulut, Guo & Zhang, 2017, *Review of Educational Research* 87(6):1082–1116, DOI 10.3102/0034654317726529.)* `T1`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Distractor-level modelling has a cost structure that scales badly in exactly the direction people want to push it. Adding response categories — the whole point of the exercise — directly inflates parameter error variance, and the binding constraint is the ratio of sample size to *parameters*, which grows with options per item rather than with items. Concrete floors from the literature: at least 200 respondents to even *detect* whether a three-option item's distractors are informative, at least 500 for a seven-option item, and considerably more to actually fit the scoring model rather than screen for it. There is a nasty second-order trap: the better a distractor discriminates, the fewer low-ability people choose it, so the most informative distractors are also the ones most likely to fall below the minimum-frequency threshold and drop out of estimation. And instability is not hypothetical — the richest model in a study with nearly three thousand respondents failed to produce standard errors at all. Finally, the whole approach presupposes distractors written to be diagnostic, and the field's own review says that is the least-studied part of item writing.
    - **Link to source:** [DeMars 2003 on sample size and nominal model parameter recovery](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621603027004003)

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **"The model fits better" versus "the estimate is more precise" versus "the estimate is reliable enough to report."** These are three separate milestones and the literature conflates them constantly. Generalized Partial Credit fits polytomous data better than Partial Credit (Muraki, 1992) — that is a model-selection fact and says nothing about how much you learn per person. Nested logit models raise reliability by ~0.02 (Storme et al., 2019) — that is a precision fact. Whether a per-domain or per-error-type score clears Haberman's PRMSE bar is a third question entirely, and it is the one that determines whether you may put a number in front of a person.

2. **Direct contradiction on whether distractor information is real or artifactual.** Bock (1972), Myszkowski & Storme (2018), and Storme et al. (2019) report genuine information gains from distractor scoring. García-Pérez (2014) argues that apparent information gains from polytomous characterisations of multiple-choice items are artifacts of model misspecification, identifies a "structural inadequacy of the nominal response model for multiple-choice items," and finds real gains only under a distractor pattern that is "rarely observed in empirical data sets." Notably, the nested logit family that produced the positive reasoning-test results was *designed* to fix the nominal model's structural problem with multiple-choice items (Suh & Bolt, 2010), and in the large replication the nested models did outperform the plain nominal model — so the two positions may be partially reconcilable, but nobody in this reading has reconciled them explicitly.

3. **Diagnostic models report *higher* reliability while the same research community says diagnostic scores are usually not reportable.** Templin & Bradshaw (2013) show classification models beat continuous models on reliability at equal test length — and attribute this to the coarser answer space. Sinharay and colleagues, across a decade, show that fine-grained diagnostic reporting usually fails an added-value test. These are not actually in conflict, but they read as if they are, and the resolution matters: coarsening the question raises repeatability without raising information.

4. **The meta-analytic evidence on distractor count cuts against distractor-heavy designs.** Rodriguez (2005) finds going from five options to three costs essentially nothing in reliability. Yet distractor-level scoring becomes more attractive with *more* distractors (more categories to read information from) and simultaneously more expensive to calibrate (Forthmann et al., 2020: N ≥ 500 for seven distractors). The count that is optimal for conventional scoring is not obviously the count that is optimal for distractor-level scoring, and no source found here resolves this trade-off directly.

5. **Error type is treated as a stable person characteristic in the taxonomy literature and as a labile within-session variable in the strategy literature.** Gonthier & Roulin (2020) document intraindividual strategy shifts within a single test; the error-taxonomy tradition mostly reports group-level error-type proportions as if they characterised persons. Only about 70% inter-rater agreement on the coding itself (Vodegel Matzen et al., 1994) puts a low ceiling on how stable any person-level error signature could be measured to be.

6. **A conflict of interest pattern worth naming.** A large fraction of the subscore-skepticism literature comes from researchers at one large testing organisation, and a large fraction of the distractor-scoring optimism in applied settings comes from vendors selling short assessments. Both bodies of work are peer-reviewed and internally rigorous, and in this case the incentives arguably run *against* the findings in the skeptical case (a test publisher arguing its own subscores are not reportable). But the asymmetry in who studies what is real.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own — a connection between sources that looks load-bearing but has not been established by any single study cited here.*

- **Seed A — "Distractor information is a low-ability subsidy, not a general precision gain."** Bock (1972) reported the gain was substantial below the median and nil above it. Myszkowski & Storme (2018) and Storme et al. (2019) independently report the gain concentrated at low ability. This is mechanically forced: high scorers produce few wrong answers, so there is nothing to read. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* if the decision being supported is a selection decision at the top of the distribution, distractor-level scoring buys close to nothing, and the ~0.02 average reliability gain is a misleading summary because it averages a real gain at the bottom with zero at the top. The corollary — that distractor scoring is most valuable precisely where you least need precision for a top-end decision, and most valuable where you *do* need it for a placement or support decision — needs to be tested against the conditional reliability curves in the source papers rather than assumed.

- **Seed B — "Distractor quality, not distractor quantity or model sophistication, is the actual limiting reagent."** Rodriguez's (2005) meta-analysis found reliability drops .06 when 3-option items are made by deleting random distractors but is unchanged when ineffective distractors are deleted. de la Torre (2009) requires options *constructed* against an attribute map. Gierl et al. (2017) report distractor creation is the least-studied part of the whole multiple-choice literature. Forthmann et al. (2020) had to invent screening statistics because the models themselves are too data-hungry for pilot samples. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* every reported success in this category rests on distractors that were engineered to be diagnostic, and the modelling machinery is downstream of an item-writing practice that is under-specified in the literature. If that is right, then "should I model distractors?" is the wrong first question and "can I write distractors that map to distinguishable error mechanisms?" is the right one — and the answer to the second determines whether the first is even worth asking.

- **Seed C — "Haberman's criterion is a general firewall against any fine-grained reporting, not just against subscores."** The PRMSE test asks whether a part-score predicts its own true value better than the whole score does, and 16 of 25 operational tests failed it outright (Sinharay, 2010), with roughly 20 items and disattenuated correlations below .85 as necessary conditions. Attribute-level classifications from diagnostic models, per-error-type profiles from an error taxonomy, and per-domain subscores are all structurally the same object: a claim that a sub-part of the evidence carries information the whole does not. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* the added-value criterion — or its diagnostic-model analogues (Johnson & Sinharay, 2020) — should be applied to *any* proposed fine-grained readout, not only to number-correct subscores, and doing so would likely disqualify most error-type profile reporting on short instruments. Testing this requires checking whether PRMSE-style reasoning has actually been formalised for categorical attribute estimates or whether that is an extrapolation.

- **Seed D — "There may be a dimensionality tax hiding inside the reliability gain."** Bolt, Wollack & Suh (2012) built a multidimensional nested logit model specifically because distractor selection may reflect a *different* trait than the correct response. Forthmann et al. (2020) summarise evidence that response-elimination strategy indicators correlate *negatively* with ability while constructive-matching indicators correlate positively — so the behaviour that generates an informative distractor choice is itself strategy-dependent, and strategy shifts within a session (Gonthier & Roulin, 2020). *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* part of the ~0.02 reliability gain from distractor scoring may be reliability of a *second* construct (strategy or test-taking approach) being folded into a single score, which would raise internal consistency while degrading construct validity. Reliability going up is not evidence against this — it is exactly what adding a coherent second dimension would do. Whether any of the cited studies tested for it is the thing to check.

### Category 5: Confidence, metacognition and calibration — what the extra rating actually buys

*General idea: Asking a test-taker "how sure are you?" is the cheapest supplementary signal available, and it is the one with the deepest measurement literature behind it. That literature is also the one most contaminated by its own arithmetic. Almost every popular calibration index is a function of accuracy, so an apparent "added" signal can be a rearrangement of the score you already had; the field's own methodologists spent two decades building bias-free alternatives precisely because of this. Where incremental validity over accuracy has been measured properly, it is real but small and inconsistent across criteria — positive on some outcomes, exactly zero on others in the same study. The trait interpretation is weaker still: within-person accuracy of monitoring has poor test–retest and cross-task stability, in adults and in children. Young children can produce confidence judgments that discriminate correct from incorrect answers from about age three to five, so feasibility is not the binding constraint; validity and stability are. And the act of asking is not free: eliciting confidence measurably changes the primary task, in at least one large pre-registered study by suppressing rule-based learning.*

    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the subcategory that decides the whole question, and its answer is uncomfortable. The most widely used calibration indices are arithmetically entangled with the accuracy they are supposed to supplement. Signed bias — mean confidence minus proportion correct — is a linear function of the score by construction. Gamma is demonstrably biased under realistic conditions. Type-II d′ and type-II ROC measures confound metacognition with both first-order ability and response bias, which is the stated reason meta-d′ exists at all, and the entire concept of "metacognitive efficiency" is an admission that sensitivity is uninterpretable unless you condition on performance. The single most-cited "metacognitive bias" phenomenon in the literature, the hard–easy effect, largely disappears once scale-end effects and linear dependency are controlled. Two independent factor analyses of the measurement space agree that no one index captures monitoring, and that at minimum you need separate accounts of judgments about correct answers and judgments about incorrect answers. The practical implication is blunt: any claim that a calibration score "adds information beyond accuracy" is not evaluable unless the index used is one that has been explicitly de-confounded from accuracy, and most reported indices are not.

      - **The fairness objection, quantified.** Integrating item response theory with cumulative prospect theory across six CBM examinations taken by 334 first-year physiotherapy students, "the results showed that with the given CBM scoring matrix, students' choices of a certainty level were affected by their risk attitudes. Meanwhile, they were risk seeking in case of small success probabilities on the items, resulting in the over-reporting of certainty." *(Wu, Vanerum, Agten, Christiansen, Vandenabeele, Rigo & Janssen, 2021, *Psychometrika* 86(2):518–543, DOI 10.1007/s11336-021-09759-0.)* `T2` `[Interest note: the CBM examinations analysed were run by the authors' own institution]`

We see no differences in knowledge of the material or confidence in the test takers, and differences in risk preferences explain less than half of the observed gap."     - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Confidence weighting has been tried continuously since the 1960s and has never established a durable advantage. The largest head-to-head comparison — seven methods, four content domains, six evaluation criteria — concluded that no method was uniformly superior and that the methods interacted with test content. The most recent controlled application found the certainty-weighted score statistically indistinguishable from plain accuracy on the same exam, which is the cleanest possible null: the added response cost bought no change in the distribution being used to make decisions. Meanwhile the fairness objection is not speculative arm-waving. A psychometric-plus-decision-theoretic analysis of real CBM exams shows that reported certainty is systematically distorted by risk attitude in *both* directions — under-reported when the student is likely right, over-reported when likely wrong — which means the certainty channel carries a risk-preference signal mixed in with the knowledge signal. When a scoring rule penalises wrong high-confidence answers, that risk-preference signal converts into score differences between demographic groups even when knowledge and confidence are equal. There is a genuine countervailing finding — one 1994 study found a self-assessment format *narrowed* a male–female score gap — but the mechanisms differ (self-assessment without penalty versus penalised certainty), and nothing located here reconciles them.

In the best-controlled pre/post study located, groups with equivalent prior knowledge but different *pre-instruction* calibration learned equally well; what separated them was whether calibration improved *during* instruction. - **Subcategory 5.5: Metacognition as a trait — the general-factor question and the reliability problem underneath it**
  - **Source:** Kelemen, Frost & Weaver (2000); Schraw, Dunkle, Bendixen & Roedel (1995); Rouault, McWilliams, Allen & Fleming (2018); Mazancieux, Fleming, Souchay & Moulin (2020); Eberhart, Murayama, Sakaki & Bryce (2025)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The core negative result, and it is about reliability rather than theory.** Four common metacognitive tasks (ease-of-learning judgments, feeling-of-knowing judgments, judgments of learning, and text comprehension monitoring) were administered twice with a one-week interval, and alternate-forms correlations computed. "Although individual differences in memory and confidence were stable across both sessions and tasks, differences in metacognitive accuracy were not. These results pose considerable practical and theoretical challenges for metacognitive researchers." *(Kelemen, Frost & Weaver, 2000, *Memory & Cognition* 28(1):92–107, DOI 10.3758/BF03211579.)* `T2`
      - **A partial positive, with the same asymmetry.** Across eight different knowledge domains, "college students' performance and discrimination accuracy were not correlated across 8 different domains, whereas confidence and judgment bias were." With tests matched on all salient dimensions except content, everything correlated, and "confidence was correlated even after the effect of performance was removed." The authors describe this as "qualified support for the domain-general hypothesis." *(Schraw, Dunkle, Bendixen & Roedel, 1995, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 87(3):433–444, DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.87.3.433.)* `T2`
      - **Meta-analytic verdict: partly domain-general at best.** "A meta-analysis of behavioral studies found that perceptual metacognitive ability was correlated across different sensory modalities, but found no correlation between metacognition of perception and memory." The authors add that "evidence for domain-generality from behavioral data may suffer from a lack of power to identify correlations across model parameters indexing metacognitive efficiency." *(Rouault, McWilliams, Allen & Fleming, 2018, *Personality Neuroscience* 1, DOI 10.1017/pen.2018.16.)* `T2`
      - **Directly contradicting result, two years later.** Correlations in retrospective metacognitive sensitivity across tasks "were significant, even for pairs of tasks in which first-order performance was not correlated. This suggests that at least some components of metacognitive efficiency in retrospective confidence are domain general." *(Mazancieux, Fleming, Souchay & Moulin, 2020, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(9):1788–1799, DOI 10.1037/xge0000746.)* `T2`
      - **In children, the negative result reappears.** Among 53 elementary school children assessed with four monitoring tasks, "correlational analysis showed that there was no significant association between children's monitoring judgment accuracies," while the *frequency* of monitoring behaviours on two construction tasks was significantly positively associated. The authors conclude that "the processes underlying children's monitoring judgments may be task specific, whereas their tendency to engage in monitoring behaviours may be domain general." *(Eberhart, Murayama, Sakaki & Bryce, 2025, *Cognitive Development* 74:101561, DOI 10.1016/j.cogdev.2025.101561.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is a live and unresolved dispute about whether a general metacognitive ability exists, but the dispute is downstream of a more damaging fact: monitoring-accuracy scores are unreliable. When the same people were measured twice a week apart on four standard metacognitive tasks, their memory scores and their raw confidence levels replicated and their monitoring accuracy did not. Every claim about metacognition-as-a-trait has to survive that ceiling, and low reliability is by itself sufficient to produce weak cross-task correlations — which is exactly the pattern the meta-analytic review flags as a possible power artifact. The same asymmetry appears in three independent studies across twenty-five years and across adults and children: *confidence level* behaves like a stable person characteristic, while *confidence accuracy* does not. That is a specific and actionable finding. If a person-level readout is wanted, mean confidence is the psychometrically better-behaved quantity — but mean confidence is exactly the quantity that is a linear function of accuracy plus a bias term, and therefore the one least likely to add anything.
    - **Link to source:** [Kelemen, Frost & Weaver 2000, evidence against a general metacognitive ability](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03211579) · [Mazancieux et al. 2020, "Is there a G factor for metacognition?"](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000746)

- **Subcategory 5.6: High ability and the Dunning–Kruger literature — most of the effect is statistical**
  - **Source:** Kruger & Dunning (1999); Krueger & Mueller (2002); Gignac & Zajenkowski (2020); Nuhfer, Cogan, Fleischer, Gaze & Wirth (2016); Snyder, Nietfeld & Linnenbrink-Garcia (2011)

      - **The artifact is graphical as well as statistical.** Random-number simulations applied alongside paired self-assessed and demonstrated competence measures from 1,154 participants showed that "graphical conventions common in the self-assessment literature introduce artifacts that invite misinterpretation," specifically naming "(y minus x) vs. (x) scatterplots; (y minus x) vs. (x) column graphs aggregated as quantiles; line charts that display data aggregated as quantiles; and some histograms." *(Nuhfer, Cogan, Fleischer, Gaze & Wirth, 2016, *Numeracy* 9(1), DOI 10.5038/1936-4660.9.1.4.)* `T2` — *interest note: the authors advocate their own self-assessment instrumentation elsewhere.*

- **Subcategory 5.7: Children — when confidence judgments become usable, and what still fails at school age**
  - **Source:** Lyons & Ghetti (2011); Destan, Hembacher, Ghetti & Roebers (2014); Vo, Li, Kornell, Pouget & Cantlon (2014); Schneider (2008); Baer, Malik & Odic (2021); Ohtani & Hisasaka (2018)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **Discrimination is present before school age.** Among 74 three-, four- and five-year-olds completing perceptual and lexical identification tasks with certain/uncertain reports, "even 3-year-olds provided confidence judgments that discriminated accurate from inaccurate responses, but this discrimination increased with age."       - **Developmental trends favour knowledge and control over monitoring.** "Whereas the findings for declarative metacognitive knowledge show steady improvement through childhood and adolescence, mainly due to increases in knowledge about strategies, the results are not similarly clear-cut for procedural metacognition. So a school-age screener can collect the signal. The problems are elsewhere and they are the same three problems as in adults, only sharper. First, children's monitoring accuracy is task-specific: measure it on one task and it does not tell you about another, and the only case where it predicted school achievement was when the metacognitive task and the achievement domain matched. Second, the cross-task correlations that would justify treating it as an ability are absent in the target age band, in a study of 374 children explicitly designed to remove the usual confounds. #### Tensions and contradictions

1. **Direct contradiction on whether metacognition is domain-general.** A 2018 meta-analysis reports perceptual metacognitive ability correlating across sensory modalities but *no* correlation between metacognition of perception and metacognition of memory. A 2020 study by an overlapping author group reports significant cross-task correlations in retrospective metacognitive sensitivity "even for pairs of tasks in which first-order performance was not correlated." Both cannot be straightforwardly right. The 2018 review pre-emptively supplies the reconciliation — behavioural studies may be underpowered to detect correlations across efficiency parameters — but that reconciliation also implies the earlier null literature is uninformative, which no one in this reading states outright.

2. **Domain-generality in adults versus domain-specificity in children.** Whatever the adult answer, two independent child studies point the other way: a nonverbal wagering study found numerical and emotional metacognition unrelated in five-year-olds, and a 2025 study of 53 elementary children found no association between monitoring accuracies across two tasks. If metacognition becomes more general with age, then the age band a school screener targets is precisely the band where a general metacognitive score is least defensible. No located source tests that developmental-convergence hypothesis directly.

3. **The reliability paradox.** Confidence *level* is stable within person across sessions and tasks; confidence *accuracy* is not. This is reported in 2000 for adults and echoed in 2025 for children. But the stable quantity is the one that is arithmetically tied to accuracy, and the unstable quantity is the one that would carry new information. Every proposal to use calibration as a person-level trait has to pick one horn of this.

4. **"Small incremental validity" and "no incremental validity" from the same dataset.** In the one clean test located, confidence added a small increment over accuracy for the test's own total score, two of its sections, and a numeracy measure — and added exactly nothing for an admissions test or high-school GPA. Papers citing this work in support of confidence measurement generally cite the first half. The pattern is more consistent with confidence adding method-shared variance to same-instrument criteria than with confidence carrying general predictive signal.

5. **Certainty-based marking is simultaneously "fair" and "risk-loaded."** One study reports 78% of students perceiving CBM as fair and finds no significant difference between certainty-based and accuracy scores; a psychometric analysis of six real CBM exams finds certainty reports systematically distorted by risk and loss aversion in opposite directions at high and low success probability. Both can be true — a risk-distorted score can still be distributionally similar in aggregate and still feel fair — but the second result means the certainty channel is not measuring only knowledge, and the first means it may not be measuring much of anything extra.

6. **Overconfidence is a red flag and a benign trait, depending on when you measure it.** The best-controlled learning-gain study located found that pre-instruction overconfidence "may actually be beneficial to learning" and did not hamper gain, while overconfidence that persisted or emerged after instruction was associated with markedly lower gain. The Dunning–Kruger framing treats overconfidence as a static deficit; this result treats it as informative only as a trajectory.

7. **A conflict-of-interest pattern worth naming.** Much of the confidence-scoring advocacy literature is written by the developers of the scoring scheme being advocated, or by faculty evaluating the format in their own courses; and the founding review of confidence testing was written from inside a large commercial testing organisation. In this case the incentives do not obviously run one way — the institutional self-evaluations produced the most prominent *null* — but the concentration of the evidence base in self-interested hands is real and is flagged inline throughout.

- **Seed A — "Most reported calibration 'signal' is accuracy wearing a hat."** Signed bias is mean confidence minus proportion correct, a linear function of the score. Gamma systematically deviates from its true value. Type-II d′ and type-II ROC confound metacognition with first-order sensitivity, which is the stated reason meta-d′ was invented. The hard–easy effect, the field's most-cited bias phenomenon, largely vanishes under control for scale-end effects and linear dependency. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* an incremental-validity claim for calibration is only meaningful if the index has been de-confounded from accuracy — meta-d′/d′ or an equivalent — and essentially none of the educational calibration literature uses such an index. If that is right, the applied literature and the methodological literature are measuring different things, and the applied effect sizes cannot be taken at face value. Testing this requires checking which index each reported effect size was computed on, which the summaries above deliberately do not assume.

- **Seed B — "The stable part is useless and the useful part is unstable."** Raw confidence level replicates within person across sessions and across tasks; monitoring accuracy does not, in adults in 2000 and in children in 2025. But confidence level is the quantity mechanically tied to accuracy, and monitoring accuracy is the quantity that would add something. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* this is a formal trade-off, not an incidental measurement problem — the reliability of a derived monitoring index is bounded by the reliability of the difference between two noisy quantities, so the more you succeed in removing accuracy from a calibration index, the less reliable what remains must become. Whether that bound has been derived anywhere, or whether high-reliability de-confounded indices exist, is the thing to check before assuming the trade-off is inescapable.

- **Seed C — "Calibration predicts learning as a trajectory, not as a level."** In the best-controlled pre/post study located, subgroups with statistically indistinguishable prior knowledge but different pre-instruction calibration achieved similar learning gains; what separated the top group (53% normalised gain) from the bottom (15%) was whether calibration *improved* across instruction. Pre-instruction overconfidence was explicitly described as not debilitating. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* if the predictive signal is in the change in calibration under teaching, then a single-administration screener is structurally incapable of capturing it, and any instrument that wants this signal must be a two-point or repeated design. Note that the counter-reading is also available and untested here — posttest calibration is measured after learning has happened, so it may be an *outcome* of learning rather than a predictor of it, in which case the study shows nothing predictive at all. Both readings need to be tested against the paper's timing.

- **Seed D — "The confidence prompt is not a passive probe."** Judgments of learning change study-time allocation and what gets recalled, and are "partially constructed in response to the measurement question." A pre-registered study of 710 people found confidence ratings specifically reduced rule-based learning relative to both no-judgment and judgment-of-learning controls. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* for any instrument whose target construct is reasoning or rule induction rather than recall, adding a confidence prompt may trade a small measurement gain for a real degradation of the behaviour being measured — and the degradation falls on the higher-order component (transfer, rule use) rather than uniformly. If so, the reactivity literature is not a methodological footnote but a first-order design constraint, and the correct comparison is not "accuracy versus accuracy-plus-confidence" but "accuracy-without-prompt versus accuracy-with-prompt-plus-confidence," where the first term differs between conditions. No located study runs that comparison on a reasoning test.

- **Seed E — "The high-ability advantage, if it exists, is retrospective and local."** Gifted high-school students outperformed peers on local and global *postdictive* calibration and on nothing else — no difference in global predictive judgments, no difference in calibration bias — and only item-level calibration mediated the achievement relationship. Independently, delayed judgments beat immediate ones by g = 0.93, and item-level judgments outperform global ones in the wider literature. *Starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate:* the two findings may be the same finding — both say that judgments made with more evidence in hand (after the answer, after a delay, per item) carry information that judgments made prospectively and globally do not. If that holds, then the only confidence measurement worth collecting is item-level and retrospective, and the common design of asking for an up-front global self-estimate is measuring something close to self-concept instead. Whether the giftedness result replicates at all is prior to this: n = 23 in the comparison group and no replication was located.

- **Gap, and it is the headline one:** only a single study was located that measured confidence on the same items as accuracy and reported a proper incremental-validity test against external criteria. ### Category 6: Measuring learning rate and modifiability

*General idea: Three claims are routinely fused into one and must be kept apart — that people differ in how fast they learn, that a short assessment can measure an individual's learning rate precisely enough to act on, and that such a measurement predicts a real outcome beyond a static ability score. The first is weakly supported and contested at the level of magnitude; the second runs into a hard mathematical wall (differences between two imperfectly reliable measures are less reliable than either, and the standard textbook version of that result is itself the worst case rather than the general case); the third has a real but modest evidence base — median unique variance of roughly 5% in the best-controlled synthesis — accumulated almost entirely in early literacy and clinical rehabilitation, and it is contaminated throughout by retest effects that are themselves as large as many reported learning gains.*

      - A 2014 conceptual critique is titled "The Umbrella That Is Too Wide and Yet Too Small: Why Dynamic Testing Has Still Not Delivered on the Promise That Was Never Made." *(Beckmann, 2014, *Journal of Cognitive Education and Psychology* 13(3):308–323, DOI 10.1891/1945-8959.13.3.308.)* `T2`

      - The same 2018 review, updating a 2003 review by its first author, states that "the belief that deficient cognitive/executive functions could be identified and ameliorated, and subsequently result in academic progress, has not been supported," that where laboratory training gains have been found "these have tended not to transfer meaningfully to classroom contexts," that dynamic assessment "continues to be supported primarily on the basis of case studies," and that the 2003 call for research systematically examining the assessment–intervention relationship "has yet to be realised." Its closing sentence: "Sadly, the perceived potential of learning potential (dynamic) assessment continues to remain unfulfilled." *(Elliott, Resing & Beckmann, 2018, *Educational Review* 70(1):7–17, DOI 10.1080/00131911.2018.1396806.)* `T2`
      - The same review distinguishes **dynamic testing** (a research programme in reasoning and problem-solving) from **dynamic assessment** (a practitioner activity oriented to informing instruction), and argues the two have been conflated in the literature — including in the first author's own earlier review. *(Elliott, Resing & Beckmann, 2018, *Educational Review* 70(1):7–17, DOI 10.1080/00131911.2018.1396806.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The critique is not that dynamic testing does nothing. It is threefold and more specific than that. (1) **Construct incoherence:** the field carries at least half a dozen non-identical labels — learning ability, cognitive modifiability, trainability, intellectual change potential, learning potential — and validating "dynamic testing" as a category is a category error; only individual instruments can be validated. (2) **Psychometric under-development:** standardisation and reliability remain unresolved after six decades, and the field's own most prominent reviewers concede the point. (3) **Failure of the translational claim:** the specific promise that motivated most practitioner enthusiasm — that assessment would reveal remediable cognitive deficits whose remediation would produce academic gains — is the claim the evidence has most clearly failed to support, and laboratory gains have not transferred to classrooms. Note that the strongest critics are not outsiders: the 2018 update is written by an author revising his own earlier and more optimistic review, and the 2009 concession comes from a co-author of the field's canonical *Psychological Bulletin* review. This is a literature that has audited itself and found itself wanting, which makes the critique substantially harder to dismiss as hostile.
    - **Link to source:** [Elliott, Resing & Beckmann 2018 — the field's own audit](https://doi.org/10.1080/00131911.2018.1396806)

- **Subcategory 6.4: The arithmetic of change — gain-score reliability and regression to the mean**
  - **Source:** Cronbach & Furby (1970); Rogosa, Brandt & Zimowski (1982); Rogosa & Willett (1983); Williams & Zimmerman (1996); Nesselroade, Stigler & Baltes (1980)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The foundational treatment examined previously recommended procedures for estimating change scores, residual or "basefree" measures of change, and other difference scores, and concluded with "a series of recommended procedures which solve research and personnel-decision problems **without estimation of change scores for individuals**." *(Cronbach & Furby, 1970, *Psychological Bulletin* 74(1):68–80, DOI 10.1037/h0029382; emphasis added.)* `T2`
      - The same paper did not simply prohibit change measurement — it "develops superior estimators for the kinds of problem where we do recommend using a measure of change," extending an approach due to Lord to obtain more precise estimates of an individual's true change. *(Cronbach & Furby, 1970, *Psychological Bulletin* 74(1):68–80, DOI 10.1037/h0029382.)* `T2`
      - A 1982 rebuttal reframed the problem as one of individual growth curves rather than pre–post differences. *(Rogosa, Brandt & Zimowski, 1982, "A growth curve approach to the measurement of change," *Psychological Bulletin* 92(3):726–748, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.92.3.726; bibliographic record verified via registry metadata, full text not retrieved.)* `T2`
      - A companion paper is titled, in the original capitals, "Demonstrating the Reliability of the Difference Score in the Measurement of Change." *(Rogosa & Willett, 1983, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 20(4):335–343, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.1983.tb00211.x.)* `T2`
      - **The crux, stated precisely.** The general expression for the reliability of a difference depends on the reliabilities of pretest and posttest, their correlation, *and* the parameter λ, the ratio of the pretest to the posttest standard deviation. The familiar textbook formula is a special case of this general expression obtained by assuming equal reliabilities and equal standard deviations. *(Williams & Zimmerman, 1996, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 20(1):59–69, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000106.)* `T2`
      - **And the special case is the worst case.** Citing an optimisation proof by Sharma & Gupta (1986), the same paper states that when pretest and posttest reliabilities are equal, the reliability of a difference is *minimised* when λ = 1.00 — so "the assumptions necessary to develop Equation 4, found in many textbooks … are precisely those that show the reliability of differences in the most unfavorable light." Difference-score reliability improves as λ departs from 1.00. *(Williams & Zimmerman, 1996, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 20(1):59–69, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000106.)* `T2`
      - A second, subtler point from the same paper: because the pre–post correlation is itself attenuated by unreliability of the components, difference-score reliability is a *composite* function of component reliability, not a simple function of it with the correlation held fixed. Plotting difference-score reliability against component reliability with the *true-score* correlation held constant "makes it apparent that the discrepancy between the reliability of a difference and the reliability of the components is less" than the standard textbook plots suggest. *(Williams & Zimmerman, 1996, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 20(1):59–69, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000106.)* `T2`
      - The same paper notes that reliability and validity "are not inherent characteristics of an educational or psychological test," but are defined relative to the true-score distribution of a particular population — so gain-score reliability can differ between populations administered the same instrument, and can change as a consequence of the gains themselves. *(Williams & Zimmerman, 1996, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 20(1):59–69, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000106.)* `T2`
      - The classical treatment of regression artefacts in change research is a 1980 *Psychological Bulletin* paper titled "Regression toward the mean and the study of change." *(Nesselroade, Stigler & Baltes, 1980, *Psychological Bulletin* 88(3):622–637, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.88.3.622; bibliographic record verified via registry metadata, full text not retrieved.)* `T2`
      - The difference-score debate remains unresolved in practice rather than in theory: the 1996 paper observes that "despite these arguments and empirical evidence to support them, many introductory textbooks in tests, measurement, and evaluation continue to criticize the reliability of gain scores." *(Williams & Zimmerman, 1996, *Applied Psychological Measurement* 20(1):59–69, DOI 10.1177/014662169602000106.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The popular version of this result — "gain scores are unreliable, full stop" — is wrong, and the correct version is more useful and more demanding. Difference-score reliability is not a fixed property; it is a function of four things, and the textbook horror stories are generated by fixing three of them at the single least favourable configuration (equal pre/post reliabilities, equal pre/post standard deviations, high pre–post correlation). Loosen any of those and the picture improves. But the loosening carries its own price. Difference-score reliability rises as the pre–post correlation *falls* and as the variance ratio departs from 1 — which is to say, a gain score becomes reliable precisely to the extent that the posttest is measuring something different from, or more dispersed than, the pretest. That is not obviously good news for anyone who wanted the gain score to represent "the same construct, later." The practical corollary is that the reliability of any specific change measurement is an empirical question about that specific instrument in that specific population, and cannot be inherited from either the optimists or the pessimists. And the 1970 recommendation still stands unrefuted in its narrow form: for most research and personnel-decision problems there exists a formulation that answers the question *without* computing an individual change score at all.
    - **Link to source:** [Williams & Zimmerman 1996 — why the textbook case is the worst case](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662169602000106)

- **Subcategory 6.5: Estimating true change — reliable change, growth models, latent change scores, and how much data they need**
  - **Source:** Jacobson & Truax (1991); Blampied (2022); Willett (1989); McArdle (2009); Rast & Hofer (2014); Siegler & Crowley (1991)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The Reliable Change Index was introduced "to determine whether the magnitude of change for a given client is statistically reliable," and was combined with a normative-range criterion into "a twofold criterion for clinically significant change." *(Jacobson & Truax, 1991, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 59(1):12–19, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.59.1.12.)* `T2`
      - A 2022 methodological review states the underlying logic — "the measured change must reliably exceed that which might be due just to measurement error alone" — and notes the conventional index is computed as 1.96 times the standard error of the difference between scores; it also notes the index can be expressed as the absolute raw-score difference required for change to be reliable at a stated criterion level. *(Blampied, 2022, *the Cognitive Behaviour Therapist* 15, DOI 10.1017/S1754470X22000484.)* `T2`
      - The same review stresses the ordering: establishing reliable change is a *prerequisite* for any claim of meaningful change, quoting the original authors — "It is nonsensical to speak of clinically significant treatment effects when no change has occurred." *(Blampied, 2022, *the Cognitive Behaviour Therapist* 15, DOI 10.1017/S1754470X22000484, quoting Jacobson et al., 1984, p. 343.)* `T2`
      - **Design requirements, quantified.** The reliability of change measurement depends on exactly three things: the magnitude of inter-individual heterogeneity in *true* growth, the measurement-error variance, and the number of waves of data collected. The paper "demonstrates that dramatic increases in the reliability of change measurement can be achieved by collecting relatively few additional waves of data." *(Willett, 1989, *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 49(3):587–602, DOI 10.1177/001316448904900309.)* `T2`
      - The same growth-rate reliability index "confounds the unrelated influences of group heterogeneity in growth-rate and measurement precision" — meaning it will be zero when there are no individual differences in slope even if the instrument's own reliability is high. *(Rast & Hofer, 2014, *Psychological Methods* 19(1):133–154, DOI 10.1037/a0034524, quoting Willett, 1989, p. 595.)* `T2`
      - A simulation study based on parameters drawn from actual longitudinal studies establishes that the power to detect variances and covariances among rates of change depends on the slope variance, the measurement-error variance, the number of waves, the spacing between waves, the total study duration, and sample size — with the wave count, spacing and duration being "typically under the control of the researcher." *(Rast & Hofer, 2014, *Psychological Methods* 19(1):133–154, DOI 10.1037/a0034524.)* `T2`
      - The modern structural-equation alternative to raw difference scores is reviewed in a 2009 *Annual Review* article that moves from "classic SEMs based on the inclusion of invariant common factors" to "newer SEMs based on latent change scores," explicitly framed around "classic problems in longitudinal data analysis … definitions of differences and changes, and … issues about measurement errors." *(McArdle, 2009, *Annual Review of Psychology* 60(1):577–605, DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.60.110707.163612.)* `T2`
      - **The intensive-observation alternative.** The microgenetic method is defined by exactly three properties: "(a) Observations span the entire period from the beginning of the change to the time at which it reaches a relatively stable state. (b) The density of observations is high relative to the rate of change of the phenomenon. (c) Observed behavior is subjected to intensive trial-by-trial analysis, with the goal of inferring the processes that give rise to both quantitative and qualitative aspects of change." *(Siegler & Crowley, 1991, *American Psychologist* 46(6):606–620, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.46.6.606.)* `T2`
      - The same paper is explicit about the cost: "The cost in time and effort of such studies is often high, but the value of the information about change can more than justify the cost." *(Siegler & Crowley, 1991, *American Psychologist* 46(6):606–620, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.46.6.606.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** There is a well-developed toolkit for doing this properly, and its price is stated openly in every source: **occasions**. Two waves is the minimum arithmetic case and the worst statistical one; reliability of an individual growth estimate rises steeply with a small number of additional waves, and the number, spacing, and total span of those waves are the design levers the researcher actually controls. Two conceptual points matter more than the machinery. First, the Reliable Change Index is a *screening* criterion, not a measurement: it tells you whether an observed change exceeds measurement noise for one person, and it must be satisfied before any claim about the meaning of that change is even coherent. Second, growth-rate reliability is not a property of the instrument — it goes to zero when the population genuinely has no individual differences in slope, no matter how precise the instrument is. That is a trap with a specific shape: a low observed growth-rate reliability is ambiguous between "our measure is noisy" and "there is nothing there to measure," and distinguishing them requires a design that could have detected slope variance if it existed. The microgenetic tradition solves the occasion problem by brute force — observe densely across the whole transition — and pays for it in cost per participant, which is precisely why it lives in developmental laboratories rather than in operational assessment.
    - **Link to source:** [Willett 1989 on waves and the reliability of change](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448904900309)

- **Subcategory 6.6: Learning curves — functional form, the averaging artifact, and the precision of an individual rate estimate**
  - **Source:** Estes (1956); Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort (2000); Myung, Kim & Pitt (2000); Brown & Heathcote (2003); Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote (2018); Waltmann, Schlagenhauf & Deserno (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - The problem was formally classified in 1956 into three cases: "Class A, Functions unmodified by averaging; Class B, Functions for which averaging complicates the interpretation of parameters but leaves form unchanged; and Class C, Functions modified in form by averaging." The paper's central conclusion: "The form of a group mean curve may provide a way to test exact hypotheses about individual curves, although the form of the latter is not determined by the form of the group mean curve." *(Estes, 1956, *Psychological Bulletin* 53(2):134–140, DOI 10.1037/h0045156.)* `T2`
      - **The decisive empirical demonstration.** Power and exponential functions were fitted to "40 sets of data representing 7,910 learning series from 475 subjects in 24 experiments." Result: "The exponential function fit better than the power function in **all** the unaveraged data sets. Averaging produced a bias in favor of the power function." A new four-parameter function nesting both (the APEX function) fit better than a power function with an extra pre-experimental-practice parameter. *(Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort, 2000, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 7(2):185–207, DOI 10.3758/BF03212979; emphasis added.)* `T2`
      - The mechanism of the artifact was isolated as "the combined contributions of three factors: arithmetic averaging of data that are generated from a nonlinear model in the presence of individual differences." Geometric rather than arithmetic averaging "sometimes eliminated the artifact, improving the fit of the exponential model relative to the power model." *(Myung, Kim & Pitt, 2000, *Memory & Cognition* 28(5):832–840, DOI 10.3758/BF03198418.)* `T2`
      - The mathematical statement of the problem predates the modern debate: Sidman showed that "the average of a finite number of exponential functions could never be exactly exponential in form itself, if the rate parameters of the components differ," and "a similar result holds for the average of power functions." *(Brown & Heathcote, 2003, *Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers* 35(1):11–21, DOI 10.3758/BF03195493, reporting Sidman, 1952.)* `T2`
      - The intuitive account of *why* averaging exponentials produces something power-like: "a power function can emerge because all learners contribute to improvements in the average function early in practice, whereas only slow learners contribute later in practice." *(Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote, 2018, *Psychological Review* 125(4):592–605, DOI 10.1037/rev0000105.)* `T2`
      - The same 2018 paper notes a further complication for short-block estimation: "there can be an initial delay in learning, sometimes followed by very fast learning," which "neither the exponential nor power laws can account for," motivating their extension of both. *(Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote, 2018, *Psychological Review* 125(4):592–605, DOI 10.1037/rev0000105, citing Rickard, 1997, 2004.)* `T2`
      - **How precisely can an individual learning rate be estimated?** In a study of 40 healthy adults completing a 160-trial probabilistic reversal-learning task twice, one week apart, the retest reliability of the model-estimated **learning-rate parameter** depended almost entirely on the estimation method: parameters estimated by maximum likelihood "generally had very poor reliabilities"; maximum a posteriori estimation with uninformative priors gave "poor or moderate" reliabilities; and only expectation-maximisation MAP estimation with *empirical group-level priors*, fitted jointly across both sessions, reached "fair to excellent." *(Waltmann, Schlagenhauf & Deserno, 2022, *Behavior Research Methods* 54(6):2993–3014, DOI 10.3758/s13428-021-01739-7; reliability bands per Cicchetti, 1994: <.40 poor, .40–.59 fair, .60–.74 good, .75–1.00 excellent.)* `T2`
      - The same authors state the operational consequence directly: in cross-sectional designs "the current number of trials in one run of the task may be insufficient to produce reliable estimates," and "the most difficult situation arises under the hypothetical condition that the task is used as a test or diagnostic tool in a single individual — here, neither group-level data nor a large number of trials are available to inform the estimation, which, based on our results, is problematic in particular for the reliability of computational modeling parameters." Their proposed remedy is to obtain a normative reference sample and extract empirical priors from it. *(Waltmann, Schlagenhauf & Deserno, 2022, *Behavior Research Methods* 54(6):2993–3014, DOI 10.3758/s13428-021-01739-7.)* `T2`
      - Their simulations (500 datasets, 160 trials, 38 simulated subjects, two sessions) showed that reliability estimates based on session means or on separately modelled sessions "tend to substantially underestimate the true retest reliability," while estimates from jointly modelled sessions "tend to moderately overestimate it"; only model-calculated variances that incorporate estimation uncertainty showed "no obvious bias." *(Waltmann, Schlagenhauf & Deserno, 2022, *Behavior Research Methods* 54(6):2993–3014, DOI 10.3758/s13428-021-01739-7.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** This is the most quantitatively secure result in the whole category, and it is a warning. The "power law of practice" — one of the most-cited empirical regularities in cognitive psychology — is substantially an artifact of averaging over people who differ in learning rate. Fit at the individual level, the exponential wins in every one of 40 unaveraged datasets. The consequence for anyone reading learning curves is direct and unforgiving: **a curve computed over a group tells you almost nothing reliable about the shape of any individual's curve**, and the specific direction of the distortion is that heterogeneity in rate manufactures the appearance of diminishing returns. Note the symmetry with subcategory 6.5 — averaging over people destroys the form of the individual curve; averaging over too few occasions destroys the precision of the individual rate. There is no configuration in which you get an individual learning rate cheaply. The best available direct evidence on the price says that 160 trials per person, with two sessions, was enough for *fair-to-excellent* individual rate reliability only when the estimator borrowed strength hierarchically from a group and used both sessions jointly; the same data yielded very poor reliability under the naive per-person maximum-likelihood fit that a single-individual assessment would be forced to use.
    - **Link to source:** [Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort 2000 — the aggregation artifact demonstrated](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212979)

- **Subcategory 6.7: Practice and retest effects — the contamination floor under every within-person "learning" signal**
  - **Source:** Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007); Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018); Calamia, Markon & Tranel (2012); Duff & Hammers (2022)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - A meta-analysis of 50 studies of practice effects on cognitive ability tests, comprising 107 samples and 134,436 participants, found "an adjusted overall effect size of .26," with larger effects when practice was accompanied by test coaching and when identical forms were used across administrations. The same paper notes that "as many as 25% to 50% of applicants in organizational and educational settings are retested with measures of cognitive ability." *(Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard, 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 92(2):373–385, DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.92.2.373.)* `T1`
      - A larger, longitudinal multilevel meta-analysis covering 174 samples from 122 studies, 786 outcomes and N = 153,185, spanning studies published 1921–2016, reported standardised mean change of **0.33 from first to second administration, 0.50 from first to third, and 0.52 from first to fourth**. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Between *consecutive* administrations in the same meta-analysis, gains shrank sharply and then stopped: 0.17 from the second to the third administration, and 0.02 from the third to the fourth (p = .318, non-significant). The authors' conclusion was "no further score gains after the third test administration." *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Heterogeneity in that meta-analysis was high: for the first-to-second effect of 0.33, the estimated standard deviation of the true effect was τ = 0.32, giving a 95% prediction interval of **[−0.30, 0.96]**. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - Significant moderators of retest effect size in the same analysis were cognitive ability operation and content, equivalence of test forms, retest interval, and participant age. Notably, the moderator analysis found only one significant difference between cognitive operations across four administrations: processing-speed tasks showed no gain from the third to fourth administration (−0.07) whereas memory tasks did (0.11, p < .001). *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - The same paper also reports that roughly half of its samples were control groups drawn from intervention studies — that is, the retest baseline is largely constructed from the untreated arms of studies about something else. *(Scharfen, Peters & Holling, 2018, *Intelligence* 67:44–66, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003.)* `T1`
      - In clinical assessment, a set of meta-analyses of "nearly 1600 individual effect sizes" found that use of alternate forms, participant age, clinical diagnosis, and test–retest interval "were associated with the magnitude of change in many cases," and concluded by highlighting "the need for practice effects to be taken into account in interpreting change across time with multiple measurements." *(Calamia, Markon & Tranel, 2012, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist* 26(4):543–570, DOI 10.1080/13854046.2012.680913.)* `T1`
      - **A negative validation of practice-effect correction.** When the 2012 meta-analytic prediction equations were applied prospectively to 93 older adults with amnestic mild cognitive impairment over one year, three of seven observed follow-up scores were significantly lower than predicted; on one memory measure, "40% of the sample had … predicted scores that were one or more standard deviations above their observed scores," with the largest discrepancies on tests not included in the original meta-analysis. *(Duff & Hammers, 2022, *The Clinical Neuropsychologist* 36(3):571–583, DOI 10.1080/13854046.2020.1781933.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** Any within-person improvement measured across two administrations sits on top of a retest effect that meta-analysis places at roughly a third of a standard deviation for the first repetition — comparable to or larger than many effects that dynamic-testing studies attribute to mediation. Three properties of this contamination make it particularly awkward. First, it is **non-linear**: most of it arrives on the second administration, roughly half again on the third, and essentially nothing after that, so a "learning rate" computed from repeated administrations is confounded with a decelerating artifact that mimics exactly the decelerating learning curve one expects to see. Second, it is **enormously heterogeneous**: the 95% prediction interval for the first-to-second effect spans −0.30 to 0.96, which means the meta-analytic mean cannot be used to correct any individual case. Third, the attempt to correct for it empirically has been tested and partly failed: prediction equations built from ~1,600 effect sizes systematically over-predicted follow-up performance in a clinical sample, most severely on tests outside the original evidence base. The practical implication is stark. If you want to attribute within-person score change to learning rather than to repeated exposure, you need either a genuine no-training control condition observed on the same schedule, or non-identical forms, or both — and the design that gets used most often in practice, identical forms with no control, is the one the meta-analysis identifies as maximally contaminated.
    - **Link to source:** [Scharfen, Peters & Holling 2018 retest meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003)

- **Subcategory 6.8: Is "learning rate" a stable, general individual difference at all?**
  - **Source:** Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin (2023); Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali (2024); Ackerman (1987); Zerr, Berg, Nelson, Fishell, Savalia & McDermott (2018); Vaughan & Birney (2024)
    - **DOK 1 — Facts:**
      - **The strongest evidence against.** Mixed-effects logistic growth models fitted to 1.3 million observations across 27 datasets (nearly 7,000 students, ~200 observations per student, in mathematics, science and language courses from elementary to college) found students "astonishingly similar in estimated learning rate, typically increasing by about 0.1 log odds or 2.5% in accuracy per opportunity," alongside substantial variation in initial performance (median 55% correct for the lower half of students versus 75% for the upper half). *(Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin, 2023, *PNAS* 120(13):e2221311120, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2221311120.)* `T2`
      - Quantified as an interquartile range: "the interquartile range in variation for student learning rate … is only about 1% per opportunity (2.56 to 1.70%), whereas the variation in initial knowledge is about 20% (75.17 to 55.21%)." Translated into practice, "a typical student in the bottom half of learning rate (a slower learner) requires about 8 (Median = 7.89) opportunities to reach mastery, whereas a typical student in the top half … requires about 7 (Median = 6.94)" — while the bottom half of *initial performance* is about 10 opportunities behind the top half (13.13 versus 3.66). *(Koedinger et al., 2023, *PNAS* 120(13):e2221311120, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2221311120.)* `T2`
      - The authors pre-empt the obvious objection that their model simply cannot see rate differences: substantial learning-rate variation *was* detected at the level of knowledge components, and simulated students with rate differences produced visibly non-parallel curves — "limitations in the data or measurement model do not appear to be good explanations for our near constant student learning-rate observation." *(Koedinger et al., 2023, *PNAS* 120(13):e2221311120, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2221311120.)* `T2`
      - A replication on a larger, more diverse, operational dataset (over 15,000 students, 821,890 observations, 6 mathematics topics, 2022–2023 school year) "recovered the same interquartile range … to the thousandth decimal place"; the largest student-slope IQR found was 0.116 against the original study's largest of 0.102, and student *intercept* variances again had a much wider range. *(Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali, 2024, Proceedings of the 17th International Conference on Educational Data Mining, pp. 420–425, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.12729850.)* `T3` `[COI: all three authors employed by the commercial vendor of the intelligent tutoring system that supplied the data, and whose product benefits from the "more practice opportunities suffice" conclusion]`
      - The same replication registers a modelling caution against its own conclusion: the underlying growth model "is a very new model and will require subsequent evaluations before researchers should unqualifiedly accept its estimates," and it exhibited "large uncertainty in estimation around the fixed intercepts and skill slopes" (maximum confidence-interval range 1.04 logits). *(Simpson, Norberg & Fancsali, 2024, EDM 2024, pp. 420–425, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.12729850.)* `T3` `[COI: as above]`
      - **The classic psychometric position, which is genuinely two-sided.** A 1987 integrative review reports that earlier studies of intelligence and skill learning "indicated small or trivial correlations between individual differences in intelligence and gain scores," and that later studies indicated "small correlations between performance measures on skill learning tasks and standard intellectual and cognitive ability measures, as well as increasing amounts of task-specific variance over learning trials." *(Ackerman, 1987, *Psychological Bulletin* 102(1):3–27, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.102.1.3.)* `T2`
      - The same review concludes that individuals "converge on performance as tasks become less dependent on attentional resources with practice" — but also that "when appropriate methodological techniques are used and crucial task characteristics are taken into account, intellectual abilities play a substantial part in determining individual differences in skill learning." *(Ackerman, 1987, *Psychological Bulletin* 102(1):3–27, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.102.1.3.)* `T2`
      - **The strongest evidence for a stable learning-efficiency difference.** Using a foreign-language paired-associate "learning-efficiency task" designed to eliminate ceiling effects, quicker learners were also more durable learners — they showed better retention across a delay "despite studying the material for less time." The learning and memory measures were reliable across 30 hours in Study 1 (N = 281) and across **3 years** in Study 2 (N = 92; follow-up n = 46). *(Zerr, Berg, Nelson, Fishell, Savalia & McDermott, 2018, *Psychological Science* 29(9):1436–1450, DOI 10.1177/0956797618772540.)* `T2`
      - That same finding is simultaneously evidence that learning rate is *not* separable from status: those who scored better on the initial test also learned more quickly, and those who learned faster scored higher at final test — the study's own framing is a single construct, "learning efficiency," rather than a rate parameter independent of ability. *(Zerr et al., 2018, *Psychological Science* 29(9):1436–1450, DOI 10.1177/0956797618772540.)* `T2`
      - **An adjacent positive result that is often mistaken for this one.** Repeated, short, in-context cognitive measurements (ecological momentary assessment) had incremental validity for predicting first-year university performance over and above a traditional single-occasion cognitive assessment — but the authors attribute this to capturing "an individual's typical ecological cognitive performance over the short-term," i.e. within-person variability and typical performance, not learning rate. *(Vaughan & Birney, 2024, *Intelligence* 107:101869, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2024.101869.)* `T2`
    - **DOK 2 — Summary:** The three claims must be scored separately, and they score differently. **"People differ in learning rate"** — supported in principle but the best-powered modern estimate of the magnitude is startlingly small: an interquartile spread of about one percentage point of accuracy per practice opportunity, amounting to a *one-opportunity* difference in time to mastery between the slower and faster halves of students, against a *ten-opportunity* gap attributable to where they started. This has now been replicated on a much larger operational dataset, though by a commercially interested team using a model whose authors caution it is new. The older skill-acquisition literature reached a compatible verdict from a different direction: correlations between ability and gain scores were "small or trivial," between-person variance tends to *converge* with practice, and task-specific variance grows — meaning whatever "rate" you measure becomes progressively more about that task and less about the person. **"It can be measured reliably in a short session"** — the answer is a heavily qualified no for naive per-person estimation, and a qualified yes only with hierarchical estimation borrowing strength from a normative sample. **"It predicts an outcome beyond static ability"** — the one strong demonstration of a *stable* learning-efficiency difference over three years also shows that fast learners were the ones who started higher and finished higher, which is to say the construct is entangled with general ability and memory rather than orthogonal to it. Finally, note the live alternative interpretation: the same data that show near-constant learning rate are read by their authors as showing that achievement gaps arise from differences in *opportunity*, not differences in *capacity to learn*. Whether one accepts that reading or not, it is the sharpest available challenge to the premise that a person-level learning-rate parameter is the interesting thing to measure.
    - **Link to source:** [Koedinger et al. 2023 — near-constant learning rate](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2221311120)

#### Tensions and contradictions

1. **The gain-score verdict has been reversed and un-reversed, and the field never noticed.** The 1970 recommendation to avoid individual change scores is one of the most-cited results in measurement. But by 1982–1996 a substantial technical literature had shown that the pessimistic conclusion depends on assumptions (equal pre/post reliabilities, equal standard deviations) that are precisely the worst case, and one 1996 paper documents that textbooks continued to teach the pessimistic version anyway. Both the "gain scores are useless" and "gain scores are fine" camps are quoting real mathematics; they differ on which parameter configuration is realistic. Nobody can settle it in the abstract — it is an empirical question about a specific instrument and population.

2. **Dynamic assessment's biggest reported wins come from its weakest designs.** The same systematic review reports variance-explained figures of 72–82% in studies that did not control static predictors, and a median of ~5% in those that did. Meanwhile the 2008 review found predictive validity was *highest* when the criterion was itself a dynamic or criterion-referenced measure and *lowest* against norm-referenced tests and teacher judgment — the pattern one expects from shared-method variance. The literature's headline numbers and its best-controlled numbers differ by an order of magnitude.

3. **A pooled correlation of g = 0.49 and a median incremental variance of 5% describe the same literature.** The 2024 meta-analysis and the 2023 systematic review overlap heavily in primary studies. The former reports a strong raw association with outcomes; the latter reports a small unique contribution after static predictors. Both are correct. They are routinely cited interchangeably as evidence for "dynamic assessment works," and they answer different questions.

4. **Retest effects and learning gains are the same measurement.** A meta-analytic retest effect of 0.33 SD on the second administration, decelerating to essentially zero by the fourth, has the same functional signature — an improvement that decelerates with exposure — as a genuine learning curve. Nothing in a two-occasion design distinguishes them. Yet the dynamic-testing literature's dominant design is exactly two occasions, and a substantial share of the studies in the field's clinical review used gain scores or difference scores as the learning-potential index.

5. **Averaging destroys the thing the field claims to measure.** Group learning curves systematically misrepresent individual curve shape whenever rate differs across people — and the specific artifact is that heterogeneity manufactures apparent diminishing returns. So the standard practice of characterising "the learning curve" for a task, then interpreting an individual's position on it, is invalid in precisely the case (heterogeneous learning rate) that motivates measuring learning rate at all.

6. **The two strongest modern findings point in opposite directions and are not obviously reconcilable.** One programme, on 1.3 million educational observations, finds learning rate near-constant across people while initial knowledge varies hugely. Another, on paired-associate learning, finds a reliable and three-year-stable individual difference in "learning efficiency." Candidate reconciliations exist — different tasks, different timescales, one measures rate conditional on knowledge components while the other measures trials-to-criterion which confounds rate with starting point — but no source located here adjudicates between them directly.

7. **The critics and the proponents are increasingly the same people.** The field's most damaging recent verdicts ("has not been supported," "continues to be supported primarily on the basis of case studies," "continues to remain unfulfilled") come from authors who have spent careers inside dynamic assessment, including one revising his own earlier and more favourable review. That makes the critique unusually credible and simultaneously raises the question of why the positive literature keeps growing at roughly the same rate.

8. **Commercial interest sits on both sides.** The best-known dynamic-assessment instrument is a proprietary product with an institute that publishes its own supporting literature and training. The most influential recent finding *against* meaningful learning-rate variation was replicated by employees of a company selling the practice-delivery technology whose value proposition is "more opportunities suffice." Neither direction of this literature is free of interested parties.

#### Candidate DOK 3 seeds

*Each of the following is a starter hypothesis for the author to interrogate and own — a cross-source connection that looks load-bearing, not a finished insight. Each is stated so that it could be shown false.*

- **Seed A — "Prompt count is a better bet than gain, and for a reason nobody argues from."** The graduated-prompts family measures *how much assistance was required*, not *how much the score changed*. That means it is not a difference between two error-laden measurements and does not inherit the gain-score reliability penalty at all; it is closer to an ordinal item-difficulty measurement of the scaffolding sequence. It also dominates the modern reading literature two-to-one, and it was the prompt count — not a post-test gain — that predicted achievement in the study where teacher judgment did not. *Hypothesis to interrogate:* the incremental validity that survives in this literature may be attributable disproportionately to the prompt-count operationalisation rather than to "dynamic assessment" as a category, and the two families should never have been pooled. *How it could fail:* if the systematic reviews' incremental-variance figures are similar across the two families, or if prompt counts turn out to be nearly a monotone transform of pretest ability.

- **Seed B — "Learning rate may be small, real, and useless in the same breath."** Combine three numbers: the interquartile spread in learning rate corresponds to roughly one extra practice opportunity to reach mastery; the retest effect on the second administration is about 0.33 SD with a prediction interval spanning −0.30 to 0.96; and the naive per-person estimate of a learning-rate parameter from 160 trials was "very poor" in reliability. *Hypothesis to interrogate:* the true between-person signal in learning rate may be smaller than the measurement noise plus the retest artifact of any short assessment designed to detect it — not because rate does not vary, but because the variance to be detected is an order of magnitude smaller than the variance in status that a static test already captures cheaply. *How it could fail:* if a domain exists where slope variance is large relative to intercept variance (the educational-data authors themselves note knowledge components show far more rate variation than students do — so the analogous person-level case may exist in domains not yet studied).

- **Seed C — "Incremental validity has been demonstrated where static measurement is broken, and generalising from there is the field's core error."** The incremental contribution of dynamic measures is largest in pre-literate children (g = 0.48 at ages 4–5, falling to 0.22 by 8–9), in students with disabilities, and in clinical rehabilitation populations — all settings where the static test floors out or where the construct is not yet expressible in the person's behaviour. *Hypothesis to interrogate:* dynamic assessment's incremental validity may be principally a *floor-effect repair*, not a distinct construct — in which case it should shrink toward zero in any population where the static measure has adequate range, and the correct question becomes "does the static instrument floor here?" rather than "should we measure learning?" *How it could fail:* if incremental validity is demonstrated in an unselected, unfloored population with a well-ranged static covariate. No such demonstration was located here, which is itself informative but not conclusive.

- **Seed D — "The averaging artifact and the aggregation of validity evidence are the same mistake at two scales."** Individual exponential curves average into a group power curve; individual instruments with incompatible operationalisations of "learning potential" average into a pooled effect size. In both cases the aggregate has a form and a magnitude that belongs to no member of the set, and in both cases the aggregate is then read back onto individuals. The clinical review found 11 different dynamic tests and at least five distinct indices of learning potential across 24 studies; the reading meta-analysis reported I² = 91.99% with most heterogeneity between study clusters. *Hypothesis to interrogate:* pooled estimates in this literature may be non-interpretable in the same technical sense that averaged learning curves are non-interpretable — not merely imprecise but wrong in form — and the appropriate evidentiary unit is the individual validated instrument, exactly as the 2014 conceptual critique argued on entirely separate grounds. *How it could fail:* if subgroup or meta-regression analyses by operationalisation type substantially reduce heterogeneity, which would indicate the pooling is recoverable rather than incoherent.

#### Open assumptions and evidence gaps

- **Gap — no meta-analysis of incremental validity per se was located.** The syntheses located here either pool raw criterion correlations (2024 meta-analysis, g = 0.49) or narratively summarise per-study incremental variance (2023 systematic review, median ≈ 5%). No pooled estimate of *incremental* validity over a static covariate, with a confidence interval, was found in any domain. The 5% figure is a median of narratively extracted values, not a meta-analytic estimate, and carries no interval.
- **Gap — the pooled effect size of the 2001 dynamic-assessment meta-analysis was not obtained.** Only the abstract was accessible; its moderator structure is reported above, its magnitude is not. Any claim about "how large the dynamic-assessment effect is" from that source should be sourced directly before use.
- **Gap — the substantive content of the 1998 canonical review was not read.** Its existence, venue, volume, pages and DOI are verified. Its criticisms are reported here only as characterised by two later peer-reviewed sources. The frequently repeated claims about its conclusions on gain-score psychometrics should be verified against the original before being quoted.
- **Gap — the substantive findings of three foundational statistical papers were not retrieved:** the 1982 growth-curve rebuttal, the 1980 regression-to-the-mean paper, and the 1986 graduated-prompts study. All three have verified bibliographic records; none had full text retrieved. They are cited above only for what their titles and registry records establish.
- **Assumption, unverified — that the 160-trial reliability findings for reinforcement-learning-rate parameters generalise to learning-rate estimation in other paradigms.** That study used one specific probabilistic reversal-learning task, N = 40, in healthy adults. It is the most directly relevant evidence located on "how many trials do you need," but it is a single study on a single task and its reliability figures should not be transported to educational or skill-acquisition learning curves without checking.
- **Assumption, unverified — that the near-constant-learning-rate finding generalises beyond interactive practice technologies.** Both the original and the replication study populations were students using online practice systems with immediate feedback, in courses where knowledge-component models had been empirically refined. The original authors explicitly attribute the regularity in part to those "favourable learning conditions." Whether learning rate is equally uniform under less structured instruction is untested in the located evidence.
- **Assumption, unverified — that the replication of the near-constant-learning-rate finding is independent.** It was conducted by employees of the vendor of the studied system, used a model developed by the original authors, and acknowledges assistance from an original author. It is a valuable large-sample replication but not an adversarial one.
- **Gap — no evidence was located on whether learning rate measured in one domain predicts learning rate in another.** This is the central question for treating learning rate as a general trait, and nothing directly addressing it was found. The nearest evidence is indirect and points toward domain-specificity: task-specific variance grows across learning trials, and the best-fitting model in the classic preschool dynamic-assessment study *maintained separate verbal and spatial domains* rather than a general learning factor. Neither is a direct test.
- **Gap — no located study reports the test–retest reliability of a dynamic-assessment gain score itself.** The reliability of the *instrument* is discussed extensively in critiques; a numeric retest reliability for a learning-potential index, in any population, was not found. This is the single most consequential missing number in the category.
- **Gap — no evidence was located on whether any dynamic-assessment result changes a downstream decision.** The 2018 review makes exactly this complaint (recommendations that are meaningful to practitioners and produce outcomes unlikely to have been achieved otherwise), and states that no such studies had been published in the fifteen years since the request was first made. No later study meeting that description was found here.
- **Conflict-of-interest caveat carried throughout:** this literature contains proprietary instruments with institutional publishers, commercially licensed training programmes, and vendor-employed replication teams on both sides of the central empirical question. Marked inline where identified; absence of a marker should not be read as verified absence of interest.
